Violet light scatters in the air even more than blue does, so by rights the sky ought to be violet. The colour we see when we look up is a compromise between physics and our own eyes. On Mars, that compromise is turned on its head, and there the sunset is blue.

When you look up at a clear midday sky, the colour you see is really the answer to an enormous question. For centuries, people simply accepted this blue as it was. Some thought it was the colour of the air itself; others believed it was a mixture of the darkness of space and the light of the sun. In his notebooks, Leonardo da Vinci wrote that blue arises from fine particles lit up against a dark background. His intuition was remarkably good, but he had no experiment to back it up. To truly understand why the sky is blue, someone first had to recreate it inside a glass tube.

That someone turned out to be the Irish physicist John Tyndall. In the eighteen sixties, the Royal Institution in London was Tyndall's laboratory. There he studied how light passes through gases and vapours. An experiment he carried out in eighteen sixty-nine produced, in his own words, a kind of artificial sky. He filled a long, horizontal glass tube with a very dilute organic vapour and, using a lens, sent the beam of a powerful lamp in through one end. At first the tube was empty, invisible. But as the light interacted with the vapour, a chemical reaction triggered by the light began to form extraordinarily tiny particles. And at that moment, inside the tube, right where the beam passed through, a faint but unmistakable blue cloud appeared.

What truly excited Tyndall was not just the colour. When he looked at this blue cloud from the side, at a right angle to the beam, he noticed that the light coming from it was polarised. In other words, the light waves were not vibrating randomly in every direction, but within a particular plane. This detail mattered, because the real sky was known to do exactly the same thing. The French physicist François Arago had shown in eighteen oh-nine that skylight is polarised, and that the effect is strongest at points ninety degrees away from the sun. Tyndall's tube mimicked the sky both in its colour and in its polarisation. The conclusion he drew was this: the sky is blue because the atmosphere contains particles, far smaller than the wavelength of light, that scatter it. Tyndall thought these particles might be fine dust and water droplets suspended in the air.

Tyndall was an experimenter; he had made the observation, but he had not built the mathematics behind it. That gap was filled by a young English aristocrat: John William Strutt, known to the scientific world as Lord Rayleigh. In papers published in eighteen seventy-one, Rayleigh asked a simple but extraordinarily powerful question: how does an obstacle much smaller than the wavelength of light respond to a light wave?

To understand the answer, it is enough to think of light as a wave of electric and magnetic fields. As this wave passes over a tiny particle, it shakes the electric charges inside the particle back and forth. And those shaking charges, like tiny antennas, send out new light in every direction. That is what scattering is: part of the incoming light is knocked off its path and spread all around. Through an elegant line of reasoning known as dimensional analysis, followed by more detailed calculations, Rayleigh showed that the intensity of the scattered light is inversely proportional to the fourth power of the wavelength. Halve the wavelength, and the scattering does not double; it grows by two to the fourth power, that is, sixteen times.

This fourth power is the hidden rule that sets the colour of the sky. In visible light, red has a wavelength of around seven hundred nanometres, and blue around four hundred and fifty. Plug those numbers into Rayleigh's formula, and you find that blue light is scattered roughly six times more strongly than red. When white light from the sun enters the air, its short-wavelength components are flung in every direction and reach your eyes from every corner of the sky. The long wavelengths, meanwhile, largely carry straight on along their path. When you look not at the sun itself but at a point far from it, what you see is this scattered light, shifted towards blue.

The formula also explained polarisation. A shaking charge cannot emit light along the direction of its own oscillation; it emits most strongly at right angles to it. That is why light coming from the band of sky at a right angle to the sun is strongly polarised. Today we know that honeybees and some migratory animals use this pattern like a compass. A single physical principle of Rayleigh's explained both the colour and this invisible pattern at the same time.

But the story did not end there. Because two troubling questions still stood in the way. The first: was the thing scattering this light really dust and droplets? The second, and even more vexing: if the formula was so clear, and shorter wavelengths scatter more strongly, why was the sky blue and not violet?

The answer to the first question came from a place that seemed strange at first glance. When Rayleigh returned to the subject in eighteen ninety-nine, he argued that dust was not needed at all. The molecules of the air itself, nitrogen and oxygen, were thousands of times smaller than the wavelength of light, and they too could act on their own like tiny antennas. Rayleigh pointed out that on mountain summits, in air almost entirely free of dust, the sky stayed blue, and was even a deeper blue. The source of the scattering had to be the air itself.

But there was a trap hidden inside this idea, and it took physicists a decade to notice it. Imagine a perfectly uniform, completely homogeneous medium. A flawless block of glass, for example. Inside the glass there are countless atoms too, and each one oscillates with the incoming light. Yet glass does not scatter light to the sides. It is transparent. Why? Because when atoms are evenly arranged, the wave each tiny antenna sends sideways is met by its neighbor's wave in exactly the opposite phase. The waves cancel each other out. Only the light travelling forward survives. Perfect order kills scattering.

So why does air glow blue instead of being as transparent as glass? This is where the Polish physicist Marian Smoluchowski, and after him Albert Einstein, enter the story. Smoluchowski in nineteen oh-eight, and Einstein in a famous paper published in nineteen ten, showed that the answer lay not in the molecules themselves but in their disorder. Gas molecules move about randomly and without pause. So when you divide the air into small volumes, the number of molecules in each one is never exactly the same. At any moment one corner holds a few molecules more than average, and another a few fewer. The density is constantly fluctuating. And it is these small, random fluctuations that keep the scattered waves from cancelling each other out completely.

Einstein's real goal was not actually the sky. He was trying to explain a phenomenon called critical opalescence. When a liquid approaches the special critical point between boiling and becoming a gas, it suddenly turns cloudy, like milk, because its density fluctuations grow enormous. Einstein calculated how much light these fluctuations would scatter. Then, when he applied his formula to an ordinary gas, something beautiful happened. Rayleigh's fourth-power law reappeared all by itself. Because the positions of molecules in an ideal gas are independent of one another, the calculation based on fluctuations and the calculation based on individual molecules arrived at the same result. But now we knew why it was true. What makes the sky blue is the grainy, discrete nature of air, the fact that it is made of atoms. If air were a continuous, smooth fluid, the sky would be black.

This result had a dramatic side effect. The amount of scattering depended on the number of molecules per unit volume. That meant if you measured the brightness of the sky carefully, you could calculate the number of molecules, in other words, Avogadro's number. Rayleigh had tried this roughly, and with Einstein's calculation the method was put on solid ground. At a time when people were still arguing over whether atoms really existed, the blue above our heads became a silent witness to their existence.

Now let us return to the second, more stubborn question: the missing violet. Rayleigh's law is merciless. Violet light has a wavelength of around four hundred nanometers, and it scatters about one point six times more strongly than blue at four hundred fifty nanometers. On paper, the sky should be painted violet. So why isn't it?

The answer lies not in a single cause but in a chain of three links. The first link is the Sun itself. Sunlight does not contain all colors in equal amounts. The solar spectrum measured outside the atmosphere reaches its peak somewhere between green and blue, and falls off noticeably toward violet. Elements such as calcium and iron in the Sun's outer layers create many narrow dark lines at the violet end of the spectrum, swallowing part of that light from the very start. So there is less violet light to scatter than blue.

The second link is that what scattering produces is not a pure color. Rayleigh scattering does not pick out a single wavelength and throw away the rest. It scatters the whole spectrum, just the short wavelengths more strongly. The light that reaches your eye from the sky is a broad mixture containing every color from violet to red, weighted more and more toward the short wavelengths. Within this mixture there is violet, but also green, and yellow.

The third, and perhaps most decisive, link is your eye. In our retina there are three kinds of cone cells that let us see color: those sensitive to short, medium and long wavelengths. That broad mixture coming from the sky stimulates these three cones in particular proportions. The short-wavelength cones are strongly stimulated, but the medium- and long-wavelength cones are not left idle either, because the mixture also contains green and yellow light. The brain reads the ratio of these three signals, and the resulting perception is not violet, but a blue mixed with white. What's more, our eyes respond only weakly to violet light to begin with, and the lens of the eye yellows with age, filtering out even more of the short wavelengths. The violet is there. We simply lack the equipment to pick it out as a color of its own.
In short, it would be wrong to say that "the sky is really violet and we simply see it wrong." The sky has no single true color. There is a spectrum of light, and there is a living creature that interprets that spectrum. A bee or a bird, with different cones in its eyes, may well perceive the same sky differently from us. This is the border where physics ends and biology begins. The blueness of the sky is made half in the atmosphere and half inside our own skulls.

At the end of the day, the same physics paints a completely different scene. When the sun is high overhead, its light takes the shortest path through the atmosphere. As the sun nears the horizon, that path grows longer. When the sun sits right on the horizon line, its light passes through roughly thirty-eight times more air than it does at noon. On a journey that long, the blue light is scattered off in every direction before it is even halfway there. What reaches you are the longer wavelengths that survive the trip: yellow, orange and red. The crimson of a sunset is really the other side of the same scattering that makes the sky blue. The blue has been taken from your sun to become somebody else's sky. While you watch a red sun go down in the west, people thousands of kilometers to the east are living through their afternoon under that same scattered blue light.

Larger particles in the air make the picture richer. Rayleigh's formula only holds for scatterers much smaller than the wavelength of light. For bigger particles, such as cloud droplets, a more general theory takes over, developed by the German physicist Gustav Mie in nineteen oh-eight, and these particles scatter all colors almost equally. That is why clouds look white, and why a hazy city sky looks pale and milky. The same principle explains why, after major volcanic eruptions, fine particles gathering high in the atmosphere can tint sunsets with unusual colors for months.

But after sunset, the stage opens on a far quieter secret. Once the sun has slipped below the horizon, the sky overhead keeps glowing for a while in a deep, almost velvety blue. For a long time, this twilight blue was also put down to Rayleigh scattering. Yet the calculations did not add up. With the sun below the horizon, the light reaching the air overhead must have travelled a very long way through the atmosphere, and it should have lost most of its blue along the way. By a simple Rayleigh calculation, the twilight sky overhead should have looked greyish, or even faintly yellowish, rather than blue.

The American physicist Edward Hulburt solved this puzzle in nineteen fifty-three. The key lay in the ozone layer, concentrated between fifteen and thirty-five kilometers above the ground. Ozone is known for absorbing ultraviolet light, but it also has a weak absorption band in visible light. This band, discovered by the French chemist James Chappuis in eighteen eighty, swallows mostly orange and yellow light. During the day the effect is barely noticeable, because sunlight crosses the ozone layer by a short route. At twilight, though, sunlight passes through the ozone layer sideways, for hundreds of kilometers. Along that long path the orange and yellow are filtered out, and the light that remains shifts toward blue. Hulburt's calculations showed that the deep blue of twilight comes mostly from this quiet filter of ozone, not from Rayleigh scattering. So the color you see above you as night falls is a signature of the very same layer of gas that shields you from ultraviolet rays.

Perhaps the most beautiful test of this story took place on another planet. The atmosphere of Mars is very thin. The pressure at the surface is less than one percent of Earth's, and the air is made up mostly of carbon dioxide. In air this sparse, scattering by molecules stays weak. What gives the Martian sky its color is a fine, iron-oxide-rich dust that the winds keep constantly aloft. These dust grains are about one point five micrometers across, which makes them larger than the wavelength of visible light. The dust partly absorbs blue light, while scattering red and yellow tones in every direction. As a result, the daytime sky on Mars has a color somewhere between caramel and a pinkish brown.

And at sunset, the colors trade places. Dust particles of this size are better at scattering blue light forward than red light, at angles very close to the direction the light is coming from. When the sun sinks toward the horizon and its light travels a long way through the dusty air, a cold, pale blue halo forms right around the sun. This sight had been known since the Viking missions, and on April fifteenth, twenty fifteen, the Curiosity rover showed it to the whole world in a series of photographs taken from Gale Crater. On Earth, the noon sky is blue and the evening is red. On Mars, the noon sky is red and the evening is blue.
This reversal is the most elegant confirmation of an idea that is a hundred and fifty years old. The colour of the sky is not some magical property of the air; it is the result of the relationship between the wavelength of light and the size of the particles that scatter it, along with whatever absorbs light along the way. An astronomer who understands this calculation can now run it in reverse. By studying the light that filters through the atmospheres of planets passing in front of distant stars, astronomers are trying to tell whether those atmospheres are dominated by small molecules or by particles of haze and mist. Rayleigh's fourth-power curve shows up as a distinct slope in the spectrum of that light, and the shape of this slope offers a clue to what colour the sky might be on a world we have never seen. These estimates are not yet certain; they often rest on indirect measurements interpreted through models. But the principle is the same one at work in Tyndall's glass tube.

The next time you look up at a clear midday sky, you might remember that what you are seeing is really the meeting of three separate worlds. There is light filtered by the atoms on the surface of the Sun, a wave scattered in every direction by the randomly vibrating molecules of the air, and three kinds of cells that read that wave as blue. Violet is there too, but the sky has kept it hidden, not for you, but perhaps for entirely different eyes.
