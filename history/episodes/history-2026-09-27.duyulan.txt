A nation's most precious possession was the land it had spent centuries stealing from  the sea.  In the summer of 1672, the Dutch decided that the only way to save that land was to drown  it.  That same week, they tore apart, in the open street, the man who had governed the country  for 20 years.  In the spring of 1672, the richest country in Europe was also one of the smallest.  The united provinces of the Dutch Republic, seven provinces in all, covered no more ground  than a medium-sized province does today, and its population was a little over 2 million.  Yet in the harbor of Amsterdam, the masts of anchored ships rose into the sky like a  forest, and on the city's exchange the price of everything from Baltic wheat to Javanese  pepper was settled.  This republic had no king.  When the young stadtholder died of smallpox in 1650, he left behind an unborn son, and  the merchant families of the towns decided never to fill that vacancy again.  For 22 years the country was governed by assemblies, clerks and ledgers.  The architect of that order was Johan de Wit, chief administrator of the province of Holland  since 1653, a mathematician, the author of a book on the calculus of chances.  The first man to work out correctly the true value of a contract for a life annuity.  His strategy was simple and consistent.  The money of a maritime state goes to the navy.  An army is expensive, dangerous, and hands political power to its commanders.  There was one flaw in this calculation, Louis XIV of France had no intention of forgiving  the Dutch.  They had stood at the center of the Triple Alliance that in 1668, forced him to break  off his conquests in the Spanish Netherlands halfway through.  The tariff walls of his minister Colbert had failed to crush Dutch trade.  On top of that, the printing houses of Amsterdam were turning out pamphlets, medals and caricatures  that mocked the French king.  In 1670, Louis signed a secret treaty with Charles II of England at Dover, in exchange  for French money, the English navy would go to war against the Dutch.  In two German church states, the prince-bishoprics of Munster and Cologne, were bought with the  same French gold.  Four enemies.  One plan of attack.  The first blow came before any declaration.  On the 12th of March, English ships fell upon the Dutch merchant convoy returning from Smyrna  in the channel, the declaration of war followed two weeks later on the 27th of March.  France declared on the 6th of April.  In May an army of 120,000 men gathered near Liège, with Turenne, Condé and the king  himself at its head.  A separate force of 30,000, hired by Munster and Cologne, would advance from the east.  On the other side, there was on paper, a Dutch army of as many as 80,000, but most units  stood at half strength.  Officers' commissions had become salary lines distributed among town families.  And the bulk of the soldiers were waiting in the southern fortresses, in the wrong place.  In February, in desperation, the boy born fatherless in 1650 was brought forward, William,  21 years old, was made captain-general of the army.  The perpetual edict of 1667, drawn up to keep him away from that office forever, was forgotten  five years later.  On the morning of the 12th of June, the French army crossed the lower branch of the Rhine  in front of an old customs house called the Toulouis, near the village of Lobith.  The river was low that summer.  The cavalry rode across a shallow ford, and the infantry were carried over on pontoons.  At Versailles this crossing was worked for years into paintings, sealing frescoes and  reliefs.  The scene, known as the crossing of the Rhine, was sold as the king's greatest feat of arms.  In fact the losses were almost nil, because there was no one facing them.  The defensive line built along the river Ijssel was turned from behind in a single stroke.  In the weeks that followed Düsberg, Zutphen, Deventer, Arnhem and Nijmegen fell one after  another.  The fortresses held out not for days but for hours.  At the end of June the city of Utrecht opened its gates without firing a shot, and its council  went out to receive the king of France.  Three provinces were lost in six weeks, without an army even having to be beaten in the field.  The people fleeing back poured in floods into the towns of the province of Holland.  Cues formed outside Amsterdam's Citibank as depositors tried to withdraw their money,  bond prices collapsed, days of public repentance were proclaimed in the churches.  In the street, the name of Johan de Wit was now spoken together with treason.  The Dutch would later remember that year in three rhyming words, the people irrational,  the government helpless, the country beyond saving.  The only weapon left was the thing they had regarded as an enemy for centuries.  Most of the province of Holland lies below sea level.  This land was an artificial landscape, one from marsh over centuries, ringed with dikes,  drained by windmills, encircled by canals.  Every polder, every drained basin inside its ring of dikes, was in fact a machine that  could be run in reverse.  If you opened the sluices of the system that pushed the water out, the same system would  let the water back in.  The decision was taken on the 8th of June by the states of Holland.  A strip running from Gorinkum in the south, to Muiden in the north, on the shore of the  Zuiderzee, was to be put under water.  This line would later be known as the Old Dutch Water Line, and its logic demanded a  merciless precision.  If the water rose too high, a boat could cross it, if it rose too little, soldiers would  simply walk through.  The depth they were after was roughly 30 cm.  A sheet of water above a man's heel and below his knee.  Under such a cover the ground becomes invisible, beneath it lie hidden ditches, irrigation  channels, pits of mud.  Horses dragging heavy gun carriages sink into them, infantry lose formation, an engineer  cannot tell where to throw a bridge.  The only dry passages were the raised roads and the crowns of the dikes.  A redoubt was built at the head of each one, and guns were placed there.  Opening the sluices was harder than taking the decision.  That ground was somebody's pasture, somebody's hay waiting to be cut.  It was the middle of June, asked to drown their own fields in the middle of the harvest,  farmers went out at night and stopped up the breeches in the dikes with brushwood and earth.  Some town governments delayed the order, others sat down to bargain over compensation.  Those were posted as sentries at the dikes, with permission to open fire on anyone closing  a gap.  Day after day the water was raised, measured afresh each morning, sluice by sluice and  breach by breach.  There was one moment when all of it came very close to being wasted.  The little town of Muiden, just east of Amsterdam, held the great sea sluice at the heart of  the whole system.  On the 20th of June a French cavalry detachment reached the town, the gates stood open and  there was no garrison.  The detachment rode in, looked around a little, saw that no one was expecting them, and rode  back out, it had neither an officer who understood the value of what he was holding, nor orders  to stay.  Dutch soldiers reached Muiden only hours later.  Had that sluice remained in French hands, flooding the northern flank of Amsterdam would  have been impossible.  On the 22nd of June the water took its place, and the French army came to a halt 40 kilometers  west of Utrecht, on the shore of a sea of mud it could not cross.  Louis was in no hurry at that point.  He preferred to dictate terms of surrender to a country he considered militarily finished.  The terms were read out to the Dutch delegation summoned to Utrecht, the great fortresses  in the south would be handed to France, Catholic worship would be permitted, millions of guilders  in war indemnity would be paid.  The Republic would from then on enter no alliance without French approval, and every year a  special embassy would travel to Versailles to present the King with a medal of gratitude.  The last article was not a military demand, but a declaration of ownership.  When the terms became known in The Hague, public opinion exploded, what people talked  about was not the money on offer but the medal.  The answer attributed to the young Captain General, William was handed down years later  by the pen of an English historian, told that he was doomed to see the end of his country.  He said there was a way of not seeing it, and that was to die in the last ditch.  The same weapon was used a second time in the north.  Bernhard von Galen, Prince Bishop of Münster, whom the Dutch called bomber baron for his  passion for siege guns, arrived before the city of Groningen at the end of July with  more than 20,000 men.  The defense of the city was directed by Karl von Rabinhaupt, an old Bohemian soldier in  the service of the Republic.  He too flooded the surrounding marshes and fields, condemned the siege lines to mud,  and sent raiding parties against the trenches by night.  The bishop threw thousands of bombs into the city, he brought down stone walls but not  the will behind them.  On the 28th of August he lifted the siege and withdrew.  Groningen still celebrates that day every year.  The north and the west of the country had survived by sacrificing their own soil.  But in those same weeks, in the political center of the Republic, something was happening  that no enemy had done.  Johann de Witt was the man who had governed this country for 19 years, and the country  he governed had decided to pin the label of treason on him.  He came from an old family of Dordrecht, he had studied law and mathematics at Leiden,  and the book he wrote on the algebraic treatment of curves was taken seriously among the mathematicians  of his day.  His political creed was built like a theorem as well.  True freedom meant government by the shared reason of the towns, not by a single dynasty.  The most brilliant fruit of that creed had been gathered in 1667, when the Dutch fleet  sailed into the branches of the River Thames, burned part of the English fleet in its own  harbor, and forced England to peace.  The man aboard on that raid, as the state's representative with the fleet, was Johann's  elder brother Cornelis de Witt.  Five years earlier the brothers had been national heroes.  In 1672, the very same choice, money going to the ships and the land army left unmaintained,  became their death warrant.  Within weeks the presses of Amsterdam and The Hague turned out hundreds of pamphlets,  the de Witt brothers had made a deal with the French, they had deliberately left the  fortresses empty.  They had kept the young prince away on purpose from the office, that was his by right.  On the evening of the 21st of June, while the French army waited in front of the waterline,  four young men stabbed Johann de Witt near his house in The Hague.  One of the attackers, Jacob van der Graef, was caught and executed within weeks.  Part of the crowd began visiting his grave.  The gravely wounded chief administrator could not leave his bed for weeks.  In the country's most critical days, the helm of the state stood empty.  The vacancy filled quickly.  At the beginning of July, under pressure from crowds besieging the town councils, the  states of Holland repealed the perpetual edict of 1667, and on the 3rd of July proclaimed  William Stadtholder.  22 years on, the republic annulled its own founding decision.  For the brothers, this was not enough.  A barber surgeon from Pierschel named Willem Tixillar came forward and claimed that Cornelis  de Witt had offered him money to kill the prince.  There was no evidence and no witness, the only thing on the table was the barber's word.  Cornelis was arrested, shut up in the town prison in The Hague and put to torture to  make him confess.  According to the story handed down, through hours of torture he repeated Horace's famous  lines in praise of the just and steadfast man, and signed nothing.  The court could not sentence him to death, it sentenced him to exile.  At the beginning of August, Johann de Witt resigned from all his offices.  On the morning of the 20th of August, Johann went to that prison, the gate prison of the  city known as the Gewangenport, to collect the brother who was to go into exile.  A crowd gathered in front of it before long, the city's militia companies took up positions  in the square under arms.  The cavalry regiment, the only force capable of keeping order in The Hague, had been sent  out that day to put down a disturbance outside the city, and its commander announced that  he would not return without an explicit order.  In the afternoon the crowd broke down the door.  The two brothers were dragged into the street and killed.  Their bodies were hung head down from the gallows opposite, torn apart, and passed from  hand to hand through the crowd as souvenirs.  Johann was 46, Cornelis 49.  Afterwards there was no inquiry and no trial.  Some of the leading figures of that day were rewarded in later years with offices and incomes,  the barber-surgeon among them.  What the young stadtholder knew that day, and what he was in a position to stop and  did not stop, is still debated among historians, what is certain is that he asked for no penalty  for the killers.  The rift between the merchant government of the Republic and the House of Orange took,  in that street, a shape that would last two centuries.  While all this was happening, the real fate of the war was being settled not in The Hague,  but at sea, and had been since two weeks before.  The water line had stopped the army coming by land.  But the real part of the plan was this, the English and French fleets would join, land  troops on the Dutch coast, and take the cities behind the line from the sea.  Against that plan the Republic had a man of 65, Michiel de Ruder, who had gone to sea  at nine years old as a rope worker, and risen from ship's captain to admiral.  The force in his hands was some 75 ships, around 4,500 guns and close to 21,000 men.  The combined fleet facing him was larger, its ships of the line alone numbered more  than 80.  It carried over 6,000 guns, and at its head was the brother of the King of England, James,  Duke of York.  De Ruder did not wait.  On the morning of the 7th of June he found the allied fleet on the east coast of England,  in Sol Bay off Suffolk, ships at anchor, taking on water and provisions, part of the cruise  ashore.  He took the wind behind him and drove into the bay.  The allied line was split before it could form.  The French division, by the reading of its admiral d'Estrès, stood away to the south  and spent most of the day out of the battle.  Seeing this, de Ruder detached a quarter of his fleet to keep an eye on the French and  threw the rest in a mass against the English.  In a close-range gun duel that lasted until evening both sides took heavy losses.  The hundred-gun flagship of the Earl of Sandwich, Admiral of the English Blue Squadron, was  set alight by fire ships.  The ship blew up and the admiral died.  The next day both sides declared victory.  But the allied fleet had to return to port for repairs, and not one soldier was landed  on the Dutch coast that summer.  The next threat came not from the wind, but from the temperature.  The flaw in the waterline was obvious, if 30 centimeters of water freezes, an army walks  over it.  In December of 1672, a hard frost set in, and in those days William III had taken the  greater part of the field army south, towards the French fortress of Charleroi.  That the line was being held by a thin screen.  The French commander in Utrecht, the Duke of Luxembourg, saw his chance and marched  west over the ice with pick troops.  The target was the Hague.  As they passed the villages of Bodegraven and Zwamerdam on the line, the weather turned  at midnight and the thaw began.  The ice started to crack under them, and the roads began to turn to swamp.  They were forced to fall back.  On the way back, between the 28th and the 30th of December, they burned those two villages  and used severe violence against the inhabitants.  In the hands of the Dutch presses, the episode became the most widely printed image of the  war.  The village in flames, families running, soldiers' boots.  The French threat was no longer an abstract military problem but an engraving that could  be hung on the wall of every house.  In the north, in those same days, the ice worked for the Dutch instead.  Rabenhout, who had defended Groningen, raided the fortress of Coevorden across the frozen  ditches on the 30th of December, and took back this key point that Münster had captured  in the summer.  Before the winter was over, the eastern flank of the occupation was beginning to come apart.  In 1673 the matter was closed for good at sea.  The English and French fleet came this time under the command of Prince Rupert, with transports  carrying soldiers alongside it.  The intention was again to make a landing.  In June de Reuter turned them back twice in the Schoonewald Shoals, on the 7th and the  14th of June, using the shallow water of his own coast like a fortress.  On the 21st of August, off Texel, he fought an all-day battle against a numerically superior  fleet and at the same time got through the merchant convoy returning from the East Indies  with a year's revenue of the Republic in its holds.  The landing troops waited aboard their ships and were never put ashore.  The Republic had survived because it had not lost the sea.  By 1673 the ledger of Europe had changed.  France swallowing a small merchant republic in six weeks, woke Madrid, Vienna and Berlin  from their sleep.  William III's envoys made good use of this.  Spain, the Holy Roman Emperor and Brandenburg entered the war on the Dutch side one after  another.  The young Stadtholder was no longer alone, and for the first time he could attack at  a place of his own choosing.  In September he retook the fortress of Nardin, which held the eastern gate of Amsterdam,  this was his army's first real success in a year.  Then he did something nobody expected.  Instead of grappling with the French army inside Holland, he joined the Imperial and  Spanish forces and marched southeast, up the Rhine, and in November took the city of Bonn.  Bonn was the lock on the line by which the French army in Utrecht was supplied along  the river.  When the lock was broken, the occupying force inside the country found itself suddenly without  supply and surrounded.  In the spring of 1674 the French evacuated Utrecht and the eastern provinces without  fighting a battle worth the name.  As the coalition began to come apart, the enemies came to the table one by one.  England left the war with the Treaty of Westminster, signed on the 19th of February, 1674.  Munster and Cologne made peace the same year.  The war with France lasted until 1678, and ended with the Peace of Nijmegen, the Republic  came out of it without losing territory.  On paper, it was a war that had been won.  Under the paper lay the bill.  The water line had been the savior, but the ones who paid for it were the people who lived  under that water.  The salt water let in from the Zuiderzee left meadows that gave no crop for years.  Dykes, mills and farm buildings were wrecked, in the occupied provinces French commanders  had loaded the towns with heavy cash levies.  The state debt multiplied during the war years, and never came back down to the level it had  held before.  Historians do not agree on exactly where the Dutch Golden Age ended, but there is broad  agreement that the country's commercial momentum never regained its old speed after 1672.  The Republic kept its existence and lost its primacy.  And the longest shadow of that year fell somewhere nobody could have predicted that spring.  In 1672 a 21-year-old with no office had come to power because a country was being sunk  underwater.  In 1677 he married Mary Stuart, the daughter of his uncle and heir to the English throne.  In November of 1688, at the invitation of the English opposition, he crossed the channel  with a fleet of hundreds of ships, and landed troops at Torbay in the southwest of England.  His father-in-law James II fled the country.  In the spring of 1689, William and Mary sat down together on the throne of England.  One of the two navies that had joined 16 years earlier to drown his country was now under  his command.  That Louis XIV would fight all his remaining wars with the same man in front of him was  the long-term price of not having been in a hurry at Utrecht in 1672.  The water itself remained at the center of Dutch military thinking.  The dyke breaches hastily cut in the 17th century turned over the following two centuries  into a system that was a marvel of engineering.  Calculated sluices, measured canals, hundreds of redoubts and forts strung along the line.  This arrangement, known as the New Dutch Water Line, was still standing when it was called  into service for the last time in May of 1940, and it was still designed to hold the  water at 30 centimeters.  But in that war the enemy flew over the water.  Today the line's dykes, sluice houses and redoubts are on the UNESCO World Heritage  List.  Cyclists ride along the dykes between the pastures, and cafes operate inside the forts.  War does not occur to anyone looking at that scene.  Yet underneath it lies this decision, what a country does when it understands that it  can defend itself only by ruining its own land.  In 1672 the Dutch answered that question, and opened the sluices.  Neuron Historiete Bugungkubelimun Sonnunna Galdik.  Yeni berhikaya de Yenedan Bulushmakuzere.
