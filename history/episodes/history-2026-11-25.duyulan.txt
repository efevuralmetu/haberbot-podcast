In the spring of 1661, the half Japanese son of a pirate arrived with 400 junks and demanded an island from the richest company in Europe.  The fortress was thought to be impregnable.  Nine months later, the Dutch lowered their flags and marched out of it in formation.  Cannon fire was not the only thing that had beaten them.  A storm, a traitorous sergeant, and warnings ignored for 40 years, also played their part in that defeat.  In 1624, two events took place at the same moment, and for a long time neither had anything to do with the other.  On the southwestern coast of Taiwan, the large island facing the southern coast of China, soldiers of the Dutch East India Company built their first wooden fortifications on a spit of sand.  Over the years, that outpost grew into a fortress with stone walls and corner bastions, and it came to be called Fort Zealandia.  That same year, in the Japanese port of Hirado, a baby was born.  His mother was a Japanese woman from the Tagawa family.  His father was a Chinese merchant and pirate who would become one of the most powerful men on the seas of East Asia, Zheng Zhilong.  The child was given the Japanese name Fukumatsu.  37 years later, he would tear down the world the Dutch had built on that spit of sand.  The life of Zheng Zhilong could fill a novel on its own.  As a young man he spent time in Portuguese-held Macau, was baptized a Christian, and worked for a while as an interpreter for the Dutch.  Then he built a fleet of his own.  Soon no ship carrying silk, porcelain or silver could pass through the Taiwan Strait without paying him for protection.  When the Ming dynasty realized it could not defeat him, it chose to buy him instead.  Zheng Zhilong was made an admiral, and overnight the pirate became the state's guardian of the coast.  The appointment also let him multiply his fortune many times over.  When Fukumatsu was seven years old, he left his mother behind in Japan and was brought to China to live with his father.  There he was given a Chinese name, the finest tutors were hired for him, and he was schooled in the classical texts.  In time the young man entered the imperial academy in Nanjing.  The pirate's son, in other words, was being raised to be a scholar and a servant of the state.  History had other plans.  In 1644, the Ming capital of Beijing fell, first to a peasant rebellion, and then to the Manchus sweeping down from the north.  The Manchus proclaimed a new dynasty, the Qing.  In the south, princes of the Ming line took the throne one after another and tried to hold out.  One of them, who reigned as the Longwu Emperor, depended on the support of the Zheng family.  He received the young man at court, was greatly impressed by him, and granted him Zhu, the surname of the imperial family.  From then on people addressed him by a title meaning, Lord of the Imperial Surname, in the dialect of southern China, that title sounded something like Kok Sengie.  The Dutch wrote it down as Kok Sengie, and that is the name Europe came to know him by.  In Chinese sources, his proper name is Zheng Chenggong.  His father, Zheng Zhilong, rid the winds differently.  In 1646, taken in by the promises of the Qing, he surrendered to them.  Meanwhile, Qing soldiers stormed the family stronghold at Anping.  Kok Sengie's mother, who had come over from Japan only a few years earlier, lost her life in that raid.  Most accounts say she took her own life.  Soon afterward, Kok Sengie went to a Confucian temple, burned his scholar's robes there, and declared that he was now a soldier.  He had turned his back on his father and bound himself to the Ming cause.  Over the next 12 years, Kok Sengie became master of the southern Chinese coast.  The islands of Siamen and Kinmen were his bases.  He commanded tens of thousands of soldiers and hundreds of ships, and he fed his army on the income of seaborne trade.  In 1659 he made a bold move, sailing up the Yangtze River to lay siege to Nanjing.  The campaign ended in disaster.  Qing cavalry scattered his army, and Kok Sengie fell back to the coast, having lost thousands of men and some of his best commanders.  The Qing, for their part, were preparing a ruthless strategy against him.  They would drive the coastal population inland and empty the shoreline, cutting off his supplies.  Kok Sengie needed new land across the sea.  It was at exactly this moment that a man arrived from Taiwan.  He Bin was a Chinese go-between who had worked for the Dutch as an interpreter and tax collector.  Cornered by charges of debt and corruption, he fled to Siamen in 1659.  According to the accounts, he brought with him a map and a priceless piece of information,  a sea channel that lay beyond the range of Zealandia's guns, one the Dutch had dismissed because it was so shallow.  He Bin told Kok Sengie that the island was fertile, that the Dutch were few in number, and that the door stood open.  The Dutch had heard the rumors.  The governor of Taiwan, Frederick Coyet, was a seasoned company official born in Sweden,  and for a long time he had been writing letters of warning to Batavia, the company's Asian headquarters in what is today Jakarta.  Among the Chinese farmers on the island, there was talk that an attack was coming soon.  Some of them were abandoning their fields and fleeing.  In 1660, Batavia finally sent a relief fleet, 12 ships and roughly 600 soldiers under the command of Jean Van der Laan.  But Van der Laan thought the danger had been exaggerated.  His real goal was to attack Portuguese-held Macau, and waiting around in Taiwan struck him as a waste of time.  He quarreled bitterly with Coyet, accused him of cowardice, and in February of 1661 he sailed back to Batavia, taking most of his officers with him.  Some of the soldiers stayed behind at the fortress, but the company's most experienced men were gone.  About two months later, on the morning of April 30, 1661, the sentries on the ramparts of Zealandia looked out into the mist and could not believe their eyes.  The horizon was crowded with masts.  Coxinga's fleet was made up of hundreds of junks.  In the Dutch records, the figure climbs as high as about 400.  The ships carried tens of thousands of soldiers.  Most historians settle on a figure of around 25,000.  The main channel in front of the fortress lay under the mouths of the Dutch cannons.  Coxinga did not go in there.  Instead, he headed for a passage a few kilometers to the north, known as Looermen.  This passage was so shallow that the Dutch were certain large ships could never get through it.  But that morning, as local accounts insist, the sea was higher than usual.  Chinese tradition attributes this to a prayer Coxinga offered to Mazu, the goddess who protects sailors.  The Dutch records simply note that the water rose to an unusual level.  Whichever explanation we choose, the outcome was the same, the junks slipped through the passage on the tide and entered the inner lagoon.  The sea shield the Dutch had relied on for years had been breached in a single morning.  Once inside the lagoon, the fleet split the Dutch positions in two.  On one side stood Fort Zealandia, on its sandy island.  On the other, across the water on the mainland, at a place called Sechem, stood the smaller Fort Provintia.  The water between them now belonged to Coxinga.  Chinese soldiers landed on the mainland.  The Chinese settlers of the surrounding area, farmers who had paid taxes to the company for years,  rushed out to watch them, and some came to help.  They brought carts, oars, and pack animals.  Within a few hours, thousands of soldiers were ashore.  Coyet had a few warships, a few hundred musketeers, and one assumption.  That assumption had been distilled from the company's decades of experience in Asia,  Chinese soldiers cannot stand up to firearms, and they scatter after a few volleys.  The next day, he decided to put that assumption to the test.  The plan was a three-pronged counter-attack.  At sea, the ships would attack the junks in the lagoon, one detachment would try to reinforce Provintia,  and another would march against the Chinese soldiers who had landed on the sandy island north of the fortress.  At first, the naval action looked promising.  The Dutch ships were far larger than the junks, and carried far more guns.  The opening volleys smashed many of the junks.  But the Chinese sailors did not fall back.  With their small vessels, they surrounded the Dutch, pulled up alongside them, and sent in fireships.  In the middle of this chaos, the largest Dutch warship, the Hector, suddenly exploded.  Its powder magazine had probably been ignited either by its own guns or by fire hurled at close range.  Most of the crew were killed.  The remaining ships withdrew.  One of them, a small yacht called the Maria, struggled against the wind for weeks to reach Batavia,  and it was the first to bring news of the disaster there.  When the detachment sent to Provintia neared the shore, it found a large Chinese force waiting.  Only a small number of the soldiers managed to get into the fort, the rest turned back.  And so, that day, nothing had been achieved, either at sea or on the mainland.  That left the third prong, the sandy island to the north.  That sandy island was called Baxamboy.  It was a narrow strip of land stretching north of Zealandia, nothing but dunes and scrub.  Coyet sent about 240 soldiers there, led by Captain Thomas Peddle.  Peddle was an aging officer who had spent many years in the colony.  He had put down Chinese peasant revolts and clashed with the Chinese time and again.  In his eyes, and in the eyes of many in the fortress, what they faced was not a dangerous enemy but a mere crowd.  According to the defense Coyet later wrote, the Dutch truly believed that Chinese soldiers could not bear the smell of gunpowder,  and that as soon as a few of them fell dead, the whole lot would run.  Peddle's soldiers landed on the beach and advanced in neat ranks.  Facing them stood several thousand Chinese soldiers.  The Dutch fired in turn, just as they had drilled, first the front rank, then the ranks behind.  The Chinese answered with arrows and muskets.  The route the Dutch had been expecting never came.  The Chinese soldiers did not budge.  Meanwhile, Koxinga's commanders had made a move the Dutch could not see.  One unit had circled around behind the dunes and slipped onto the flank and rear of Peddle's column.  Suddenly the Dutch were under pressure from the front and from behind.  At that very moment, the most feared troops in the Chinese army stepped forward.  These were heavy infantrymen clad in armor of iron scales, known in some sources as the iron men.  They carried heavy long-handled swords gripped with both hands, whose name meant horse-cutting sword.  They advanced as one body, with disciplined steps.  The Dutch ranks broke apart.  Soldiers threw down their muskets and ran for the water, and some flung themselves into the waves.  Peddle died fighting.  According to Dutch records, nearly half the unit, 118 men, lost their lives on that beach.  The survivors barely made it back to the fortress by boat.  What Coyet watched that day from the fortress bastions was the collapse of a legend that had carried the company's entire confidence in Asia.  The historian Tonio Andrade examines this clash as a moment that put to the test the assumption that the European musket was inherently superior in Asia.  Coxinga's soldiers had fought the Qing for years.  They were an experienced, well-trained, tightly disciplined army.  The Dutch had taken this army for a crowd of peasants.  After the defeat on the beach, Provintia could not hold out for long.  The fort held only a handful of soldiers, with little water and little gunpowder.  Coxinga surrounded them and let them know that if they surrendered, their lives would be spared.  On the 4th of May, 1661, Provintia's commander, Jacobus Valentine, surrendered the fort.  Not a single Dutch flag now remained on the main island.  The Dutch held only Zealandia, on its sandy islet, and the small town beneath its walls.  Coxinga now wanted to persuade Coyet to surrender without bloodshed.  In his letters he wrote that the island had in fact belonged to his father, that the Dutch had only ever stayed there as guests, and that the time had come to return it to its rightful owner.  As one of the envoys to carry these messages, he chose an extraordinary man, a Dutch minister named Antonius Hambroke.  Hambroke had spent years as a missionary in the island's indigenous villages, and at the start of the war he had been taken captive by the Chinese, along with part of his family.  Coxinga sent him to Zealandia. His task was to deliver the surrender offer to Coyet, and to persuade him to accept it.  Hambroke entered the fortress and delivered the offer.  Then he did the thing that would be retold in the Netherlands for generations.  According to the accounts, he told the defenders not to surrender, that help was on its way, and that they must hold on.  His daughters were inside the fortress too.  Tradition says they begged him to stay, but Hambroke, believing that the other captives in Chinese hands would suffer if he did not return, walked back to Coxinga's camp.  Months later, when rumors spread that the Dutch were inciting the indigenous villages against the Chinese, Hambroke was executed along with other Dutch captives.  Hambroke was remembered in the Netherlands as a hero.  In the 18th century his story was brought to the stage, and it became an epic of country and conscience taught in Dutch schools.  The fortress, however, did not surrender.  In the last week of May, Coxinga decided to show his strength.  Under cover of night, gun emplacements were set up in the town at the foot of the fortress, and at dawn a heavy bombardment began.  But Zealandia was an advanced design.  The projecting bastions at its corners were placed so that every point along the walls could be covered by flanking fire.  The Dutch gunners answered the exposed Chinese batteries and inflicted terrible losses.  When the firing stopped, the streets of the town were filled with corpses.  Coxinga learned his lesson, this fortress could not be taken by assault.  He encircled it, and settled in to wait for hunger and time to do their work.  The siege dragged on all summer.  Hundreds of soldiers and civilians were trapped inside the fortress.  Company officials, families, slaves, the wounded.  Food was running low, and disease was spreading.  The heat, the humidity and the foul water brought scurvy and dysentery.  Outside, things were not easy for Coxinga's army either.  An army this large had to be fed, and supplies from the Chinese coast had become harder to obtain because of the Qing policy of evacuating the coastline.  The soldiers gathered rice from the island's indigenous villages and from Chinese farmers, and then began clearing fields.  One side bore the weight of hunger, the other the weight of impatience.  In August, sales appeared once more from Zealandia's bastions.  This time, those arriving were friends.  On the news the Maria had carried to Batavia, the company's leadership had sent a relief fleet.  At its head was Jacob Cayu.  Under his command were about 10 ships, and some 700 soldiers.  A festive mood swept through the fortress.  But once again, the sea around Taiwan took Coxinga's side.  As the fleet tried to anchor, a storm struck.  The ships were driven out to sea and for weeks could not get near the fortress.  One of the ships, the Irk, ran aground, and its crew fell into Chinese hands.  In an instant, the relief had lost provisions, ammunition, and, most important of all, intelligence.  The captives told Coxinga how strong the fleet was.  When the storm had passed, the Dutch planned a counter-attack.  On September 16, 1661, ships and landing troops struck at the Chinese fleet in the lagoon and at the positions along the shore.  The result was a repeat of Baxomboy.  The Dutch ships could not maneuver in the narrow shallow waters.  The Chinese junks swarmed them in great numbers, several of the ships ran aground or were captured, and many soldiers were killed.  Once again, the men on the fortress walls had watched a rout unfold before their eyes.  That autumn, an unexpected player entered the war.  Qing commanders on the Chinese mainland offered the Dutch an alliance.  Their common enemy was Coxinga.  Coyet saw this as an opportunity, and decided to send a fleet to conduct the negotiations.  The mission was given to Cao.  Cao set sail in December with a handful of ships.  But he never went to the Chinese coast.  He turned his course south, to Siam, and from there on to Batavia.  Only much later would the fortress learn that the man who had come to help them had abandoned them.  That same December betrayal came from inside the fortress as well.  A sergeant of German origin named Hans-Jurgen Radis slipped over the walls one night and crossed to Coxinga's camp.  Radis told them that morale in the fortress had hit rock bottom, that the soldiers were being cut down by disease, and that the provisions were running out.  But what mattered most was the military advice he offered.  Right beside Zealandia stood a sand dune that looked down on the fortress.  On that rise, the Dutch had built a small fortification they called the Utrecht Redoubt.  According to Radis, this was the key to the fortress.  If Utrecht fell, the courtyard and the interior of the fortress could be brought under cannon fire from there.  Coxinga took this advice seriously.  For weeks, his men dug earthworks, trenches, and gun emplacements.  The knowledge of Radis and the other European deserters was combined with the skill of the Chinese gunners.  On the morning of January 25, 1662, the bombardment began.  According to Dutch records, some 2,500 cannonballs were fired at the Utrecht Redoubt that day.  The small fortification was shattered within the first hours.  By evening, the Dutch realized they could not hold it, and before withdrawing, they blew the Redoubt up.  But the hill now belonged to Coxinga, and the inside of Zealandia lay within sight of his guns.  The war council in the fortress met over several days.  Some officers wanted one final assault, others argued that further resistance would now mean nothing but more dead.  After a siege of nearly nine months, the survivors were exhausted.  The council decided to surrender.  On February 1, 1662, the treaty was signed.  Coxinga was generous, he allowed the Dutch to march out of the fortress with their weapons and personal belongings, their flags flying.  The goods in the company's warehouse, the cannons, and the money, however, were left to Coxinga.  A few days later, Coyet and the survivors boarded their ships and set out for Batavia.  The Netherlands' 38 years on Taiwan had come to an end.  After raising his own flag over the ramparts of Zealandia, Coxinga began to reorganize the island.  He gave the fortress the name of his family's stronghold back home, Anping.  He organized the island as Ming territory, settled his soldiers on the land and made them farmers, and established administrative districts.  His eyes were already turning further south.  Through an Italian priest, he sent a letter to the Spanish governor in Manila demanding tribute.  It seemed he was contemplating an expedition to the Philippines.  But this victory did not grant him a long life.  While the siege was still underway, in the autumn of 1661, the Qing had executed his father, Zheng Zhilong, along with the other members of the family in Beijing.  Soon afterward came news that the last Ming emperor had been captured in Burma.  The dynasty for which Coxinga had fought had, for all practical purposes, ceased to exist.  On top of this, a family scandal broke, his eldest son, Zheng Jing, who had remained in Xiamen, had had an affair with his younger brother's wet nurse, and a child had been born of it.  In Coxinga's eyes, this was an unforgivable moral transgression.  He ordered the execution of his son and of his son's mother.  The commanders in Xiamen refused to carry out the order.  His own officers would no longer obey him.  Coxinga fell ill in June of 1662.  It was most likely malaria.  On June 23, five months after the surrender of Zealandia, he died at the age of 37.  Contemporary accounts say that his final days were filled with rage and grief.  The man the Dutch had seen as an invincible conqueror died watching his own family and his cause fall apart.  And what became of the loser?  When Frederick Coyet arrived in Batavia, he was not welcomed as a hero.  The company's leadership was looking for someone to blame for this great loss, and the easiest target was the governor himself.  Coyet was arrested, tried, and sentenced to death.  The sentence was later commuted to lifelong exile in the Banda Islands.  He lived for years on a remote island.  Thanks to petitions from his family and the intervention of influential friends in the Netherlands, he was released in 1674 and returned to Europe.  The year after, in 1675, a book appeared in Amsterdam, signed not with a name but with initials.  Its Dutch title can be rendered as Neglected Formosa.  Formosa was the name Portuguese sailors had given to Taiwan, and it meant beautiful island.  Neglected Formosa was Coyet's defense of his own case.  According to the book, what lost the colony was not the governor's cowardice, but the blindness of the administrators in Batavia.  The warnings had not been taken seriously, Van der Laan had packed up and left without good reason, KU had abandoned the fortress, and help had always come too late and too little.  The book was filled with letters, testimonies, and documents.  Rather than accounting for a defeat, it laid bare the inner workings of an institution.  Even today, every historian who sets out to tell the story of the siege turns to this book, knowing full well that Coyet was anything but impartial.  The state Coxinga founded outlived him by 21 years.  Zheng Jing, who had escaped his father's order of execution, took over the governance of the island and carried on the resistance in the name of the Ming.  But across the sea, the Qing were growing stronger.  In 1683, the Qing navy defeated the Zheng fleet off the Penghu Islands, between Taiwan and the mainland.  The commander of that navy was Shilang.  Years earlier, Shilang had been one of Coxinga's officers.  Because of a quarrel between them, his family had been killed on Coxinga's orders, and he had gone over to the Qing.  Coxinga's grandson, Zheng Kishuang, surrendered, and for the first time, Taiwan was joined to an empire that ruled mainland China.  In the centuries that followed, the memory of Coxinga became something different for everyone who looked back on him.  The Qing dynasty, in time, came to embrace this man who had once been its enemy as a symbol of loyalty, and in the 19th century it allowed an official temple to be built in his honor.  In Japan, it was never forgotten that his mother was Japanese.  The great playwright Chikamatsu Monzaemon turned his life, in 1715, into a puppet play whose title can be rendered as The Battles of Coxinga, and it became one of the best-loved works of the Japanese stage.  In China, Coxinga is remembered as a national hero who drove a foreign colonizer into the sea.  In Taiwan, he is counted among the island's true founders, and in the city of Tainan, a temple dedicated to him is still crowded with visitors today.  For military historians, Zealandia sheds light on another question.  For a long time, it was common to explain Europe's expansion across the world in the 17th century by a decisive advantage in firearms, fortress design and sea power.  Zealandia shows that the picture is more complicated.  The Dutch fortress truly was superior. It could not be taken by assault, and it held Coxinga back for months.  Their ships, too, were genuinely powerful on the open sea.  But on land, a disciplined, experienced army routed the Dutch musket.  Knowledge of the shallow waters, the tides and the storms was on Coxinga's side.  In the end, the fortress fell from a hill pointed out by a deserter, when knowledge taken from Europe was combined with the skill of Chinese artillery.  Technology alone had not decided the victory, the experience of the hands that wielded it had.  Today, in the Anping district of Tainan, you can visit a red brick ruin, all that remains of the walls of Fort Zealandia.  Among the bricks stand old trees, whose roots, over the centuries, have worked their way deep into the wall.  This fortress, which once stood on the shore at the tip of a sandbar, now lies far from the sea.  The lagoon where the ships of the Dutch and of Coxinga once sailed has silted up over time, giving way to the streets of the city.  And on the site of Provintia, on the mainland side, there now rises a Chinese pavilion built in later years.  Two structures that once turned their cannons on each other, now stand side by side on the same city's tourist trail.  We have come to the end of today's episode of Norontari.  Until we meet again in a new story.
