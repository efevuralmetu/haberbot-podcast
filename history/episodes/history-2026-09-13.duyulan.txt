An Austrian soldier had to rise to his feet to load his rifle, the Prussian facing him,  lying flat on the ground, fired five rounds in the same span of time.  That morning the fate of two empires rested on which end of the barrel a gun was loaded  from.  And by evening a new giant had been born in the middle of Europe.  In February of 1866, in the headquarters at Verona in northern Italy, a 61-year-old commander  sat looking for a way to turn down the greatest promotion of his life.  His name was Ludwig von Benedict, and he was the most beloved soldier in the Austrian empire.  Vienna was offering him command of the northern army, to be assembled in Bohemia, command,  in other words, of everything the empire possessed.  Benedict refused three times.  His reason was not an excuse, but a cold diagnosis.  He knew neither that terrain nor that enemy.  To understand the weight of those words, you have to look at where the man came from.  Benedict was born on the 14th of July, 1804, in the town of Sopran in western Hungary,  the son of a provincial physician.  He was Protestant, of Hungarian descent, and within an officer class defined by commanders  who carried the title of count, he remained an outsider all his life.  He owed his rise not to family connections but to his courage, and to the strange, almost  familial devotion his soldiers felt for him.  Among the rank and file he was a legend, a commander who spoke plainly, who stood beside  his men in the trenches, who despised paperwork.  And that entire life had been spent in a single geography, Italy.  He knew every ditch of the Lombard plain, every crossing of the river Mincho, every  hill around Verona.  On the 24th of June, 1859, at Salfarino, as the Imperial Army's center dissolved before  the French, Benedict held the right wing on the ridges of San Martino against the Pied  Montes all day long.  The battle was lost, but on his own front he was not beaten.  He returned to Vienna as the Lion of Salfarino, the one bright name to emerge from a catastrophic  decade for the Empire.  And that was precisely the problem.  What Austria needed in 1866 was not a symbol but a mind that knew the north.  Friction with Prussia had been escalating for months.  Two years earlier the two states had fought shoulder to shoulder against Denmark, taking  the duchies of Schleswig and Holstein together, and had then failed to agree on how to divide  the spoils.  In Berlin there was a minister president named Otto von Bismarck, and he was saying openly  that a two-headed arrangement in the German world could not be sustained.  Vienna's real fear was a war on two fronts.  In the south the Kingdom of Italy wanted Venetia, in the north Prussia could march into Bohemia.  The logic at the decision table ran like this.  Put your best commander where the danger is greatest.  And so Benedict was taken out of Italy, and sent to a country where he had never once  campaigned.  Emperor Franz Josef insisted.  The commander who had written refusal after refusal finally bowed.  He delayed his departure for weeks, but reached Vienna on the 12th of May, 1866.  Archduke Albrecht, sent to the Italian front in his place, would win a brilliant victory  there two months later at Costoza, which is to say Austria had placed both of its commanders  on the wrong front.  It is said that when Benedict arrived at his headquarters, he shut himself in with maps  of Bohemia, trying until past midnight to memorize the names of villages he did not  know.  On paper, the army in his hands was enormous.  Seven corps, a powerful mass of cavalry, and the finest artillery in Europe.  But this army had no staff tradition.  The corps commanders were archdukes and counts, unaccustomed to taking orders, and they did  not speak to one another.  Benedict's own chief of staff, Gideon von Christmanich, was a man who never fully explained  the war plan to his commander, who carried that plan around like a secret.  So the empire had sent a commander into terrain, he did not know, and made his own staff a  stranger to him as well.  In mid-June the war began.  In the assembly of the German Confederation, mobilization against Prussia was put to a  vote on Austria's motion.  Prussia declared the Confederation dissolved and on the 15th and 16th of June marched into  the neighboring German states.  What followed was that short, sharp conflict that would go down in history as the Seven  Weeks War, the one that redrew Europe.  As Benedict dragged his army from Moravia toward Bohemia, he was aware that he was walking  into a sentence he had written himself.  The heaviest thing that can happen to a commander is not defeat, it is having said in advance,  in writing, that he would lose, and being sent anyway.  On the other side, in Berlin, there was a 65-year-old man, frail, silent, very nearly  invisible.  Helmut von Moltke had been chief of the Prussian General Staff since 1857, and to that day  he had never commanded an army in war.  The place where he had proven his military gift, was not the battlefield, but the desk.  Moltke's achievement was to change the very technology with which the art of war was practiced.  In the middle of the 19th century, two new instruments had appeared, the railway and  the telegraph.  The railway could carry an army to the frontier in a matter of days instead of marching it  for weeks.  The telegraph made it possible to issue orders from hundreds of kilometers away.  Moltke fused these two instruments with an institution, the General Staff.  Officers who had passed through the same training, who spoke the same language and thought in  the same way, were distributed among armies far apart from one another.  So so that the commander at the top did not have to spell out every detail, they all worked  towards the same objective.  The figures lay the difference bare.  Prussia had five railway lines reaching towards the theater of war, Austria had one.  Prussia completed its mobilization within weeks, while Austrian troops were still swallowing  dust in marching columns.  And then there was the rifle.  Prussian infantry carried the breech-loading Dracy needle gun.  A soldier could lie flat on the ground, behind cover, and fire four or five rounds a minute.  Austrian infantry, with the muzzle-loading Lorenz rifle, had to stand upright and tip  the barrel skyward, and could barely manage to.  Austria had misread the lesson it learned from the French in 1859, and had adopted a  doctrine resting not on firepower but on the bayonet charge.  That meant attacking in close order, at a run, trying to close chest to chest.  Against that, Austrian artillery was better than Prussia's, the rifled Austrian guns shot  further and more accurately.  The war would come down to the question of on what ground these two advantages would  meet.  Moltke divided his army into three.  On the left wing, Herworth von Bittenfeld's army of the Elbe, roughly 39,000 strong.  In the center, Prince Friedrich Karl's first army of 85,000, and far off on the right.  Crown Prince Friedrich Wilhelm's second army of 100,000.  The three armies were strung out along an arc of some 400 kilometers.  To the classical military mind this was suicide.  The enemy could push into the gaps and crush the armies one by one.  Moltke's principle was precisely the opposite, and it fitted into a single sentence, march  divided, strike together.  Marching divided meant speed and supply, striking together meant catching the enemy from the  front and the flank at the same moment.  The price of this principle was timing, and the price of timing was communication.  At the end of June there was a series of clashes along the frontier.  On the 27th of June, at Trautnau, Ludwig von Gablunds's Austrian corps threw the Prussians  back.  It was the only Austrian victory of the entire campaign.  Dot and Austria paid for it with several times the Prussian casualties.  In those same days, at Nachhaud, at Skalitz, at Gichin, the needle gun showed what it could  do.  Prussian divisions ran up the slopes in dense columns and melted away in front of Prussians  firing from the prone.  On the 1st of July, Benedek sent his emperor a telegram.  He wrote that the army was being dragged towards catastrophe, and begged that peace be made  at any price.  Franz Josef's reply was short, peace was impossible, if a withdrawal could not be avoided, it must  be carried out in good order.  And he added a question, has a battle been fought?  Benedek now had no choice but to fight.  He placed his army on the hills northwest of Konigreits, the fortress town on the bank  of the Elbe.  These high ridges put his guns in a commanding position, but behind them lay a river, if  he were beaten the army would be funneled into a single narrow bottleneck.  The battle would be given two names.  The Prussians used the name of the fortress at their enemy's back, the Austrians and the  French used the name of the small village in the middle of the front.  Sadowa  On the night of the 2nd of July, a cavalry patrol reported to Prussian headquarters that  the Austrian army had not fled, that it was standing on the hills.  When Moltke heard this, he knew the opportunity he had been waiting for had come.  The first army would hold the enemy from the front, while the crown prince's second army  came down on the flank from the north.  There was only one problem.  The crown prince was 40 kilometers away, and the telegraph line to him was not working  that night.  The order went on horseback.  Among the aides who set out at midnight, Lieutenant Colonel Count Finkenstein crossed that distance  at a gallop, through rain, through mud, through darkness, and delivered the order at 4 in  the morning.  The fate of the whole campaign hung, for a few hours, on the speed of a single rider.  The morning of the 3rd of July, 1866, opened with rain and a low mist.  The fields were knee-deep in mud, the wheat was beaten flat, the stream beds were in flood.  Towards 8 o'clock the Prussian 1st army came down to the bistritz, the stream running through  the middle of the front, and began to cross by the bridges around the village of Sadoa.  Facing them, on the lipa heights 2 or 3 kilometers beyond, hundreds of Austrian gun barrels were  waiting.  The moment the Prussians crossed the stream, those guns began to speak, and they did not  fall silent all morning.  The Prussian center was pinned on the open slope, under a fire its own guns could not  reach.  The real slaughter, though, took place on the right, in the woods at the northern end  of the line.  It was called the Sweepwald, the Sweep Forest, for short.  A few kilometers long, thick with old trees, a wood in which no one could see more than  about 20 meters in any direction.  The Prussian 7th Division followed its commander, Eduard von Franzeky, into that forest, and  never came out again.  Benedict's order had been clear, the corps on the right wing were to hold their positions  and keep the Horno's heights against the 2nd army, which might come down from the north.  But the sound of rifle fire drifting out of the woods, and the sheer smallness of the  Prussian division visible outside them, drew two corps commanders into an irresistible temptation.  First count Festetics's 4th corps, then count Thun's 2nd corps threw themselves forward  against orders, and plunged in among those trees.  Two entire corps were spent to crush a single division, and the right wing was left empty.  The forest stripped Austria of every advantage it had.  The artillery could not fire, there was no room for a bayonet charge in close order.  The only thing possible was to shoot between the tree trunks at 15 paces, and that was  precisely the range at which the needle gun was the finest weapon in the world.  Then battalions advanced again and again and again and again were thrown back.  A counter attack led by Colonel Karl von Poch, drove the Prussians back for a moment, then  fire from a Prussian division coming in on the flank all but annihilated that battalion,  and Poch was killed in the fighting.  A fragment of a cannon shell tore off count Festetics's foot.  Anton von Molinari, who took his place, went on pressing into the woods.  Franzeky's division stayed in there for 5 hours.  It had gone in with 9,000 men and lost close to 2,000.  But it held.  The other side paid far more dearly, by some accounts those two Austrian corps lost nearly  10,000 men over a single copse.  Toward midday the Prussian headquarters, on the low rise called the Roscosburg, came face  to face with an unpleasant truth.  King Wilhelm I was there, Moltke was there, and Bismarck was there too, moving about not  in civilian dress but in the uniform of a reserve officer.  The center could not advance, the right wing was bleeding inside a forest, and there was  still no word from the crown prince.  Shells were falling so close that Bismarck is said to have seized the king's bridle to  pull him back out of the line of fire.  A detail Bismarck later recounted conveys the tension of those hours better than any  commander's report.  He held out his cigar case to Moltke.  Indeed were one good cigar and a few ordinary ones.  Moltke leaned over the case, looked without hurrying, picked out the best one and took  it.  In that instant Bismarck was reassured, if the man was still calm enough to make a choice,  things could not be all that bad.  And indeed they were not.  The price the Prussian center had been paying all morning was, in fact, Moltke's plan working.  Bismarck's entire attention and all his reserves had been drawn to the front and to that forest.  The door facing north stood wide open.  Crown Prince Friedrich Wilhelm had been on the march since six o'clock that morning.  He was thirty-four years old, a crown prince known for his liberal leanings, inwardly opposed  to the policy his father was pursuing with Bismarck, on that day none of it mattered  in the slightest.  He had one hundred thousand men in hand, and kilometers of road that had to be pushed through  the mud.  Around half past two in the afternoon, the vanguard of the second army appeared on the  heights to the north.  The Austrian right wing they should have found in front of them was not there, that wing  had been fighting in the sweep forest since morning.  The Prussians climbed an utterly empty ridge line, and when they reached the crest they  saw, spread out below them, the rear of the Austrian army.  The key to the whole position was Klum, the village and hill known as Klum.  It lay right beside Benedict's headquarters.  It commanded the entire front, and at that hour it was undefended.  The Prussian 1st Guard Division, under the command of Wilhelm Hiller von Goertringen,  came up the slope without meeting any resistance at all, and took the hill around three o'clock.  The Austrian batteries were struck while still trying to work out what had happened, as the  first volleys came from behind their own lines.  When Benedict was told that Hullman had fallen, he did not believe it.  He rode forward to see it with his own eyes, and saw the Prussian guards up on the hill.  At that moment the battle was over, the only question left was whether the army could be  saved.  In that last hour the Austrian army showed what it had failed to show all day.  Wilhelm von Ramming's VI Corps made a desperate counter-attack toward the village behind Hullum,  for a while they came within reach of retaking the hill.  Hiller von Goertringen, the commander of the Prussian guards, died of a wound he received  in that fighting.  But the flow of arriving Prussian troops could not be stopped.  At three o'clock Benedict gave the order to withdraw, and to make that order possible  he sacrificed the two finest arms of his army.  The Austrian reserve artillery stayed where it stood in the open ground, battery by battery,  and went on firing, they did not pull back until the guns fell silent.  Then the Austrian and Saxon cavalry, charged the advancing Prussian lines one wave after  another.  In those final charges close to two thousand horsemen and horses were lost.  What was bought in return was a few hours.  And those few hours kept the army of the north from being destroyed altogether.  The retreat turned into a nightmare all the same.  The army poured back toward the single way out behind it, toward the crossings over the  river Elbe and the fortress of Conegretz.  The fortress commander, afraid that the Prussians might slip inside, kept the gates shut, and  he had flooded the surrounding countryside for the sake of the defense.  Thousands of soldiers, gun carriages and horses became entangled on a banked turn to swamp  and at the narrow bridgeheads.  By evening the figures spoke for themselves.  On the Prussian side there were around nine thousand dead, wounded and missing.  Austria and her ally Saxony had lost close to forty-four thousand men, roughly twenty  thousand of them were prisoners.  One hundred and eighty-seven guns and dozens of colors had been left in enemy hands.  In a single day, in a single afternoon, one of the oldest dynastic armies in Europe had  lost a third of its fighting strength.  That evening Benedek sent his emperor a second telegram, the catastrophe he had warned of  two days earlier had come to pass that day.  At Prussian headquarters, a very different sentence was being spoken.  When the crown prince's colors appeared on the ridge to the north, Moltke is said to  have turned to the king and told him that his majesty had won not merely a battle but  the entire campaign.  That same day in Berlin, voting was underway for the Prussian House of Representatives.  The liberals who had resisted Bismarck through all the years he governed without a budget  fell back seriously for the first time in that election.  He had won on the battlefield and at the ballot box on the same day.  In the days after the victory was won, the real quarrel began inside Prussia's own headquarters,  and the outcome of that quarrel determined more than the war itself did.  The army was advancing toward Vienna.  For King Wilhelm I and most of his commanders the matter was self-evident, they would enter  the enemy capital, hold a parade, take territory from Austria, and punish the German states  that had sided with the Habsburgs.  The bill for centuries of Prussian-Austrian rivalry would finally be presented.  Bismarck stood against it alone and found himself in a strange position.  The man accused of starting the war was now trying to stop it.  His argument was not sentimental but cold.  Prussia's aim was not to destroy Austria but to push her out of the German world.  Since that work was done, the Austria of tomorrow ought to be a partner rather than an enemy.  A state that has been humiliated looks for revenge at the first opportunity.  What is more, the clock was running.  In France, Napoleon III had stepped onto the stage with an offer of mediation, and he was  not pleased to see Prussia growing so large within the Prussian army, meanwhile, it was  said that cholera was carrying off more men than the fighting.  If peace were not signed within a week, the victory already won could turn into a European  crisis.  The dispute came to a head in the Moravian town of Nickelsburg.  In his own memoirs Bismarck describes those days as a moment when, unable to persuade  the king, he walked out of the room, so desperate and so close to a nervous collapse that he  considered throwing himself from the window.  The man who unlocked it was the crown prince.  He went in to his father, held a conversation that lasted two hours, and by the time he  came out the king had given way.  Wilhelm wrote a bitter sentence in his own hand in the margin of Bismarck's memorandum,  he said that after his army's brilliant victories he was being forced to bite into this sour  apple.  An armistice was made on the 22nd of July, the preliminary peace of Nickelsburg was signed  on the 26th of July, and on the 23rd of August 1866.  The Treaty of Prague brought the war to an end.  The text is a textbook example of how measured a victor can be.  Austria gave Prussia not a single inch of territory, she paid a symbolic indemnity and  surrendered Venetia to Italy through the hands of Napoleon III.  In return, the German Confederation, which had stood since 1815, was dissolved, and Austria  was shut out of German affairs entirely.  Prussia annexed Hanover, Hesse-Kassel and Nassau, which had stood beside Vienna in the  war, along with the city of Frankfurt and Schleswig-Holstein.  The following year, all the German states north of the River Main were united in the  North German Confederation under Prussian leadership.  The question that had run for a century and a half, around whom would Germany gather,  had been answered in seven weeks.  The consequences spread outward in waves.  In Paris, revenge for Sadoa became a slogan, the French realized too late that a great  power had been born on their own frontier.  And when they faced that power four years later they met the machine of 1866.  On the 18th of January 1871, the German Empire was proclaimed at Versailles, and the man  who took the crown was the same Wilhelm who had been forced to bite the sour apple at  Nickelsburg.  In Vienna, defeat pushed the empire into changing its internal structure.  A compromise with the Hungarians was signed in 1867.  And the Austrian Empire spent the remaining half-century of its life as Austria-Hungary.  In Prussia, the liberal opposition approved the law that, in September of 1866, retroactively  absolved a prime minister who had governed in violation of the constitution, success  had bought the principle.  Every army in Europe took the lesson too, and for the next forty years they copied the  Prussian model, the general staff, conscription and railway planning.  A single man was left outside this new world.  Benedict stepped down from his command on the 26th of July, 1866.  A military inquiry was opened against him, then halted by the intervention of the emperor,  had the case gone forward the decisions of the army and of the court would have stood  trial as well.  In December, the empire's official paper, the Wiener Zeitung, the Vienna Gazette, published  a piece laying the entire responsibility for the defeat on him alone.  Benedict was given no right of reply, he was ordered never to utter a single word about  the circumstances of the battle for the rest of his life, and he obeyed that order.  He withdrew to Grasse, kept silent for fifteen years, and died on the 27th of April, 1881,  without military honors.  His papers, so the story goes, were burned after his death at his own request.  This is what Konigretz really tells us, on the morning of the 3rd of July, it was not  two armies that faced one another, but two centuries.  On one side, the old world, commanded by titles, prizing courage over order, carrying its news  on horseback.  On the other, a new kind of organization, running on railway timetables, telegraph lines  and shared training.  The victor that day was not a commander, but a method.
