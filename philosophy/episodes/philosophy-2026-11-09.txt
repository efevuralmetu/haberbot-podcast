You check their "last seen" one more time and tell yourself it's only because you care. But is that racing heart proof that you love them, or proof that you believe they belong to you? Tonight we ask whether jealousy guards love or quietly strangles it.

It's nearly one in the morning. A phone lies face down on the table, and a short vibration makes it shudder. For a moment the screen glows, then it goes dark again. The phone's owner is in the shower. The other person in the room looks at that light and feels something. Nothing has happened yet. There's no name, no message, no evidence. There is only a vibration, and a question growing fast inside. Who is writing at this hour?

We know that feeling. A knot in the stomach, heat rising at the back of the neck. Within a few seconds the mind builds a whole story: last week's meeting that ran late, the way the phone always seems to be face down lately, the answer that came a little too quickly when asked. The pieces fall into place on their own. And strangely, the darker the picture, the more convincing it looks. In Shakespeare's Othello, Iago calls this feeling the green-eyed monster, and says it mocks the very meat it feeds on. The monster's favorite food is not certainty. It is doubt.

So where does this feeling come from? The first answer that comes to mind is this: I love, and so I am jealous. If I didn't care, the light on that phone wouldn't matter to me. There is some truth in that answer. No one is jealous of someone who has no place in their life. Jealousy shows that a bond exists. But it does not show what kind of bond it is. And that is where the question of this episode begins: is jealousy proof of love, or the sign of something else that has crept into love, the urge to possess?

First, we need to separate two ideas, because in everyday speech they are always getting mixed up. Envy is wanting something that someone else has: the neighbor's car, a colleague's promotion. Jealousy is the fear of losing something you believe is already yours. Envy involves two people: me, and the other who holds what I want. Jealousy involves three: me, the one I love, and whoever might come between us. At the root of jealousy lies not desire, but fear.

What kind of fear? On the surface, the fear of being abandoned. Scratch a little deeper and other layers appear. The fear that my place could be filled by someone else. The fear that I am not good enough, not interesting enough. And perhaps, deepest of all, the fear that without someone's love I would not know who I am. The French thinker Roland Barthes, in the book where he took apart the language of love fragment by fragment, writes that the jealous person suffers four times over: they suffer because they are jealous, they suffer because they blame themselves for being jealous, they suffer because they fear their jealousy will hurt the one they love, and they suffer because they have given in to so ordinary a feeling. Not one of these four has anything to do with the other person. All of them turn within the jealous person's own inner world.

The person looking at the phone isn't really curious about the name on the screen. They want to know whether their own place is secure. The difference looks small, but nearly every philosopher who has thought about jealousy has pointed to exactly this: at the center of jealousy may stand not the beloved, but the lover.

In seventeenth-century Europe, a great wave of thinking about the passions swept through. The philosophers of the age of reason began to see the human being not only as a thinking creature, but as one whose emotions could also be studied in an orderly way. Jealousy did not escape this study.

In sixteen forty-nine, one year before his death, René Descartes published Les Passions de l'âme, The Passions of the Soul, a work in which he defined the passions one by one. Descartes describes jealousy as a kind of fear: a fear tied to the desire to hold on to some good. But what is truly interesting is that he connects this fear to proportion. For him, whether jealousy is honorable depends on the value of what is being guarded, and on whether the fear is justified. A commander defending a vital position deserves praise for staying alert to every possible attack. But for a man who is jealous of his wife, Descartes speaks far more harshly. Such jealousy, in his view, shows that the man does not love her rightly, and that he holds a poor opinion either of himself or of her. In other words, jealousy is a confession that the jealous person does not trust either themselves or the one they love.

Turn back to the scene with the phone, and Descartes' observation is unsettlingly accurate. Two sentences run through the mind of the person staring at that light on the screen: "I am not enough," and "They are not faithful." Both are born less of love than of insecurity.

About thirty years after Descartes, in sixteen seventy-seven, Baruch Spinoza's Ethica, known in English as the Ethics, was published after his death. Spinoza sets out the passions in propositions, like a geometry textbook. His definition of jealousy is just as cool and composed: if a person imagines that the thing they love is joined to someone else by a bond as close as, or closer than, the one that ties them to it, they will feel hatred toward the thing they love and envy toward that other person. Spinoza calls this love mixed with hatred jealousy, and he defines it as a wavering of the mind, born of love and hatred at the same time.

There are two important details inside this definition. The first is the phrase "if a person imagines." For Spinoza, jealousy does not arise from an event itself, but from the picture of that event in the mind. What matters is not what actually happened, but what we believe happened. The second is that love and hatred can exist at the same moment. The jealous person is furious with the one they love and wants to punish them, yet at the same time is terrified of losing them. This swinging back and forth wears a person down, because it means being pulled in two opposite directions at once. In Spinoza's system, freedom means replacing the passive passions with understanding. To the extent that we understand an emotion, we stop being its prisoner. To understand jealousy, to see that it is a product of the imagination, is to loosen the first link in the chain.

In France, in the same century, another figure, less a philosopher than an observer of court life, François de La Rochefoucauld, fit this idea into a single sentence in his book of Maxims, first printed in sixteen sixty-five: in jealousy there is more self-love than love. La Rochefoucauld believed that behind most human behavior lay selfishness, vanity, and the hunger for praise. He read jealousy in that light as well: what is wounded in the jealous person is not their love, but their pride.

The Maxims contain one more sentence on jealousy, and perhaps an even sharper one: jealousy feeds on doubt. The moment doubt turns into certainty, it either becomes rage or comes to an end. This observation explains why jealousy can last so long. Faced with a certain betrayal, a person either breaks and walks away, or forgives. But as long as the uncertainty remains, the mind never stops working. Every glow of a phone screen, every late arrival, every distracted glance becomes fresh fuel.

What these three thinkers share is clear. None of them sees jealousy as a pure measure of love. Descartes sees in it a lack of trust in oneself and in the other; Spinoza, a swing between love and hatred built by the imagination; and La Rochefoucauld, self-love in disguise. But this picture may be one-sided. Let us recall Descartes's commander: if we consider certain things worth protecting, could being watchful on their behalf also be a virtue? The Islamic intellectual tradition approaches this question with a very different concept.

In Turkish, alongside the word for jealousy, kıskançlık, stands an older and weightier word: gayret. Today we mostly use this word to mean effort, as in "make an effort." But its Arabic root describes the protective sensitivity a person feels when someone else reaches for what they hold dear, especially the honor and privacy of their family. In old Turkish texts, gayret and kıskançlık often appear side by side. The Islamic ethical tradition neither glorified this feeling nor rejected it outright. Instead, it tried to draw a boundary around it.

In a narration found in the authentic hadith collections, the jealousy of Sa'd ibn Ubadah, one of the Companions, is described to the Prophet. The Prophet does not condemn it. He says that he himself has more gayret than Sa'd, and that God's gayret is greater still. In this narration, gayret appears as the very opposite of indifference toward what is precious. In this tradition, a person who finds nothing worth protecting, who does not care when the honor of a loved one is violated, is not praised.

Yet within the same tradition, another narration divides this feeling in two. According to a hadith recorded in the collections of Abu Dawud and al-Nasa'i, there are kinds of gayret that God loves and kinds He does not. The beloved kind is the gayret felt when there is a real cause for suspicion. The unloved kind is gayret felt when there is no cause for suspicion at all. This distinction speaks directly to the question of this episode. Gayret is a response, and it is legitimate only when it has a real cause. Jealousy without cause is not a virtue, but a flaw.

We can capture this distinction with another old Turkish word: vehim. Vehim means perceiving something that is not there as though it were, a groundless fancy. If gayret is a well-founded concern, vehim is a suspicion that feeds on itself. Spinoza's emphasis on the imagination echoes here in another language. Both are saying the same thing: the most dangerous form of jealousy is the one that rests not on reality, but on the mind's own fiction.

Surah al-Hujurat in the Quran contains a verse that shows, step by step, how this chain of events unfolds. The verse first tells us to avoid much suspicion, because some suspicion is a sin. Right after that, it says: do not spy on one another's private affairs. The order is striking. First a suspicion is born, and then that suspicion drives a person to go looking for proof. The one who glances at a phone screen in the night soon begins to wonder about that phone's password. The verse counsels us to stop at the very first link in the chain, at the suspicion itself.

Imam al-Ghazali, in his great work written at the end of the eleventh century and the beginning of the twelfth, the Ihya Ulum al-Din, or The Revival of the Religious Sciences, takes up this subject on its own when he discusses the etiquette of marriage. Al-Ghazali's approach is a typical search for the middle way. On one side lies a slackness that is indifferent to a spouse's honor and cares about nothing at all. On the other lies a suspiciousness that watches the spouse constantly and looks for ill intent in every move. Al-Ghazali counts both as faults. He advises against prying into a spouse's private affairs and against thinking ill of them without cause, and he quotes a saying attributed to Ali: Do not be excessively jealous over your wife, or else evil will be imputed to her because of you.

This saying reveals a hidden cost of jealousy. Excessive jealousy does not only wear down the jealous person; it also stains the one who is the object of that jealousy. Someone who is constantly suspected becomes suspect in the eyes of those around them, even if they have done nothing at all. Without realizing it, the jealous person damages the reputation of the very one they love.

The broader framework of Islamic moral philosophy fits this picture as well. In the tradition that runs from Ibn Miskawayh to Nasir al-Din al-Tusi, and from there to Kınalızade Ali Efendi, who wrote the Akhlaq-i Alai in the sixteenth century, virtue is the balance between two extremes. Courage stands midway between cowardice and recklessness; generosity, midway between miserliness and extravagance. Protective zeal, too, can be weighed on these scales. Its deficiency is indifference, not caring for the one you love or for your own honor. Its excess is delusion, becoming a captive of suspicion. The virtue lies between the two: being watchful when there is reason for doubt, and being able to trust in peace when there is none.

There is an interesting similarity between Descartes's commander and this framework. Descartes, too, had considered it honorable to stay on guard when what is being protected is valuable and the threat is real. But there is also a clear difference between the two. While the Islamic tradition ties protective zeal largely to the concepts of honor and privacy, it thinks of jealousy not as a right of ownership, but as a responsibility. This difference brings us to the final question of this episode: could the way we define our relationship with the person we love also shape the form our jealousy takes?

The accounts of Aisha's jealousy show how realistically this tradition looks at human emotion. In one account, Aisha names the Prophet's first wife, Khadija, as the person she was most jealous of, even though she had never seen her. The reason was that the Prophet still spoke of Khadija with love years later, and sent gifts to her friends. There was no living rival. The jealousy was aimed at a memory. It is impossible not to notice that this jealousy rested on no evidence; it was born of a fear and a longing. Another account tells how Aisha, in a moment of anger, broke a dish of food sent by another of the Prophet's wives. The Prophet scolded no one. He said, "Your mother was overcome by jealousy," and sent an unbroken dish to replace the broken one. Jealousy is accepted as part of the human condition. But one does not surrender to it; it is met with calm compassion.

Does the one who loves feel jealousy? An honest answer to this question has to be yes. Love is a bond, and every bond brings with it the possibility of breaking. Someone who claims never to feel jealous is either a saint or someone afraid of attachment. The real question is not whether jealousy exists, but what it turns into.

What decides this is how we see the person we love. If we see them as a possession, jealousy turns into a claim of ownership. The owner of a possession considers himself entitled to know where it is, to control who touches it, and, if need be, to lock it away. Going through a phone, demanding passwords, interrogating someone about who they have been talking to: these are the natural extensions of this way of seeing. And every act of control makes the relationship look a little more like a prison.

The German-born thinker Erich Fromm, in his book To Have or To Be, published in nineteen seventy-six, describes how modern people have turned even love into a mode of having. In the having mode, love becomes an object. It is acquired, kept, guarded, and its loss is feared. In the being mode, by contrast, love is an activity, something done anew again and again. According to Fromm, one human being cannot possess another. The moment we try to possess, we have already begun to kill the thing we love, because what makes it alive and free is precisely that it cannot be possessed.

Islamic thought arrives at the same intuition through a different word: amanah, a trust held on behalf of another. In this understanding, nothing a person holds, not their wealth, not their body, not even the people they love, truly belongs to them in any final sense. All of it has been placed in their care for a time. A verse in Surah al-Ahzab tells of the trust that the heavens, the earth and the mountains shrank from bearing, and that humankind took upon itself. A trust is both a burden and an honor at once. But it is not ownership.

Seeing the person we love as a trust does not make jealousy itself disappear. A trust, too, is guarded; one worries over a trust, too. The difference lies in the direction of that guarding. The owner of a property protects his own rights. The trustee, on the other hand, watches over the well-being of what has been left in his care. The first says, "they are mine." The second says, "I must take good care of them." In the first view, the other person is an object; in the second, they are a human being with a will of their own, a path of their own, secrets of their own. A trustee's jealousy, as al-Ghazali described it, does not spy on the other person, yet neither does it remain indifferent to them.

This way of seeing has a hard side as well, and there is no point in hiding it. A trust can one day be taken back. The person we love may leave, may choose another path, life itself may take them from us. The gaze of ownership resists this possibility; it tightens its control, trying to eliminate the chance of loss before it can ever arrive. The gaze of trust, by contrast, accepts loss as part of life. This leaves a person more vulnerable. But also more free. Because only where control is released does real trust become possible for the first time, and trust is the name not only of faith in the other, but of a person's own inner peace.

Let us return to the phone on the table at night. The screen lights up again. This time, the person looking at that glow pauses for a moment. They feel the knot inside them, but they do not immediately turn it into a story. They ask themselves: Does this fear come from them, or from me? Is there a real reason here, or only my own imaginings? Perhaps the answer is not easy. Perhaps there truly is something that needs to be talked about, and then speaking openly and honestly is the way to honor that devotion. But perhaps there is not. In that case, the hardest thing to do, and perhaps the greatest act of love, is to leave the phone right where it lies, face down.

Is jealousy the proof of love, or the chain of possession? Perhaps it is neither. Perhaps jealousy is simply a test. A mirror that shows whether a person carries their love as property or as a trust, whether they lean on their fear or on their trust. What we see in the mirror depends, in part, on how we choose to look into it.
