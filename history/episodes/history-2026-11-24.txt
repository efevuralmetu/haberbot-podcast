It was the middle of winter, on an unusually mild midday, when the rivets burst like machine-gun fire, and a brown, sticky wave as tall as a five-story building swept into the street at fifty kilometers an hour. That day in Boston, people drowned not in water, but in sugar. And the tank had been leaking for years, and everyone knew it.

On the northern edge of Boston Harbor lay an old neighborhood of narrow, winding streets. It was called the North End. At the start of the twentieth century, the North End was home largely to Italian immigrants. Families lived stacked on top of one another in crowded tenements. The streets rang with the dialects of Naples and Sicily, the smell of bread rose from the bakeries each morning, and children ran along the wharves gathering scraps of wood to burn in the stove at night. Right beside the neighborhood, running parallel to the harbor, was a broad thoroughfare: Commercial Street. Above it, on iron legs, stretched an elevated railway. Trains rumbled past all day long, while below, horse-drawn wagons, trucks, and laborers hauling freight came and went.

In nineteen fifteen, on the harbor side of Commercial Street, at lot number five twenty-nine, something new was added to the neighborhood's skyline. It was a giant cylindrical tank built of riveted steel plates, roughly fifteen meters tall and twenty-seven meters across. It could hold eight point seven million liters of molasses.

Molasses is the dark, heavy, sticky syrup left over after sugarcane is processed and the sugar is extracted. It may rarely appear in kitchens today, but in the America of that era it mattered both in food and in industry. When molasses is fermented, it yields ethyl alcohol. That alcohol could be used to make liquor, but its truly valuable use lay elsewhere. Industrial alcohol was an essential raw material in the production of smokeless gunpowder and other explosives.

The tank belonged to the Purity Distilling Company. That company, in turn, was a subsidiary of a much larger firm, United States Industrial Alcohol. The plan was simple. Molasses shipped in from the Caribbean, especially from Cuba and Puerto Rico, would be unloaded into the tank at Boston Harbor, then carried by rail to the company's distillery in Cambridge, just outside Boston, to be turned into alcohol.

The timing was no accident. In Europe, the First World War had been raging for a year. The United States had not yet entered the war, but it was selling munitions to Britain and France. Thousands of artillery shells were being fired at the front every day, and every shell needed explosives, and every explosive needed alcohol. Prices for industrial alcohol were climbing, and demand kept rising. United States Industrial Alcohol wanted to move fast to meet that demand. A large tank to store molasses was the shortest route to profit.

Nor was the tank's location an accident. It sat right on the waterfront, where ships could dock directly alongside, and the rail line was close by. But the site had a social dimension as well. The North End was one of Boston's poorest neighborhoods, and one of its least politically vocal. Many of its residents were not yet citizens, could not speak English well, and knew no one at City Hall. In Boston's wealthy districts, there would have been lawyers, journalists, and city councilors ready to object to a fifteen-meter steel tank. In the North End, there were none. Most historians believe that this silence played a part in the decision to build the tank there.

And so, in the autumn of nineteen fifteen, driven by the lure of wartime profits and emboldened by the neighborhood's silence, the steel giant rose into place. The neighbors grew used to it. But before long, they began to notice strange things about the tank.

The man responsible for building the tank was named Arthur Jell. Jell was the Purity Distilling Company's treasurer, the manager in charge of its accounts and finances. He was not an engineer, and he was not an architect. As would later come out in court, he could not even read a technical drawing. Even so, the company had handed him the job of overseeing this enormous steel structure. Jell had the tank built by a firm called Hammond Iron Works, and he never sought an independent engineering opinion from anyone. Not a single expert looked over the tank's plans, and no one calculated the load the steel plates would have to bear.

Jell was under heavy pressure from the clock. A ship carrying molasses from the Caribbean was due to reach the harbor by a certain date, and the company wanted the tank ready when it arrived. Construction was crammed into the final weeks. For a tank like this, it was standard practice to fill it completely with water before putting it into service, to test it for leaks and for strength. But this tank was never filled to the brim with water. According to later testimony, only about fifteen centimeters of water was poured into the bottom, and that test was deemed sufficient. Then the first load of molasses was pumped in.
Problems appeared right away. The tank began leaking from the very first time it was filled. Brown molasses seeped drop by drop between the rivets and through the seams where the plates met, leaving long dark streaks down the side of the tank. The company's answer to this was a curious one. Rather than repairing the leaks, it painted the tank brown. That way, the oozing molasses blended into the color of the steel and was far less noticeable.

The people of the neighborhood knew about the leaks, and they made use of them in their own way. Poor families came to the foot of the tank with buckets and pots to collect the molasses that dripped out. Children dipped their fingers into it and licked them clean. At home, it was used to make sweets or added to meals. No one thought of it as a warning sign, or if anyone did, they found no one to tell.

It was said that the tank did not only leak, but also made noises. Isaac Gonzales, one of the workers responsible for its upkeep, described hearing deep groans and creaks coming from the tank when it was full. He said he was so worried that he could not sleep at night for fear the tank would collapse, and that on some nights he got up and went to check on it. Gonzales reported what he had seen to Arthur Jell. Jell, however, did not take him seriously. According to later testimony, Jell closed the matter by telling Gonzales that he did not understand the business, and that the tank was still standing. Some time later, Gonzales left the job.

The root of the problem lay in the steel. Nearly a hundred years later, in twenty fourteen, the engineer Ronald Mayville took a fresh look at the old documents and the tank's specifications. He concluded that the tank's steel plates were thinner than they should have been for a tank meant to hold a liquid of that volume and that weight. What is more, the steel was low in manganese, the element that gives steel its toughness and flexibility. Steel like this becomes brittle in cold weather. In other words, under impact or strain, instead of flexing and bending, it can crack like glass. Mayville also suspected that small cracks may have formed around the rivet holes and grown over time. These cracks were most dangerous at the very bottom of the tank, the place that bore the weight of all the molasses above it.

And molasses is no ordinary liquid. It is roughly forty percent heavier than water. The pressure bearing down on the floor and lower walls of a full tank was far greater than it would have been had the same tank been filled with water. And the tank was constantly being filled and emptied. Each time it was filled, the steel stretched, and each time it was emptied, it relaxed. This rising and falling load was slowly wearing out plates that were already weak.

In April of nineteen seventeen, the United States entered the war. Demand for industrial alcohol rose even higher. In November of nineteen eighteen the war came to an end, but the tank was still hard at work. This time, a different development was on the horizon. A constitutional amendment that would ban alcoholic beverages across the country was being ratified by the states, one after another. Some historians argue that the company wanted to produce and sell as much alcohol as possible before the ban took effect, and that this is why it filled the tank to the brim. This has never been conclusively proven, but the timing is striking.

In the first days of January nineteen nineteen, a ship arriving from the Caribbean unloaded a fresh cargo of molasses. The tank was almost completely full. It held roughly eight point seven million liters of molasses. And the weather was bitterly cold. On the fourteenth of January, the temperature in Boston had dropped as low as minus seventeen degrees.

On the morning of the fifteenth of January, nineteen nineteen, the weather changed suddenly. The temperature, which had been minus seventeen degrees the day before, climbed toward noon to plus four or five degrees. After a long cold spell, it felt like spring. People unbuttoned their coats and lingered outdoors. Commercial Street was as busy as ever. City workers were working on the road, dockworkers were hauling cargo, horse-drawn wagons and trucks rolled past. In the canteen of one of the city buildings, people were eating lunch. At Fire Station Number Thirty-One, near the tank, firefighters were playing cards. Children, as usual, were gathering scraps of firewood beside the railway tracks.

At around twelve forty, there came a sound that the witnesses would never forget. Some described a deep rumble. Others said they heard a rapid series of sharp cracks, like a machine gun firing. These sounds were the rivets, snapping off and shooting out one after another. The steel plates were tearing apart. Then the body of the tank split open, and the millions of liters of molasses inside poured out all at once.

When a tank leaks, the liquid flows out slowly. But this tank did not open slowly; it burst apart almost in an instant. What emerged was not a stream, but a wave. According to the witnesses' accounts and later calculations, the wave of molasses reached seven point five meters at its highest point and moved at roughly fifty-five kilometers per hour. In other words, faster than a running person could escape.

The torn steel pieces of the tank were flung in every direction. One of the large plates struck the iron supports of the elevated railway above the street and bent the structure. The tracks sagged. At that very moment, a train was approaching. When the motorman saw the tracks collapsing ahead of him, he stopped the train at the last moment. Another train had passed by there only moments before. By a matter of seconds, one disaster was spared from having another added to it.
The wave struck the buildings right beside the tank first. Wooden houses were torn from their foundations. One structure, it was said, was shoved with such force that it seemed to be ripped from its place and flung aside. Fire Station Number Thirty-One was knocked off its footing. Molasses flooded the inside of the station, and part of the building collapsed. The firefighters inside were trapped beneath the wreckage and the molasses.

What made molasses so dangerous as a liquid was its weight and its stickiness. A person swept away by water can try to swim, to grab hold of something, to keep their head above the surface. Someone caught in a wave of molasses could do none of that. The more they moved their arms and legs, the deeper they sank. Molasses filled their mouths and noses, making it impossible to breathe. Horses and wagons were dragged along inside the wave. Street workers, dock workers, and passersby were all swept away.

Children gathering firewood along the railway tracks were also in the wave's path. Ten-year-old Pasquale Iantosca was caught by the wave and killed. Maria Di Stasio, also ten years old, had been gathering wood with her siblings. She, too, did not survive. Some children, though, were caught at the edge of the wave and carried along on its crest, and they lived. One survivor described being carried on top of the wave like a surfer, and then simply set down somewhere.

The wave did not only move forward. The molasses spread into the narrow streets and flowed toward the wharf and the harbor. In some places, within just a few minutes, a sticky layer nearly one meter thick had formed. And that layer was changing fast. The weather may have turned mild, but it was still January. The warm molasses pouring out of the tank cooled as it met the cold air, and as it cooled, it grew thicker still. When the wave finally came to rest, what it left behind was a swamp that kept hardening, seizing and holding fast to everything that fell into it.

All of this happened within a few minutes. At twelve forty, it had been an ordinary lunch hour. A few minutes later, a stretch of Commercial Street had become a field of sticky brown wreckage.

The first help arrived very quickly. Near the tank, a training ship lay moored in the harbor: the USS Nantucket. When the naval cadets aboard heard the sound of the blast, they came running to the scene. The Boston police, the fire department, soldiers from the army, and Red Cross volunteers soon reached the area as well. But once the rescuers set to work, they realized they were facing something none of them had ever encountered before.

The molasses also trapped anyone who stepped into it. Rescuers struggled forward, sunk to their knees, and in some places to their waists. Every step was a fight. To pull the injured free, the molasses had to be scraped away by hand and with shovels. Voices and groans could be heard from beneath the wreckage, along with the screams of horses. Many of the horses, injured and stuck fast in the molasses, could not be saved. Police and soldiers were forced to shoot them so they would not suffer.

One of the longest and hardest rescue efforts took place at Fire Station Number Thirty-One. Firefighters worked for hours to reach their comrades trapped beneath the collapsed building. Some of those caught under the wreckage along with the molasses were rescued. But firefighter George Layhe was pinned beneath the collapsed part of the building. His fellow firefighters heard his voice and tried to reach him, but they could not. Layhe drowned in the molasses that surrounded him. His body could only be recovered hours later.

The injured were carried to nearby hospitals. The Red Cross turned a building in the neighborhood into a temporary hospital. Doctors and nurses now faced a problem they were not used to. Before they could examine their patients, they first had to clean the molasses off them. It clung to clothing, to hair, to skin, and it covered the wounds. Some patients could not be made to breathe until the molasses had been cleared from their mouths and noses.

The search went on without pause for four days. As the molasses hardened, the work grew even harder. Some of the victims' bodies were so coated in molasses that they were difficult to identify. For others, weeks went by before they were found at all. The bodies of some victims who had been swept into the harbor were only pulled from the water months later.

In the end, the toll was confirmed. Twenty-one people had lost their lives, and around one hundred fifty had been injured. Among the dead were ten-year-old children, and elderly laborers too. City workers, dock workers, wagon drivers, a firefighter. Most of them lived in or near the North End, ordinary people doing their jobs or simply passing by at an ordinary lunch hour.

Beyond burying the dead and healing the injured, there was another task just as hard: the cleanup. Molasses had spread across every part of Commercial Street. The streets, the houses, the shops, the supports of the elevated railway, and the wharves of the harbor were all coated in a brown layer. At first they tried washing it away with fresh water, but that did not work. Eventually they found that the most effective method was to use water from the harbor. Fireboats pumped salt water from the harbor and sprayed it onto the streets at high pressure. The salt water broke down and softened the molasses. Then workers mixed the molasses with sand and scraped it away. The job took weeks. In all, it is estimated that the cleanup took eighty-seven thousand man-hours of work.
Molasses did not stay at the scene of the disaster. Rescuers, reporters and curious onlookers carried it across the entire city on their shoes and their clothing. Sticky traces turned up in streetcars, in telephone booths, on the door knockers of people's homes. In the months after the disaster, the water of Boston Harbor looked brown. According to some accounts, it was not until summer arrived that the water finally returned to its normal color.

And then there was the smell. For decades, longtime residents of the North End would describe how, on hot summer days, a sweetish scent of molasses rose up through the neighborhood. The molasses had seeped between the streets, into the foundations of old buildings, beneath the wooden floorboards. The neighborhood remembered its catastrophe through the smell.

But amid all this suffering, Boston's real question had yet to be answered: why had the tank collapsed? And who was responsible?

The United States Industrial Alcohol Company had its answer ready. According to the company, the tank had not simply collapsed on its own. Someone had bombed it.

In the atmosphere of those days, the claim did not sound strange at all. In the immediate aftermath of the First World War, fear of political violence ran deep across America. The revolution in Russia, strikes among industrial workers, and anarchist groups dominated the headlines. The followers of the Italian anarchist Luigi Galleani in particular were known in those years for their bombing campaigns. And the North End itself was a neighborhood of Italian immigrants. The company argued that the tank had been used for industrial alcohol, in other words for the production of explosives, and that this could have made it a target for anti-war anarchists. For the company, it was a very convenient explanation. If the blame could be placed on an outside enemy, no one would ever ask the question of negligence.

The families affected by the disaster, however, did not accept that explanation. Relatives of the dead, the injured, and those who had lost their homes and livelihoods sued the company. The lawsuits were combined and became one of the first major class action cases in the state of Massachusetts. There were more than one hundred plaintiffs. To hear and weigh the evidence in this complex case, the court appointed an independent auditor. He was a respected attorney named Hugh Ogden.

The hearings Ogden conducted went on for years. Nearly three thousand witnesses testified. The result was tens of thousands of pages of testimony. The courtroom turned into an engineering lecture hall. The company's experts argued that the tank had been sound and could only have collapsed the way it did because of an explosion. The plaintiffs' experts, on the other hand, explained that the tank had given way under its own weight, that the steel was inadequate, and that the rivets and plates could not bear the load.

Over the course of the hearings, the story of the tank came to light. When Arthur Jell admitted on the witness stand that he could not read a technical drawing, it caused a sensation. It emerged that the tank had never been fully tested with water. The leaks, the paint used to hide them, the neighborhood residents collecting molasses in buckets, Isaac Gonzales's warnings — one by one, they all entered the record. Gonzales himself testified as well, describing the sounds the tank used to make and how his warnings had been received. The company's lawyers tried to cast doubt on his credibility, but his account matched the testimony of the other witnesses.

The bombing claim, meanwhile, rested on no concrete evidence. No trace of explosives had been found at the scene, and there was neither an attacker nor any group claiming responsibility. The "machine-gun fire" that witnesses had heard could be explained not as a bomb, but as the sound of rivets snapping.

In nineteen twenty-five, Hugh Ogden released his report. His conclusion was unequivocal: the tank had not been built strongly enough for the load it carried. Its design was inadequate, its steel was inadequate, and it had never been inspected. As for the bombing claim, he rejected it for lack of supporting evidence. Ogden held the United States Industrial Alcohol Company responsible for the disaster. The company agreed to pay the plaintiffs a total of roughly six hundred twenty-eight thousand dollars in damages. The share for the families of those who had died came to about seven thousand dollars per person. In the money of the day, that was no small sum, but it was just as clear that it could never take the place of a child or a father.

The true significance of the case, however, lay less in the compensation than in the message it sent. A powerful corporation had been defeated, after years in court, by the people of a neighborhood far weaker than itself. And that defeat rested squarely on a question of engineering. Money and land were not enough to put up a structure. It took an expert who knew the structure was safe and who would vouch for it with their own signature.

In the years following the disaster, Boston and the state of Massachusetts turned this lesson into rules. It became mandatory for the construction plans of large structures to be calculated and approved by a licensed engineer or architect, and for those plans to bear that expert's seal. It was no longer legally possible for a manager who could not read a building's drawings to put up an eight point seven million liter tank without consulting anyone. Similar regulations spread to other states over the following decades. Today, no one in America finds it strange that construction plans require an engineer's approval, but the Boston molasses disaster is remembered as an important turning point in establishing that practice.
The timing of the disaster held a certain irony. On January sixteenth, nineteen nineteen, the day after the collapse of January fifteenth, the constitutional amendment banning alcoholic beverages reached the number of states required for ratification. With that, it became final, and it took effect one year later. A tank built to turn molasses into alcohol had burst on the very eve of the day alcohol was outlawed.

The science behind the disaster also stirred curiosity for many years. Witnesses described how the molasses could seize and hold a person so quickly, and why rescuers struggled so hard. In twenty sixteen, Nicole Sharp, a fluid dynamics researcher from Harvard University, and a student, Jordan Scott, set out to examine this question scientifically. They tested molasses at different temperatures and modeled the conditions of that day. Their conclusion was this: inside the tank, the molasses had been relatively fluid, thanks to the warming air and a recently added load. Once it escaped the tank, however, it was quickly exposed to the cold air. As it cooled, its fluidity dropped sharply, and the molasses grew thicker and thicker. For those caught in the wave, the result was horrifying. The liquid that surged forward in the first seconds, sweeping people along, had within a few minutes thickened almost like cement, leaving those trapped inside unable to move. This also explained why the rescuers had struggled so much.

Today, where the tank once stood on Commercial Street, there is a park. A baseball field, a children's playground, and courts where older residents play bocce. At the entrance to the park, a small plaque put up in nineteen ninety-seven recalls the disaster and those who died. In twenty nineteen, the hundredth anniversary of the disaster, memorial ceremonies were held in Boston, and the names of the twenty-one victims were read aloud. An event that for years had been remembered as a neighborhood tale, and sometimes even as the subject of a joke, was at last remembered again with the seriousness it deserved.

At first glance, the Boston molasses flood looks like a strange footnote in history. A sugar syrup that swallowed part of a city. But behind this strangeness lies a very familiar story: shortcuts taken for quick profit, warnings ignored, leaks hidden from view, and a negligence whose price was paid by the quietest people of all. The brown wave that covered Commercial Street that day receded, the streets were washed down with salt water, and in the end even the smell faded away. What remained is the engineer's signature found today on the plans of every major structure. That signature owes a great deal to Boston's twenty-one dead, and to the families who sought justice for them for years.
