While 700,000 people in the city were starving to death, tons of rice, corn and potatoes sat in the  basement of a single building. The men who stood guard over that basement starved to death as well,  without ever laying a hand on the sacks. To them those seeds were not food,  they were the bread of the world that would come after the war.  Picture a library. It has shelves, it has catalog cards, it has numbered boxes.  But not one book in this library is made of paper. Each one is a handful of seed.  Some of it wheat, some barley, some a potato tuber, some a mountain oat whose name you have never heard.  On every box it is written where it came from, the Andean slopes of Peru, the highlands of Ethiopia,  the dry valleys of Afghanistan, the rice terraces of Taiwan.  And every book in this library is alive. Put it in the right soil, and it is born again.  The man who built this library was called Nikolay Vevelov. He was born in 1887 in Moscow, the son  of a father who had been a village boy himself before becoming a merchant. The childhood stories  his father told always came back to the same thing, famine. The famines of the Russian  countryside that returned at intervals, emptied villages, drove people to eat grass.  Vevelov gave his whole scientific life to a single question.  What has to be done so that it never happens again? The answer lay somewhere that sounds  entirely obvious to us today, but that nobody at the time was thinking about on this scale  in diversity. Vevelov knew that a single variety of wheat sown across a field was fragile,  however productive it might be. A new disease, a new insect, a drought lasting a few years could  wipe that variety off the face of the earth. But peasants all over the world had spent thousands  of years sifting and selecting, keeping the varieties that endured in their own valleys,  in their own climate. Somewhere out there was a rye that stood up to the frosts of Siberia.  There was a barley that could take salted soil. There was a wheat that never caught rust.  Find them, bring them together, and you would hold a gene pool capable of saving the agriculture  of the future. What is more, Vevelov also described where these seeds were to be looked for.  According to the theory he developed on the origin of cultivated plants,  every cultivated plant had certain centers on the globe where its diversity was most concentrated.  Those were the places where that plant had been domesticated.  To this he added an idea, he called the law of homologous series.  The tendency of related species to produce similar variations.  This gave the botanist a kind of power of prophecy. You could say in advance that a  trait you had seen in one species might be found somewhere in its sister species too.  When he set out this idea at a congress in 1920, the reaction of the scientists in the hall was  this, biology has finally found its own men to live. And then he picked up his suitcase and went  out to collect the world. He went to Iran, to Afghanistan, to Ethiopia, to Japan, to Korea,  to Mexico, to Brazil, to Algeria. There were mountains he crossed on the back of a mule,  borders he crossed at the risk of arrest, nights he spent shaking with malaria.  Over the course of his life he went on 115 collecting expeditions and brought back  material from 64 countries. Every time he returned the suitcases were full of seed,  and on every seed packet, in that small neat handwriting, was written where it had been taken,  at what altitude it grew, what name the peasant had given it.  This treasure was gathered in Leningrad, in an old ministry building, looking out over St. Isaac  Square. The institution bore the name All Union Institute of Plant Breeding, let us simply call  it the Plant Institute. At the far end of the square stood the golden dome of the cathedral,  inside the building, in rooms running the length of the corridor, in wooden drawers,  in tin boxes, in thick paper envelopes, sat the largest seed collection in the world.  By 1940 the number of samples on the shelves had reached 250,000.  At that date there was nothing like it anywhere on earth.  America had collecting programs, so did Germany, but none of them was on this scale,  this systematic, this meticulous. And it was not a museum. The institute was obliged to keep the  collection alive. Seed does not live forever, within a few years it loses its power to germinate.  So every sample had to be put in the soil at set intervals, harvested again,  renewed. That was what the experimental stations set up across the country were for.  The station at Pavlovsk, south of Leningrad had been opened for exactly that purpose in 1926.  Which means that those drawers in the building were in fact the heart of a giant,  constantly turning agricultural machine. Dozens of botanists, agronomists and technicians had  given their lives to a single group of plants. One concerned himself with nothing but oats.  One with nothing but rice. One with nothing but groundnuts.  One of them, the man who held tens of thousands of potato samples in his care,  believed that the potato was the second thing after bread for humankind,  because that was what Pavlov had told him. These people were the guardians of the largest  seed collection in the world. A few years later that sentence would stop being a figure of speech.  The cracking of the world Pavlov had built, began not in the field, but at the lectern.  In the late 1920s, a name rose in Soviet agricultural circles, Trofim Lysenko.  He was an agronomist working at an experimental station in Azerbaijan,  thin on academic training but bold in the field. His fame came from the claim that  by soaking seed before sowing and holding it in the cold, winter wheat could be adapted to spring.  The method itself was not new and its effect was limited, but Lysenko's presentation was new.  He spoke not in the language of the laboratory, but in the language of the peasant.  He did not promise years of crossbreeding work. He promised harvests that would multiply within  a few seasons. For an administration wrestling with the famine that had followed collectivization,  an administration that wanted results, this was exactly the voice it had been waiting for.  As Lysenko rose, his claims grew with him. He argued that acquired characteristics passed  to later generations, that the environment could directly need a plant's heredity.  This ran against everything genetics had accumulated up to that day.  But Lysenko did not conduct the argument on scientific ground. He declared genetics a  bourgeois science, abstract, cut off from the people, foreign. He put not his opponents ideas  but their loyalty under question. Vavilov supported him at first. He thought it natural  to open a door to a young eager researcher, for a few years he gave his work room. But as Lysenko's  claims stopped being testable and turned into a system of belief, Vavilov began to push back.  At one plant breeding congress after another, the two faced each other. Lysenko accused him  of being a Mendelist Morganist, that is of serving an imported and harmful scientist.  At a meeting in 1939, Vavilov spoke that famous line of his,  we shall go to the pyre, we shall burn, but we shall not renounce what we believe.  This was one of the most dangerous sentences that could be uttered in the Soviet Union in the year  1939. The ring around the institute had already begun to tighten in those years.  Some of Vavilov's close colleagues were arrested in the late 1930s.  Some were sent to the camps, some never came back.  Inside the institution a machinery of denunciation was at work. His expeditions abroad were spoken of  as possible espionage, and his correspondence with foreign scientists went into the files.  On the 6th of August 1940, Vavilov was doing fieldwork in the western territories,  the Soviet Union had newly annexed. A van caught up with him out in the open country.  He was told there was urgent business in Moscow. When his companions came back to camp that evening,  his bag and the plants collected that day were still lying there.  His interrogation lasted 11 months, and ran through more than 400 sessions.  The accusations packed into the file were built around sabotage against the country  and collaboration with foreign intelligence. Not one of them had any connection to the truth.  In the summer of 1941, he was sentenced to death. The sentence was later commuted to 20 years  imprisonment. In the autumn of that same year, as the Germans drew closer to Moscow,  he was transferred to the prison in Saratov. It was his cellmates who told the rest.  In those cells, where nobody had anything left to say to anybody, Vavilov had begun to teach.  Genetics, plant geography, the history of world agriculture.  A course of more than a hundred hours, with no blackboard, no books, no paper. The man who had  built the largest seed collection in the world could now pass on only what was in his memory.  The building in Leningrad, meanwhile, had been left headless.  Its founder's name could no longer be spoken, the logic by which he had arranged the shelves,  which expedition corresponded to which drawer, why the collection mattered so much.  Now lived only in the heads of the people who worked there.  And those people knew perfectly well what it meant to stand by that collection in a country  where the founder of their own institution had been taken in as a traitor. A year later,  the meaning of standing by it would change once more. On the 22nd of June, 1941, Germany attacked  the Soviet Union. The armies on the northern front advanced quickly through the Baltic,  and at the end of their route stood Leningrad. In the city the children were evacuated first,  then factories began to be dismantled and carried east.  The institute saw the danger too, and applied for the collection to be taken out of the city.  But in the first months of a war, on an evacuation list where tank factories and  artillery ammunition were queuing up, hundreds of heavy crates full of seed were nobody's priority.  The answer was slow in coming. Then there was no longer a door left to answer it.  On the 8th of September 1941, German units cut the last land corridor south of Lake Ladoga.  Every road into Leningrad was closed. Inside the city were more than 2 million people,  the largest seed collection in the world, and a steadily shrinking stock of food.  In those same days, one of the city's main food depots caught fire in a bombardment.  Flour, sugar and oil burned in the blaze, the melting sugar soaked into the ground.  The following winter people would dig up that earth, fill jars with it and sell it as sugared  mud. The bread ration melted away within weeks. By November those in heavy labor were getting  250 grams a day, and everybody else 125. What is more, there was less and less flour in that bread.  Cellulose, bran waste, linseed cake, whatever could be found went into the dough.  In December the temperature fell below minus 30. Water pipes burst, the electricity went out,  the trams froze where they stood. People began carrying water by the bucket from holes cut in  the ice of the river Neva. The building on St. Isaac's Square entered that winter unprepared.  The glass had long since gone with the pressure of the bombardments,  they closed the windows with plywood and boards. There was no heating. Breath fogged in the  corridors. The staff moved the most fragile parts of the collection into the inner rooms  of the building, to the points furthest from any window. They raised the boxes off the floor and  locked them into metal cabinets. Because cold and damp were not the only enemies of the seed.  As the city's every store of food ran out, the rats got everywhere with an unbelievable hunger.  The people at the institute saw the rats knocking boxes over, gnawing through paper envelopes.  They tied the boxes with iron bars, insulated the feet of the cabinets, and in some rooms spent the  night banging on tins to keep the rats away with the noise. And there were human beings.  In that city hunger had turned every door into a possibility.  In the institute stores there was rice, wheat, corn, peas, lentils, almonds. A heap of food  that today could be measured in tons, sitting in locked rooms, in an order known only to the  botanists. The staff set up a system of watches. Rooms were kept locked, the keys were divided up,  nobody went into a store alone. Night watches were stood in pairs, both for security and so  that the person on watch would not freeze and fall asleep. When the air raid sirens sounded,  it was also their job to climb to the roof of the building and put out incendiary bombs with sand.  One fire would have meant twenty years of expeditions turned to ash.  They were not the only side that knew what the collection was worth,  either. The German Agricultural Administration had been interested in the genetic material  at Soviet research stations from the very beginning. In the summer of 1943 a collecting  detachment formed within the SS toured the research stations of occupied Ukraine and  Crimea with two lorries and gathered up a portion of the samples Vavilov had spread across the  country. They never reached the main collection in Leningrad. The city was besieged, bombed,  left to starve, but it did not fall. And so the largest seed library in the world stayed for 872  days inside a building that was wasting away at the same rate as the body heat of the people  protecting it. January and February of 1942 were the darkest months of the siege.  Thousands of people were dying in the city every day, bodies were carried on sledges,  could not be buried, and often waited on street corners. The number who died of hunger over the  course of the siege is most commonly estimated at around 700,000. The people of the plant institute  were among that number. Dmitry Ivanov, the botanist responsible for the rice collection,  worked among tens of thousands of packets of rice. Most of them had come from Vavilov's Asian  expeditions, each packet was the record of a separate village, a separate water regime,  a separate ripening time. Ivanov spent that winter protecting these packets, and in the  winter of 1942 he died of hunger in his own room. When he was found dead, thousands of packets of  rice stood around him. Alexander Shchukin, the groundnut specialist, was found at his desk.  In his hand was a packet from his own collection. Groundnuts were among the  oiliest material in that building, the one carrying the densest calories.  Shchukin had not opened the packet. Georgie Cryer, the head of the medicinal plants department,  died. Lilia Rodina, the oat specialist, died. In the institute's own account, these deaths were  for many years spoken of as nine people. But more recent work based on the archives shows that the  number of staff who died of hunger over the course of the siege was more than twice that.  None of these deaths was a suicide. None of them was a theatrical gesture, either.  Nobody at the institute had bound anybody with an oath. According to what the survivors said  afterwards, no such ceremony, no such scene of swearing, ever took place. Only, everybody knew  the same thing. If those seeds were eaten, one person would be full for one meal that day,  and a variety that was the result of centuries of selection would be wiped off the earth.  Among them were people who understood better than anyone, what agriculture actually is.  They knew how a variety is lost and how it never comes back. The war was going to end.  The fields of the country were going to be sown again. What would be sown in those fields  was in this building. One of the survivors, describing that winter, said that the hardest  part of hunger was not being unable to eat. The hardest part was getting up in the morning.  Lifting yourself out of bed, putting on your coat, walking down the dark corridor and opening  the lock, counting the boxes, writing that day's note in the register.  Every day, again. At the very time these people were dying, the founder of their institution  was dying of the same cause, 1200 kilometers to the southeast, in the prison at Saratov.  Vavilov grew steadily weaker through 1942, in prison conditions he went into a collapse  brought on by malnutrition and died on the 26th of January, 1943, at the age of 55.  The cause of death, in the medical term that appears in the records, was dystrophy.  That is, hunger. The man who had traveled 64 countries to save the world from famine  died of hunger. The people who protected the seeds he had collected died of hunger too.  There was only one difference between them, the ones in Leningrad had food  and did not eat it. Vavilov never learned what his colleagues had done.  They in turn, did not know that their director was already dead. The news of his death reached  his family only years later, and the true location of his grave was never fully disclosed.  He is accepted to have been buried in a common cemetery in Saratov.  In the spring of 1942, as the siege entered its second year, those still standing at the  institute took on an impossible piece of work. The problem was this. A potato cannot be stored  like a seed. A tuber is living tissue, within a few months it either rots or sprouts itself away.  The only way to keep the thousands of potato varieties in the collection alive was to put  them in the soil. And the soil was outside the city, and outside the city was the front.  Vadim Lechnovich, the man in charge of the potato collection, and his wife and colleague Olga  Voskresenskaya kept the tubers alive through the winter in the most suitable room in the building,  regulating the temperature by hand and hauling the fuel for the stove themselves.  In spring they loaded the tubers into rucksacks and hand carts and went out to the fields on the  outskirts of the city. Fields within artillery range. Part of the planting was done between  rounds of shellfire, in windows of a few hours a day. They tended the crop through the summer,  lifted it in the autumn, stored it again for the winter.  They repeated this every year that the siege lasted. On the 27th of January, 1944, the siege  was broken. That evening, for the first time in three years, what stood over the city was not  bombardment but celebration fire. When the inventory was taken at the institute, the picture  that emerged was this, the collection was where it should be. It had not been eaten, not been stolen,  not been burned. The registers had been kept complete right through the war.  Which box had been moved where which sample had been renewed and when was all written down.  Part of the seed had lost its power to germinate over the long cold years and had to be rescued by  sowing. The years that followed went into exactly that. In a country coming out of a war, with  burned stations and missing staff, the collection was returned to the soil sample by sample and  renewed. But the story did not end there and what came next was not the ending the people in that  building deserved. In 1948 Lysenko won his final victory. At the August session of the Academy of  Agricultural Sciences, genetics was formally declared a false science, defending chromosomal  heredity stopped being an opinion and became an offense. Courses were removed from the universities,  laboratories were closed, hundreds of researchers were put out of their jobs.  The Plant Institute took its share, the generation Vavilov had raised was dispersed.  The institution that had guarded the collection through starvation was pruned back in peacetime.  The price Soviet agriculture paid in this period would be discussed at length in the harvest  arguments of the decades that followed. Lysenko's grip only began to break from the middle of the  1950s, after Stalin's death. Vavilov was formally rehabilitated in 1955, all the charges were dropped.  His name slowly came back into the publications. In 1968, the Institute in Leningrad was named  after him. Today the world knows that institution simply as the Vavilov Institute. The collection,  for its part, went on growing. Today it holds more than 330,000 samples and among them are  varieties found nowhere else on earth whose wild relatives disappeared long ago. In 2010, a part  of this inheritance came under threat once again, the land of the Pavlovsk, experimental station  south of Leningrad was put up for sale for housing construction. That was where the collection of  fruit and soft fruit was held, the collection that can only be kept alive as living plants,  thousands of trees and bushes that cannot be put in a crate and moved. The scientists from all over  the world started a campaign, the matter was carried into the international press, and the  sale process was stopped. Vavilov's idea itself is today buried in the ice of the Arctic.  The global seed vault opened in 2008, in Norway's Svalbard archipelago is precisely the modern form  of what he imagined, a shelter carved into frozen rock, holding the backup of every gene bank in the  world. Among the more than a million samples in the vault are boxes sent from the Vavilov Institute,  which means that the descendants of those same varieties, protected in Leningrad for 872 days  from rats, from cold, from bombs and from hunger, now lie hidden inside the Arctic.  The real business of the seed library was never the past.  Nobody collected a mountain weed in Ethiopia as a keepsake. Those seeds were the carriers of the  answer to a disease that has not yet appeared, a drought that has not yet happened, a problem  nobody has yet given a name to. Today, breeders searching for new varieties under climate change  are looking at the very samples that were brought down from a mountain on the back of a mule a  century ago. In the winter of 1942, in that cold building on Saint Isaac Square, the man who died  without opening the packets of rice in front of him could not have known this. But it was for  exactly this that he died.
