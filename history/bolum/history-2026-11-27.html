On the morning of the first of November, seventeen fifty-five, every church in Lisbon was ablaze with candles, and half the city was at prayer. At twenty minutes to ten, the ground shook three times. When the survivors ran to the riverbank to escape, they found that the waters of the Tagus had drawn back, laying bare the shipwrecks on the riverbed. Then the sea came back.

In the autumn of seventeen fifty-five, Lisbon was one of the richest ports in Europe. The city spread across the slopes of seven hills on the northern shore of the broad estuary where the Tagus River opens into the Atlantic Ocean. Its population stood at around two hundred thousand. That figure placed it among the most populous cities on the continent, alongside a handful of others such as London, Paris and Naples. Ships docking in its harbor brought gold and diamonds from Brazil; spices, silk and porcelain from India and the Far East; and ivory from the coasts of Africa. From the same harbor sailed the ships that carried enslaved people, taken by force to the far side of the Atlantic. Lisbon's wealth had this dark face as well.

The real fortune came from Brazil. Veins of gold discovered in the Minas Gerais region at the start of the century had brought the Portuguese crown an extraordinary income. The previous king, John the Fifth, had spent this money lavishly. He built an enormous palace and monastery at Mafra, north of Lisbon, and brought works of art, books and holy relics from Rome. The great aqueduct that carried clean water into the city was also a work of his reign. Its pointed arches, spanning the Alcântara valley, were considered the pride of the engineering of the age.

In seventeen fifty, his son Joseph the First took the throne. The new king loved music and opera. Beside the Ribeira Palace, the Riverside Palace, on the banks of the river, he had an opera house built. Known as the Tagus Opera, the building opened in the spring of seventeen fifty-five. It was spoken of as one of the most magnificent theaters of its time, but it would stand for only a few months.

The Ribeira Palace was the heart of the city. The broad square in front of it was called the Palace Square. One side of the square looked directly onto the river, and ships would moor almost at the foot of the palace. The palace library was estimated to hold seventy thousand volumes. On its walls hung paintings by Titian, Rubens and Correggio. Its archives preserved documents from Portugal's Age of Discovery: detailed records of Vasco da Gama's voyages, maps and ships' logs. Nearby stood the India House. In the warehouses of this institution, which managed the eastern trade, lay piles of spices and cloth.

Behind the palace and the square, narrow, winding streets climbed toward the hills. This medieval street pattern was interwoven with neighborhoods dating back to the Moorish era. The houses leaned against one another, some rising five or six stories high. Above the streets rose the towers of hundreds of churches, monasteries and chapels. Lisbon was a deeply devout city. The Inquisition was still at work, and religious ceremonies set the rhythm of the city's calendar. At the edge of Rossio Square stood the great Royal Hospital of All Saints. Up on the hill, the Gothic church of the Carmo Convent looked down over the city.

Not only the Portuguese lived in the city. English merchants, drawn by port wine and the wool trade, had formed a large colony. French, Italian, Dutch and German merchants, clergy and sailors wandered the harbor. Known as much for its religious traditions as for its trade in gold and spices, this capital considered itself one of the safest places in the world.

That year, the first of November fell on a Saturday. In the Catholic calendar, the first of November is All Saints' Day, the great feast on which all the saints are remembered. Early in the morning, candles were lit in every church in the city, and altars were decked with flowers. Most of the population had put on their finest clothes to attend morning Mass. The weather was clear and calm. The royal family, however, was not in the city that morning. They had attended Mass at an early hour, and then, at the princesses' request, set off to spend the holiday at their summer residences on the riverbank in Belém, to the west of the city. It was an ordinary family decision, but it would save the king's life.

At twenty minutes to ten, the ground began to shake.

The epicenter of the earthquake lay far from the city. According to what we know today, it was in the Atlantic Ocean, some two hundred kilometers off Cape Saint Vincent, at the southwestern tip of Portugal. In this region, where the African and Eurasian plates meet, accumulated stress had been released all at once. There were no seismographs at the time, so the magnitude could only be estimated later. Scientists generally put the magnitude of the earthquake between eight point five and nine. In other words, this was one of the largest earthquakes ever recorded in European history. Witnesses described the shaking as arriving in several waves. First came a faint tremor. Some thought a heavy cart was rumbling down the street. Then the main blow struck. According to the records, the shaking lasted somewhere between three and a half and six minutes in total. For an earthquake, that is an extraordinarily long time. Houses first swayed back and forth, then their walls began to split apart and their roofs to cave in. In the city center, cracks opened in the ground, some of them as much as five meters wide. The cloud of dust that rose was so thick that witnesses said a sunny morning turned to twilight within minutes.

The greatest loss of life came in the churches. Stone vaults, domes and bell towers collapsed onto the crowds gathered for the holy day mass. Most of the city's great churches were either completely destroyed or badly damaged. The towers of Lisbon Cathedral came down. On the hill above, the roof of the church of the Carmo Convent fell in on the people inside. The church's pointed arches still stand today, like a skeleton open to the sky. Those who tried to flee were buried in the narrow streets as building fronts fell on top of them. The streets were so narrow that the rubble blocked them in an instant.

The Ribeira Palace was destroyed, along with its precious library and archives. The Tagus Opera House, opened only a few months earlier, was razed to the ground. The warehouses of the India House collapsed. Some accounts say the newly built marble quay sank into the water along with the people who had taken refuge on it. The details of this story, however, vary from source to source.

Most of the survivors had two thoughts at once: get away from the falling buildings and reach open ground. The widest open space in the city was the riverfront, Palace Square and the quays. Thousands ran toward the water, dragging their wounded, carrying their children in their arms, clutching whatever belongings they could grab. It looked safe. There were no walls to fall on them, only the river and the ships ahead.

Letters written afterward by English merchants, clergymen and diplomats who were in the city carry those minutes down to us today. Many of these letters describe a similar scene: crowds kneeling in prayer, begging for their sins to be forgiven, gathering around the priests. Many people believed the end of the world had come. A disaster that struck on a holy day morning, inside the churches, in the middle of mass, seemed like a sign in itself.

But the crowd gathered on the shore did not know that a far greater danger was about to arrive.

Some time after the earthquake, the people on the riverbank noticed something strange. The water was pulling back. The mouth of the Tagus River was draining fast, and the shoreline was slipping away. A sight no one had ever seen before was revealed: the riverbed itself, old shipwrecks sunk in the mud, cargo that had fallen into the water years before. Some people walked out onto the mud out of curiosity.

Then the sea came back.

About forty minutes after the earthquake, a giant wave from the ocean, a tsunami, entered the mouth of the Tagus and pushed upriver against the current toward the city. The waves came one after another, several times. In Lisbon, the water is estimated to have risen by about six meters. It swallowed the quays, the square and the streets near the shore. A large part of the crowd that had taken refuge on the waterfront lost their lives in these waves. Ships in the harbor smashed into one another; some capsized, others were driven aground. As the water drew back, it carried people, belongings and debris away with it.

The tsunami did not strike Lisbon alone. In the Algarve, on Portugal's southern coast, the waves are known to have climbed much higher. Coastal towns such as Lagos suffered heavy damage. In Spain, the coast of Cádiz was battered by the waves. On the far side of the Strait of Gibraltar, people also died in cities along Morocco's Atlantic coast. Hours later, the waves reached the coast of Cornwall in southwestern England, Ireland, and even the Caribbean on the other side of the ocean. In lakes in Sweden and Norway, and at Loch Lomond in Scotland, the water was recorded sloshing about for no apparent reason. Even in the far corners of Europe, people sensed that morning that something had happened, but they would only learn what it was from letters that arrived weeks later.

In Lisbon, meanwhile, the third face of the disaster had already begun: fire.

That morning, thousands of candles had been lit for the holy day in every church in the city. Hearths were burning in the homes. Candles and cooking fires buried under the collapsed buildings spread to toppled wooden beams, to curtains, to fabrics. Small fires broke out all across the city. Before long, they merged into a great firestorm. With the narrow streets choked with rubble, putting it out was impossible. The wind carried the flames from one neighborhood to the next. The fire burned for days, for six days by some accounts. Many of the buildings that had survived the earthquake were now lost to the flames. The books, paintings and maps left in the ruins of the Ribeira Palace burned. A large share of the documents from Portugal's Age of Discovery was lost forever. The Royal Hospital of All Saints in Rossio Square also went up in flames. Hundreds of patients who were unable to flee died in their beds. In the homes of wealthy merchants and nobles, gold, silver and jewels melted and mixed into the rubble.

Amid the chaos, another danger appeared. The city's prisons had collapsed, and the prisoners had escaped. Reports spread of burning houses and abandoned shops being looted. The survivors fled to the fields, the hills and the open ground outside the city. In the November cold, under the open sky, they waited, often with nothing but the clothes on their backs. Aftershocks continued for weeks, and each one set off a fresh wave of panic.

We do not know the exact death toll. Most of the records had burned. Some of the dead had been swept out to sea, and others lay buried beneath the ruins. Historians' estimates for Lisbon range from thirty thousand to fifty thousand. In a city of roughly two hundred thousand people, that means losing somewhere between a quarter and a sixth of the population. The dead in the rest of Portugal, in Spain and in Morocco must be added to that figure. Most of the city's buildings had been destroyed, burned or left unusable.

The royal family was safe in Belém. But the shock that José the First lived through was not only physical. After the disaster, the king became afraid of living within stone walls. For a long time, the court lived in tents and wooden pavilions. Eventually a wooden palace was built on the hill of Ajuda. Known among the people as the Royal Shack, it served as the king's palace for the rest of his life.

While the city burned and the king waited in his tent, someone had to take charge.

That man was Sebastião José de Carvalho e Melo. History knows him better by the title he received years later: the Marquis of Pombal. In seventeen fifty-five he was fifty-six years old and served as the king's foreign minister. He had earlier been a diplomat in London and in Vienna. He had seen Europe's styles of government, its trade and its new ideas up close. He was a harsh, determined and fearless administrator.

According to a story often told, the king, in despair after the disaster, asked what should be done. The answer was short and clear: bury the dead and feed the living. For centuries these words have been attributed to Pombal. Some historians, however, believe they were actually spoken by another member of the court, the Marquis of Alorna. Whoever said it, the sentence summed up the program of the weeks that followed, and it was Pombal who carried it out.

The first danger was disease. Thousands of bodies lay beneath the ruins and in the streets. Burying them all in the traditional way, in church graveyards, was impossible. It would have taken too long, and there was no room. With the Church's approval, Pombal turned to an unusual solution. The bodies were loaded onto barges, carried out past the mouth of the river, weighted down and released into the sea. For a devout society this was a grave decision, but it held off an epidemic.

The second danger was hunger and disorder. Pombal brought army units into the city and sealed off its exits. The aim was to stop able-bodied men from leaving and to put them to work clearing the rubble. He dealt very harshly with looting. Gallows were set up at various points around the city, and those caught looting were executed in public as a warning to others. This severity was debated even at the time. But order was soon restored. At the same time, ceilings were placed on food prices, and profiteering was banned. Grain was brought in from the surrounding regions, and provisions aboard ships arriving in the harbor were seized and distributed. Taxes on fishermen were temporarily lifted. Shelters were built for the homeless.

Help also came from across Europe. When the news reached London, the British Parliament set aside one hundred thousand pounds in aid for Portugal. Ships carried grain, flour, rice and tools. Spain sent help as well. Faced with the destruction of a capital, allies and rivals stood on the same side for a brief moment.

Pombal's most ambitious step, though, was the rebuilding. Many proposed moving the capital somewhere else, to Belém, for example. Pombal opposed this. Lisbon would be rebuilt on the same site, but as an entirely different city. For the task, he appointed the kingdom's chief engineer, Manuel da Maia. Maia, then nearly eighty years old, prepared several different options for the reconstruction. The plan chosen was the most radical one: the ruined center would be cleared completely, the old street layout erased, and a brand-new city drawn in its place. The architects Eugénio dos Santos and Carlos Mardel worked out the details of the plan. What emerged was an urban planning project with few parallels anywhere in Europe. The lower town, stretching from the riverfront to Rossio Square, was rebuilt on a grid of straight, broad streets crossing one another at right angles. Today the district still bears Pombal's name. Building heights were limited, and the facades were designed to the same dimensions, in a plain and sober style. Door and window frames, iron railings, even the roof beams were made in standard sizes and prepared in advance. It was a kind of early mass production, and it was this that set the pace of construction.

Inside the new buildings, an innovation lay hidden. Within the walls, builders set a skeleton of timber beams braced diagonally against one another. This system is known as the Pombaline cage. The idea was simple, but brilliant. Even if the stone walls cracked during an earthquake, the flexible timber frame would keep the building standing and give people time to escape. According to the accounts, the engineers had scale models of the system built, then marched companies of soldiers in step around them to create an artificial tremor and watched how the models behaved. This building technique is considered one of the first systematic examples of earthquake-resistant design. Tall firewalls were also added between buildings to stop fire from spreading.

Where the old Palace Square had stood, a new square rose. The palace was gone. In its place stood government offices, the customs house and the exchange. The square was renamed Commerce Square. Even the new name carried a message: the heart of Lisbon was no longer the royal palace, but trade. At the centre of the square stood an equestrian statue of King Joseph the First. The great triumphal arch linking the square to the main street of the lower town, however, was not completed until much later, in eighteen seventy-five.

During all this, Pombal took one more step, and it may have been his most far-sighted. He sent a detailed list of questions to every parish in the country, that is, to the local priests. The questions went like this: How many minutes did the earthquake last? How many aftershocks were felt? From which direction did it seem to come? Which buildings collapsed? How many people died? What changes were seen at sea, and how high did the water rise? Did the water level in the wells change? Did animals behave strangely before the earthquake? Did cracks open in the ground? The replies are preserved today in the National Archives of Portugal. Because this was one of the first attempts to document an earthquake across such a wide area with systematic questions, some historians of science now regard Pombal as one of the pioneers of modern seismology.

But rebuilding was not only a matter of stone and timber. A struggle had also begun over what the disaster meant.

In Lisbon, many clergymen preached that the earthquake was a punishment from God. The most influential of them was the Italian-born Jesuit priest Gabriel Malagrida. In a tract published in seventeen fifty-six, Malagrida wrote that the true cause of the earthquake was the city's sins, and that those seeking natural explanations were mistaken. He urged the people to turn to penance and retreat rather than work on rebuilding. This message clashed with Pombal's entire program. Pombal was seeking a natural explanation for the earthquake, and he opposed anything that might slow the pace of reconstruction. Malagrida was first removed from the court, then banished from the capital. Years later he was linked to an attempt on the king's life, tried by the Inquisition, and executed in seventeen sixty-one. This clash was part of Pombal's wider quarrel with the Jesuits. In seventeen fifty-nine, the Jesuit order was expelled from Portugal altogether.

Yet the real debate over the meaning of Lisbon took place far beyond Portugal's borders, in the intellectual world of Europe.

In the middle of the eighteenth century, a powerful optimism held sway in Europe's educated circles. The German philosopher Leibniz had argued that the world God created was the best of all possible worlds. By this way of thinking, the evils in the world were part of a greater good that we simply cannot see. The English poet Alexander Pope had put a similar idea into verse: the notion that whatever is, is right. In philosophy, this debate is called theodicy, the effort to defend God's goodness in the face of the evil in the world.

Lisbon shook this optimism to its core. Churches collapsing on the faithful at Mass on the morning of a holy day, some of the districts that housed the city's brothels left standing, innocent children dead. What greater good could all of this possibly be part of?

The French writer and thinker Voltaire was among those who asked this question most loudly. A few weeks after hearing the news, he wrote a long poem called Poem on the Lisbon Disaster, and published it in seventeen fifty-six. In it, he told the philosophers who claimed that all is well to come and look at the ruins of Lisbon. He asked what crime the dead had committed, and why Lisbon should be thought more sinful than London or Paris. Voltaire did not reject belief in God. What he rejected was the comfort of brushing suffering aside with a ready-made explanation. Jean-Jacques Rousseau gave the most striking reply to this poem. In the summer of seventeen fifty-six, he wrote Voltaire a long letter. In Rousseau's view, people bore part of the blame for how great the disaster had been. Nature had not piled thousands of houses, six and seven storeys high, on top of one another in that narrow space; people had done that. Had they lived in houses that were more spread out and lower, far fewer lives would have been lost. What's more, many had gone back into collapsing houses to save their belongings, their papers, their money, and had died there. Rousseau was moving the debate away from the justice of God and toward the choices people make for themselves. Today, disaster science holds that a catastrophe comes not only from a natural event, but also from how and where people live. Rousseau's letter is considered one of the earliest statements of this idea.

Voltaire gave his answer years later, in seventeen fifty-nine, in the form of a novel: Candide. Its young hero, Candide, travels the world with his tutor, Pangloss. Whatever befalls them, Pangloss keeps repeating that everything happens for the best in the best of all possible worlds. Their travels take them to Lisbon as well. They arrive in the city just as the earthquake strikes. Afterward, the authorities decide to burn a few people in a solemn ceremony to prevent further earthquakes. Pangloss is hanged, and Candide is flogged. Voltaire wrote this scene with dark humor. He was mocking two things at once: philosophical optimism, and the belief that disaster is divine punishment. Candide soon became one of the most widely read books in Europe, and the advice at the novel's end, that one must cultivate one's own garden, came to be read as a modest answer to catastrophe.

Another figure who joined the debate was Immanuel Kant, then in his early thirties and still an unknown teacher in Königsberg. In seventeen fifty-six, Kant published three short essays on earthquakes. In them, he looked for the cause of earthquakes not in divine punishment, but in natural processes beneath the ground. He proposed that vast underground caverns filled with hot gases existed below the surface, and that earthquakes arose from them. That explanation is now known to be wrong, but his approach mattered: an earthquake was a natural event that needed to be explained. A few years later, the English scientist John Michell proposed that earthquakes were waves travelling through the Earth's crust, and that their point of origin could be found by looking at the times the waves arrived. Pombal's questionnaire, Kant's essays and Michell's idea of waves were different steps, taken after the same disaster, all pointing in the same direction.

The Lisbon earthquake also left its mark on a child's memory. Johann Wolfgang von Goethe was six years old at the time and living in Frankfurt. Years later, in his memoir Poetry and Truth, he described how this news shattered the peace of his young mind for the first time. He remembered being unable to reconcile what he heard in the grown-ups' conversations, the idea of a wise and merciful creator, with the deaths of so many innocent people.

This is why some historians of ideas call Lisbon the first modern disaster. There are several reasons for this. First, thanks to printing presses, newspapers and networks of letters, the news had spread across all of Europe, creating a shared shock throughout the continent. Second, in the wake of the disaster, a state had responded not with prayer and penance, but with a planned program of relief, record-keeping and reconstruction. Third, and perhaps most important, the world of philosophy had begun to draw an ever sharper line between natural evil and moral evil. An earthquake was no longer seen as punishment for someone's sins, but as an event to be understood, measured and, where possible, guarded against.

Pombal's own fortunes, however, rose and fell. Over the next twenty years, he ruled Portugal almost single-handedly. The order he built is remembered both for its reforming side and for its very harsh one. In seventeen seventy-seven, when Joseph the First died and his daughter, Maria the First, took the throne, Pombal fell from favor and spent his remaining years in exile. But the city he built still stands.

Today, anyone walking through Lisbon's lower town is walking through the decisions made two hundred and seventy years ago. The straight streets, the plain, matching facades and the broad Commerce Square opening onto the river are the fabric of the city built after the disaster. Inside the walls of some of these buildings, the wooden frame of the Pombaline cage still stands. Engineers study this system as an important link in the history of earthquake-resistant construction. At the same time, there are warnings that some of these frames were weakened by renovations carried out in later periods. In other words, the lesson of seventeen fifty-five is still a live debate in Lisbon today.

The roofless church of the Carmo Convent on the hill, meanwhile, was deliberately never rebuilt. Its Gothic arches stand open to the sky, just as the earthquake left them, and today it houses an archaeology museum. Where the roof once fell on those who had come to Mass that feast-day morning, there is now only sky. The ocean has not forgotten either. The fault zone off Portugal's southwest coast is still active today, and scientists keep it under constant watch. The tsunami warning systems built for the northeastern Atlantic and the Mediterranean were shaped, in part, by the memory of seventeen fifty-five. In Lisbon's waterfront neighborhoods today, there are signs marking the tsunami evacuation routes. In school drills, students are taught that when the sea pulls back, they should run not toward the shore, but toward higher ground. What the crowd that took refuge in the Palace Square in seventeen fifty-five did not know is now the first rule taught to every child.

The Lisbon earthquake destroyed a city. It wiped out a library, a palace, an opera house, and tens of thousands of lives. But from among the ruins, three new questions emerged. The engineers asked: how can this be prevented? The scientists asked: why does this happen? The philosophers asked: does this have any meaning? Since that day, the first two questions have found better and better answers. The third, picking up where Voltaire, Rousseau and Kant left off, is still asked anew after every great catastrophe.
