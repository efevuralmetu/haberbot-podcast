A desert locust living alone is green and timid, and it shies away from others of its kind. But touch its hind legs for a few hours, and its color begins to change. It starts seeking out the crowd, and it becomes part of a swarm billions strong. What triggers this transformation is neither a gene nor a virus. The trigger is a single chemical, and that same chemical is found in our own brains.

For centuries, a farmer standing at the edge of his field believed he was looking at two different insects. One was greenish or sand-colored, shy, and solitary. It hid among the grasses and moved away whenever it spotted another of its kind. Farmers usually considered it harmless. The other seemed like a different creature altogether: dark, spotted with black, tinged with yellow or orange, always on the move. Thousands, even millions of them would gather until they darkened the sky. It was a catastrophe. Entomologists thought the same way the farmers did. These were two separate species, with separate names, and they sat in museum drawers under separate labels.

The man who overturned this picture was a Russian entomologist named Boris Uvarov. Uvarov was born in eighteen eighty-eight in the town of Uralsk, in what is now Kazakhstan. As a young man he worked on agricultural pests in the Caucasus and southern Russia. He knew locust outbreaks firsthand, from the middle of the field. After the revolution and the civil war, he settled in London in nineteen twenty and began working on the locust collections at the Imperial Bureau of Entomology there. Two species lay before him: the migratory locust, Locusta migratoria, and a calmer, more scattered species of the same genus, Locusta danica.

As Uvarov examined the specimens one by one, he noticed something unsettling. There was no sharp boundary between the two species. At one end stood the typical migratory form, at the other the typical calm form, and an unbroken series of intermediate forms filled the space between them. The shape of the ridge on the back of the thorax, the length of the wings relative to the legs, the color patterns: all of these shifted step by step from one form to the other. Field observations by his colleagues in Russia pointed in the same direction. In outbreak years the migratory form multiplied, and when the outbreak died down, the calm form remained in the region. It was as if one were turning into the other.

In a paper published in nineteen twenty-one, Uvarov reached a bold conclusion. These were not two species but two different states of the same species. He called these states "phases." He named the solitary form the solitarious phase and the swarming form the gregarious phase. In his view, what decided the matter was not genes but conditions. When locusts were sparse, they stayed solitarious. When they became crowded, they turned gregarious. This idea also explained why locust plagues erupted in certain years and then vanished for years at a time. Disaster did not fall from the sky. It built up slowly within a quiet population.

The idea was soon put to the test. In the early nineteen-twenties, the entomologist Faure, working in South Africa, reared the young of the local brown locust at different densities. Those raised in crowded containers took on the colors and behavior of the swarming form, while those raised one by one stayed solitarious. Now Uvarov's theory could be seen in the laboratory as well. Uvarov devoted the rest of his life to locusts. The Anti-Locust Research Centre in London, which he was instrumental in founding, remained for many years the hub of the world's knowledge about locusts.

Today this discovery stands as one of the most striking examples of what we call phenotypic plasticity. The same genetic code can, depending on the environment, give rise to two bodies and two temperaments that look nothing alike. The migratory locust has this duality, and so does the desert locust, which lives across a broad belt stretching from Africa to India. The desert locust became the species scientists studied most in the decades that followed. But one question remained open, a question Uvarov could not answer, and perhaps could never have answered with the tools of his time. What exactly did crowding do to a locust? How does an animal "know" that it is surrounded by many of its own kind, and what switch does that knowledge flip inside the body?

The answer emerged step by step nearly eighty years later, in laboratories in England. Researchers including Stephen Simpson at Oxford and Steve Rogers and Malcolm Burrows at Cambridge began by measuring how fast the transformation happens. The result was surprising. Physical changes, such as the shift in color and in body proportions, took weeks, and some were fully established only over several generations. Behavior, however, changed far more quickly. Put a solitarious desert locust into a crowded cage, and within just a few hours it became a different animal. The insect that had first fled from its own kind soon began to approach them, to move about more, and to behave at ease in the middle of the crowd.
So what was the locust actually sensing in a crowd? The researchers separated the senses one at a time. Showing a locust the sight and smell of other locusts together had some effect, but the strongest trigger turned out to come from somewhere else entirely: touch. In a study published in two thousand and one, Simpson and his team used a small paintbrush to stroke different parts of a locust's body at regular intervals. Touching its back, its belly or its antennae changed very little. But touching the thick upper part of the hind leg, the outer face of the thigh, pushed a solitarious locust toward swarming behavior. In the wild, this is the unavoidable consequence of living in a crowd. Locusts gathered around scarce patches of vegetation are constantly brushing against one another, their legs knocking together. Every neighbor that touches a leg sends the same message to the nervous system: you are not alone, there are many others around you.

The next step was to find out what this message changes in the brain and the nerve centers. An insect's nervous system is organized differently from ours. There is a brain in the head, and along the body there are clusters of nerve cells called ganglia. The ganglia that control the movement of the legs sit in the thorax. A team made up of Michael Anstey, Steve Rogers, Swidbert Ott, Malcolm Burrows and Stephen Simpson measured the chemicals in these thoracic ganglia in locusts exposed to crowding. The result, published in two thousand and nine, quickly caught the attention of the scientific world. In the first hours after swarming behavior began, the amount of serotonin in the thoracic ganglia rose roughly threefold.

Serotonin is an ancient signaling molecule. In the human brain it is linked to mood, sleep and appetite, and in many animals it performs a range of different jobs. In the locust, it turned out to play the key role. To show that the connection was no coincidence, the researchers tested it from both directions. Locusts given substances that blocked the effect of serotonin kept their solitarious behavior, even when they were exposed to crowds or to touches on the leg. Conversely, when solitarious locusts that had never seen a crowd were given serotonin, or substances that boosted its production, these animals began to behave like gregarious ones, even though they were alone. In other words, serotonin was not a by-product. It was the switch that started the transformation.

There is an important detail here. The rise in serotonin was temporary. It set the transformation in motion, but it did not stay high once swarming behavior had gone on for a long time. Other processes took over to make the gregarious phase permanent, and these processes reshaped the nervous system itself. According to measurements by Ott and Rogers in twenty ten, the brains of gregarious desert locusts were about thirty percent larger than those of solitarious ones, and the proportions of the brain regions relative to one another were different as well. Researchers are still interpreting what this difference means. One idea is that an animal living in a crowded and changeable environment, constantly on the move and dealing with a wider range of food, may need to process more information. But this is a well-supported explanation rather than a settled conclusion.

The change is also passed from one generation to the next. When a female locust living in crowded conditions lays her eggs in the soil, she covers them with a foamy secretion. Research has shown that the density the mother experienced is passed on to her offspring this way, and that the young of mothers who lived in crowds hatch with gregarious traits already in place. The chemical details of this transfer are still being investigated. The upshot is this: if a locust population stays crowded for several generations, each new generation is born more ready for the transformation. The young are marked with black and bright yellow or orange patterns, while the adults turn pink at first and then yellow once they mature.

These bright colors may serve a purpose too. Gregarious locusts do not shy away from eating toxic plants, and the substances they take in from those plants can make them unpalatable to some predators. Unlike their solitarious cousins, which try to stay hidden, these eye-catching colors may work as a warning: don't eat me. In short, a chain that begins with ordinary touches on a leg goes on to change behavior, the brain, color, diet and the next generation. Yet individually transformed locusts are not yet a swarm. The truly frightening moment comes when these individuals come together and begin to move as a single living thing.

Young locusts whose wings have not yet developed cannot fly, but they can walk. Nymphs that have entered the gregarious phase gather together to form marching crowds known as bands. These bands sometimes stretch for kilometers, advancing in the same direction for days and sweeping away the vegetation in their path. For a long time, no one knew how this order came about. There was no leader, and no one was pointing the way. And yet thousands of insects turned in the same direction and marched on.
In two thousand six, a team that included Jerome Buhl, Stephen Simpson and the physicist Iain Couzin took this question into the laboratory. They placed young locust nymphs in a ring-shaped arena and gradually increased their numbers. What they saw looked a lot like what physicists call a phase transition. When there were only a few locusts, each one wandered at random. As the density rose, brief moments of alignment began to appear, and groups would switch direction every so often. But above a certain threshold, which in this experiment came at around seventy-four locusts per square metre, the disorder suddenly vanished. Almost the entire crowd began marching in the same direction, for long stretches, without turning back. Just as water turns abruptly to ice at a certain temperature, the crowd's behaviour changed in a sudden leap rather than step by step.

So what was driving them to march? The answer showed that a locust swarm is no romantic act of solidarity. A two thousand eight study by Sepideh Bazazi and her colleagues revealed that the marching bands are driven by the fear of cannibalism. Migrating nymphs are especially hungry for protein and salt. And the nearest source of protein is the fellow locust marching just ahead. Locusts in the crowd try to bite the abdomen of the one in front, while the one in front moves forward to escape the attack from behind. The researchers found that when they reduced sensation in the abdomen, that is, the ability to sense a fellow locust approaching from behind, the insects became less inclined to move and were bitten more often. In locusts fed on protein and salt, on the other hand, the cannibalistic tendency declined. A marching band is, so to speak, an army fleeing from whatever is behind it. Each individual chases the one ahead while running from the one behind. And this forced march pushes the whole crowd forward.

That is why the swarm never stops. Marching is both a way to find food and a way to avoid being eaten. As the crowd grows, food runs short; as food runs short, hunger and cannibalism increase, and that in turn speeds up the march. When the nymphs reach adulthood and grow wings, the same drive is carried into the air. Solitary desert locusts usually fly alone and at night, but gregarious adults take off by day in vast swarms. Carried by the wind, they can cover more than a hundred kilometres a day, and sometimes more than a hundred and fifty.

From this point on, the numbers begin to strain human comprehension. According to the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations, a single square kilometre of swarm can hold between forty and eighty million locusts. Every adult locust eats roughly its own weight in fresh vegetation each day, about two grams. By a comparison the Food and Agriculture Organization often uses, a swarm covering one square kilometre eats as much food in a single day as about thirty-five thousand people. Real swarms, however, often cover hundreds of square kilometres. In major plague years, the desert locust can affect about twenty percent of the world's land surface, with the affected region stretching across more than sixty countries.

The endurance of these swarms is astonishing, too. In nineteen eighty-eight, desert locust swarms that took off from West Africa crossed the Atlantic Ocean and reached the Caribbean in about ten days. That was a journey of roughly five thousand kilometres. A more recent example is the upsurge that struck East Africa between twenty nineteen and twenty twenty-one. Unusual rainfall in Yemen and across the Arabian Peninsula first caused quiet populations to explode, and then set off the transformation. Kenya suffered its worst locust invasion in decades, and the swarms spread from Ethiopia and Somalia all the way to South Asia. The process Uvarov had described a hundred years earlier was working in exactly the same way, even in the age of satellite imagery and crop-spraying aircraft.

It is precisely here, in the logic of locust control, that the most lasting legacy of Uvarov's phase theory lies. Once a swarm has taken wing, catching up with it is very difficult and very expensive. But every swarm was once made up of solitary locusts, avoiding one another in some remote corner of the desert. That is why today the Food and Agriculture Organization keeps constant watch over rainfall, vegetation and locust density in the areas where locusts live, even when there is no plague. National field teams try to spot early the places where plants are turning green and locusts are starting to crowd together. The goal is to stop a population on the verge of transformation before bands and swarms can form. Alongside chemical pesticides, biological control agents made from certain species of fungi, which target only locusts, have also become part of this strategy. When early warning works, a disaster is averted without the farmer ever knowing. When it fails, swarms cross borders, as they did in twenty nineteen.
For science, though, the locust became a window onto a much broader question. That two entirely different personalities could emerge from the very same genes, that a brief surge in a single signaling molecule could transform an entire way of life, that thousands of independent individuals could flow in one direction without any leader: all of this drew not only biologists but also physicists and mathematicians to this insect. The models built to explain locust bands speak the same language as research that tries to understand many different forms of collective behavior, from schools of fish to flocks of birds, and even the movement of large human crowds. Here, we need to be careful. The fact that serotonin is also found in humans does not mean that human crowds behave like locusts. No such conclusion is supported by science. What they share are the mathematical tools that help us understand how large-scale order emerges from the simple rules followed by individuals.

Perhaps the most striking part of this story is how small the thing is that sets the disaster in motion. A few clumps of plants turning green in a desert after the rain. A few hundred locusts crowded around those plants, brushing against one another's hind legs. A surge of serotonin lasting a few hours in the nerve clusters of their thorax. Then animals that begin to draw closer together, bands that swell in number, young hoppers chasing one another, and finally a cloud that darkens the sky. This is what Uvarov saw when he looked at an insect we had taken for two separate species: the monster was not coming from somewhere else. It lay waiting inside the calm insect, and a single touch was enough to wake it.
