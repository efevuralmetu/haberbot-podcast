Picture a tent in the desert. The sun is going down, the sand is beginning to cool, and a single shadow appears on the horizon. Someone is walking. You don't know who they are. What tribe they come from, whether they're a friend or an enemy, a debtor or a fugitive — you have no idea. The only thing you know is this: if they spend the night out there, they will die. The water isn't theirs, it's yours. The shade isn't theirs, it's yours. Whether this man lives or dies comes down to a decision you will make in the next few minutes.

The oldest moral question in human history was born, I suspect, right there, at the mouth of that tent. It is a question that comes before even the prohibition on killing. Because not killing requires you to do nothing; taking in a stranger, on the other hand, requires you to share the little you have with someone you don't know. The first is holding your hand back. The second is opening it.

Desert societies answered this question not with custom, but with something close to law. Taking in a stranger wasn't courtesy, it was obligation. To refuse was shame, and in that part of the world, shame was a lethal currency. When Ibn Khaldun describes bedouin life in the Muqaddimah, he sees that this is not romantic but structural: with no state, no court, no police force, what holds people up is the bond of solidarity between them. Generosity is the visible face of that bond. The one who gives water today knows that tomorrow he will be the one going thirsty. Hospitality is insurance, invented in a world where insurance had not yet been invented. Today you, tomorrow me.

But if that were the whole story, hospitality would have had no business with the gods. And yet it did. In ancient Greece there was a separate epithet for the protector of strangers: Zeus Xenios, Zeus of guests. A man who mistreated a stranger was trampling not on a rule of etiquette, but on the god himself. In Homer's Odyssey, this is the hidden measure of the story. Every time the hero washes up on an island, the same test is set: will this door open, and if it does, how will it open?

In the land of the Phaeacians, it opens. The man has come out of the sea, caked in salt, naked, without name or reputation. They bathe him, they clothe him, they feed him. Only after all of that do they ask his name. Notice the order, because the whole point lies in that order: hospitality first, identity second. To take someone in before learning who they are is to sever hospitality from identity altogether.

The very same epic contains the exact inverse of this rule. The Cyclops Polyphemus tells the guests who arrive at his cave that he will give them a guest-gift, and announces that his gift will be to eat them last. For a Greek audience, this was more than a scene of monstrosity: it was a description of stepping outside of humanity. The one-eyed giant was strong, he had his cave, he had his flock, he needed nothing from anyone. And precisely for that reason, he was incapable of hospitality.

The same threshold is set, in an entirely different key, at Abraham's tent. As it is told in the Quran, guests he does not recognize come to him. He quietly slips away to his household, roasts a fattened calf and sets it before them. When the guests do not reach out for the food, a fear settles into him. The detail here is very fine: the man is not afraid of his guests, he is afraid that the guest-relation has broken down. A guest who will not sit at your table is, in the language of this tradition, a sign of threat. To share food is to declare that there is no longer enmity between you.

The Islamic tradition would later bind this attitude with a very clear sentence: whoever believes in God and the Last Day, let him honor his guest. Look at the structure of that sentence. Hospitality is established here not as a habit, a temperament, a personality trait, but as the direct consequence of faith. Failing to be hospitable becomes not rudeness, but inconsistency.

So three separate traditions, independently of one another, arrive at the same place: the stranger at the door is not an ordinary person. There is an extra weight to them. The Greek calls that weight the god himself, because he thinks the one knocking might be a deity in disguise. The Semitic tradition calls it an angel. The bedouin gives it no name at all, he simply knows.

So where does that weight come from? From prudence? From the calculation that tomorrow I too might be stranded on the road? Or is there something else beneath all of this, something no calculation explains, something that does not disappear even when the need is gone? Because we don't go thirsty anymore. We don't spend our nights on caravan routes. We have insurance, we have courts, we have hotels. The reciprocity equation was solved long ago. And yet, when there's a knock at the door, something still stirs inside us.

In the twentieth century, one thinker tried to name that stirring thing exactly. And in doing so, he took hospitality out of the realm of custom and made it the most fundamental question in philosophy.

Emmanuel Levinas was born in nineteen oh six in Kaunas, a city that lies today within the borders of Lithuania. He studied philosophy in France, attended the lectures of Husserl and Heidegger in Germany, and became a French citizen. In nineteen thirty-nine he was called up for military service; in nineteen forty he was taken prisoner, and he spent the rest of the war in a prison camp in Germany, working in forestry. Because he wore a French uniform, he was classed as a prisoner of war, and he survived. Of the family he had left behind in Lithuania, no one remained. His mother, his father and his two brothers were murdered. His wife and his young daughter survived, hidden in a monastery in France.

Years after those years in the camp, in a short piece of writing, he recalls a strange memory. Every day the team of prisoners was marched out to work under guard, and the people they passed along the way would not look at them. Not looking is the most refined form of violence; they were not being treated as human beings, they were seen as talking animals. Then a stray dog appears around the edge of the camp. For a few weeks he greets them every morning, wagging his tail, barking with delight. Levinas names him Bobby and says that this dog was the only being who saw them: the only being who never doubted for a moment that the prisoners were human.

The whole of Levinas's philosophy is, in a sense, an accounting of how that kind of look can go missing among human beings. In his great book of nineteen sixty-one, Totality and Infinity, he argues that philosophy has been starting from the wrong question for centuries. Western thought has always asked about being: what is it, what is it made of, how can it be known. Levinas says this is not the first question. The first question is this: who is the person in front of me, and what do I owe them? Ethics is not a branch of philosophy; it is where philosophy begins.

And he gives a name to the place where that beginning happens: the face.

For Levinas, the face is not an image. It is not the length of a nose, the colour of an eye, the shape of a jaw. It is certainly not the photograph on an identity card. The face is the fact that a person will never fit inside all your ideas about them. You define them, you classify them, you put them in a box, and they overflow the box. The face is what overflows.

And according to Levinas, the face speaks. Without uttering a single word, it says something. What it says is this: you shall not kill me. You shall not turn me into an instrument. This is not a plea, it is a command; and yet there is no power standing behind it. The strength of the face is precisely its powerlessness. The person before you is utterly defenceless, and what binds you is precisely that defencelessness.

To see how strange an idea this is, you need only look at everyday life. You are walking down a crowded street. Someone is sitting at the edge of the pavement with a cup in their hand. What do you do? You do what most of us do: you don't look. You look at your phone, you look at a shop window, you look across the road. Where does that reflex come from? Not from stinginess. The money in your pocket isn't really the point. You don't look, because the moment you do, something begins. Meeting someone's eyes opens a contract, and you have no idea what that contract is going to cost you. Levinas's entire claim is hidden in that small, evasive glance: to see the face is already to bring the debt into being. Which is why we choose not to see.

Levinas insists on one particular feature of this debt: it is not equal. It is not reciprocal. I owe the other person more than they owe me, and this asymmetry cannot be corrected. It is nothing like an exchange. It is not a matter of you give, and then I'll give. Because the moment I begin calculating what will come back to me, the other stops being a face and turns into a line in a ledger.

It is worth remembering who the man saying this was. A man whose family had been slaughtered. His second great book, published in nineteen seventy-four, he dedicated to the memory of the six million people murdered by the National Socialists, and to all the other human beings who were victims of the same hatred, the same hatred of the foreigner. The last part of that dedication matters enormously: all the others. Even while counting his own dead, he keeps taking in those who fall outside the count.

Because what Levinas saw was this: genocide begins with the erasure of the face. You turn people into a category, you count what you have turned into a category, you administer what you have counted, and you eliminate what you administer. The first link in that whole chain is forged by a gesture that looks perfectly innocent: not looking.

Up to this point everything seems clear. But it is exactly here that the most uncomfortable question in philosophy comes into play. Granted: the face binds me. But how far? All the way? My house, my bread, my child's room as well? Am I not entitled to stop somewhere? And if I do stop, what is the name of the place where I stopped?

Tradition had a very practical answer to that question: three days.

The wording in the hadith sources is unambiguous. The feast lasts three days; anything beyond that is charity on the part of the host. In other words, hospitality is an obligation, but it has a limit. After three days the hosting may continue, yet it ceases to be a debt and becomes a gift. That number did not stay confined to books. The caravanserais of Anatolia were built to the very same measure. The arrangement described in the endowment charters of the Seljuk period runs like this: a traveller arrives, and for three days both he and his animal are lodged free of charge; if he falls ill he is cared for; if his shoes are torn they are mended; and for none of this does anyone need to ask about his religion, his language, or where he has come from. A Muslim merchant and an Armenian merchant sleep in the same courtyard with the same right. Then the third morning comes, and the road goes on.

One has to admit this is an ingenious solution. A boundless obligation is converted into a bearable one. But it is also an unsettling solution. Because it implies something: if you are still there on the fourth day, you are no longer a guest. So what are you?

In a short treatise called Perpetual Peace, written in seventeen ninety-five, Immanuel Kant tried to draw precisely this line. Setting out the conditions for a lasting peace among states, he devoted the third and final article to hospitality. What he said was this: every human being has the right not to be treated as an enemy on foreign soil where he sets foot. Notice that this is not an appeal to charity. Kant says right, explicitly. And he grounds that right in a reason: the earth is a sphere. We cannot move away from one another forever. On a round planet, sooner or later everyone comes up against everyone else's border. Common ownership of the land is not a sentiment but a geometrical necessity.

Yet immediately afterwards Kant makes the sharp distinction we are still arguing about today. This is a right of visit, not a right of residence. The one who arrives cannot demand to live there simply because he has arrived. The host country may turn him away. On a single condition: that turning him away will not lead to his destruction.

It also matters that Kant says whom he had in mind while writing these lines. He was thinking of the European states of his own age. What countries that passed for civilized were doing in the Americas, in Africa, in Asia, he described as the exact opposite of hospitality. They had not visited; they had conquered. So even at the moment of its birth, the principle of hospitality faced two ways: while it protected the one knocking at the door, it also passed judgment on the powerful one who broke the door down and walked in.

Two centuries later Jacques Derrida went after this orderly boundary Kant had drawn, and found a crack in it. In his lectures on hospitality, he showed that the word itself carries a civil war inside it. In Latin, the word for guest and the word for enemy stem from the same root. The man at the door is at once the one to be welcomed and the one who might be a threat. Even in our own language this ambivalence lies hidden: the word foreigner names both the one who comes from far away and the one who does not belong inside.

The impasse Derrida saw is this. True, pure hospitality would have to be unconditional. It would mean saying yes to whoever comes, without asking their name, without demanding their papers, without calculating how long they will stay. The instant you ask a single question, hospitality ends and selection begins. But this unconditional yes is at the same time impossible. Because a house open to everyone forever is no longer a house. And once it is not a house, there is no place left in which to welcome anyone. So the very thing that makes hospitality possible is the thing that ruins it: the door. Without a door you cannot invite anyone in; with a door you can never open fully.

It is very easy to draw two wrong conclusions from this. The first: since pure hospitality is impossible, the whole thing is empty. The second: since we are obliged to set conditions, every condition we set is legitimate. Neither of these is what Derrida said. What he said is this: our conditional rules must be perpetually disturbed by that impossible unconditionality. The three-day rule is a good rule, but the state of the person left outside the door on the evening of the third day must keep putting that rule back on trial. Law is necessary; a law satisfied with itself is dangerous.

And then there is the reversal Derrida was fond of. The one who opens the door, in the moment of opening it, stops being free in his own house. Once the guest arrives, you can no longer decide alone when you will sleep, what you will eat, which room you will use. The host becomes a kind of hostage to his guest. To open is to hand over part of your control. And it is this handing over, not the expense, that makes opening the door so hard.

And today that handing over is being debated on a scale never seen in history. Because there is no longer a single traveller at the door.

That is one out of every sixty-seven people on the planet. Turkey stood squarely at the centre of this picture. For more than a decade this was the country hosting more refugees than any other in the world.

Something very strange happens when we say those numbers out loud, and that strangeness is what this episode is really about. As the figures grow, the face disappears. Three million is not a face. One hundred twenty-two million certainly isn't. The binding claim Levinas spoke of was born in the gaze of a single human being; politics, meanwhile, has no choice but to work in millions. And this is precisely where the knot of the modern problem of hospitality lies: the language of ethics is singular, the language of governance is plural. The two struggle to translate each other.

And then a screen is added to all this. In Homer's world, the stranger could only arrive on foot, and when he arrived, he stood before you. Today the stranger appears on your phone over breakfast. You see the face of a child pulled from the rubble before you've even decided what you'll have for dinner. This is an entirely new condition in human history. And it raises this question: is that thing on the screen a face, or is it an image?

If we stay faithful to Levinas's distinction, the answer is uncomfortable. A face is something that can answer you, that can call you back, that is in the same room as you. The one on the screen can ask nothing of you, because a flick of your finger makes it vanish. You swipe, and it's gone. The power of the face lay in its defenselessness; the screen shows that defenselessness but cannot deliver its command. And so we learn something odd: seeing hundreds of instances of suffering every day does not make a person more sensitive. On the contrary, it produces a mind that grows accustomed to being unable to do anything about what it sees. This habit has a name — it's called compassion fatigue. And it isn't heartlessness; it's an overload of capacity.

Then we come to the one nearest to us. The hardest test of hospitality, contrary to what people assume, isn't given at the border but on the landing outside your door. In the Islamic tradition there is a striking saying passed down about the neighbour: Gabriel counselled me so often concerning the neighbour that I thought he would make him an heir. To be an heir means you are bound by blood. In other words, that person you don't know on the other side of the wall is drawn close to family — not in law, perhaps, but in ethics.

Now ask yourself honestly: do you know the name of the neighbour who steps onto your floor? Do you know how many people live with them? Did you hear that they were in hospital last month? In a country that has taken in millions of people, it is entirely ordinary to nod to someone in the same building for years and never once meet them. This isn't a contradiction; it's really two ends of the same matter. Because opening the door is not a feeling, it's a habit. And habit is formed at the nearest threshold, or it isn't formed at all.

Looking back today, we can see that all those old traditions shared a single insight: they did not leave hospitality to the good intentions of individuals. They institutionalised it. They tied the caravanserai to an endowment, the public kitchen to a revenue stream, the three days to a rule. Because good intentions grow tired; rules do not. What falls to us is to build the modern equivalent of this: to anchor the welcoming of others not to a mood, but to a system that works.

But a system alone is not enough, because the real decision always remains singular. In Konya, at the entrance to Rumi's tomb, there are famous lines hanging: whatever you are, come, come again. Most scholars say these lines belong not to Rumi but to Abu Sa'id, who lived before him. Who wrote the words may be disputed, but what they say is clear: the invitation is extended before anyone asks what the newcomer is. Identity comes afterwards — just as it did with that stranger washed and fed on the seashore.

Let's return to the question we asked at the beginning of this episode, and alter it slightly. To the question of how far you keep your door open, there is no answer in the form of a number. Three days is an answer; a right of visit is an answer; never opening the door at all is also an answer. The real issue is not how far that distance extends, but which direction you're facing. Do you open the door after you've learned who has come, or do you learn after you've opened it? Which is your order?

Because a door that opens according to identity is not a door, it's a checkpoint. And a door left open all the way is not a door either, it's a hole in the wall. A door is a door because it can be opened and closed; and precisely for that reason, it asks you to decide again each time. Out there in the desert, the shadow on the horizon is still walking. You are still at the door. And who the water in your hand belongs to still depends on the answer you will give.
