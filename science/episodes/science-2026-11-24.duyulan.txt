We've all heard it, and most of us have done it.  And yet for 70 years, scientists couldn't say for certain what exactly bursts inside  the joint to make that little crack, or whether anything bursts at all.  The answer was found by slowly pulling a finger inside an MRI scanner, and it overturned the  explanation everyone thought they already knew.  Interlace your fingers, turn your palms outward, and push.  In the quiet of the room comes that familiar sound, dry and sharp, like a small twig snapping.  Perhaps you've already done it today without noticing.  Before a meeting began, before an exam paper was set in front of you, at the end of a long  hour at the computer.  A considerable share of the world's population has this habit.  Some do it to relax, some when they're tense, and some simply without realizing they're  doing it.  But there's a question worth stopping to ask, where exactly does that sound come from?  No bone is breaking, we know that much.  No muscle is tearing.  The finger is the same one second later as it was one second before.  And yet something happens so fast and so forcefully that the person next to you might turn their  head to look.  The strange part is that the source of such an ordinary, everyday sound divided scientists  for nearly 70 years.  First, let's set the scene.  Take one of the joints in your finger, say the one that sits like a knob on the back  of your hand and connects the finger to the palm.  It is really a meeting point where the ends of two bones face each other.  These bone ends are covered in cartilage, so they never grind directly against one another.  Around them is a sealed pouch called the joint capsule.  Inside, the capsule is filled with a slippery fluid, much like egg white.  This is called joint fluid, or in medical terms, synovial fluid.  Its job is to lubricate the joint, nourish the cartilage, and keep movement smooth.  One property of this fluid shapes the entire story, it carries dissolved gases.  Think of a sealed bottle of soda, where carbon dioxide sits mixed into the liquid.  You don't see bubbles in the bottle, because pressure holds the gas within the liquid.  The moment you open the cap, the pressure drops, and the gas reveals itself as bubbles.  Now picture the joint.  When you pull or bend your finger, the two bone ends are forced apart.  But because the capsule is sealed, no fluid can flow in from outside.  The volume increases while the amount of fluid inside stays the same.  The result is that the pressure inside the capsule drops rapidly.  At a certain threshold, the pressure falls so low that the fluid can no longer hold together,  and a cavity opens up within it.  Physicists call this cavitation.  The same phenomenon appears behind ship propellers, which push water so quickly that vapor-filled  cavities form in their wake.  The first to show that this cavity really forms inside the joint were two researchers  working in Manchester, England, in 1947 J. B. Rosten and R. Wheeler Haynes.  They built an apparatus that gradually pulled on volunteers' fingers, taking X-rays at every  stage.  As the pulling force increased, the joint resisted for a while.  Then came the sound, and at the same instant the two bone ends suddenly sprang apart.  When the X-ray taken after the sound, a dark area appeared inside the joint where none  had been before.  It was a gas-filled cavity.  X-rays pass with difficulty through dense tissue such as bone, which is why bones show  up light on the film.  Gas on the other hand, barely stops the rays at all, and leaves a dark patch.  The patch Rosten and Haynes saw was the shadow of a bubble born in an instant inside the  joint.  To them, the cause of the sound was clear, the sudden opening of the cavity, the rapid  separation of the joint surfaces, and that separation setting the surrounding tissues  vibrating.  In short, the sound was the moment of the bubble's birth.  This explanation seemed sensible.  But more than 20 years later, another team would look at the very same X-ray images and  reach an entirely different conclusion.  And the dispute between these two views could only be settled by a technology that made  it possible to watch the inside of the joint in real time.  In 1971, an engineering team at the University of Leeds in England took up the question again.  Anthony Unsworth, Duncan Dowson and Werner Wright approached the joint not as a biology  problem but as a mechanical system.  This time, they added sound recording and force measurement to their finger-pulling  apparatus.  They studied the waveform of the sound and estimated the composition of the gas that  collected in the joint.  According to their findings, this gas was largely carbon dioxide.  The Leeds team offered a different interpretation from their predecessors in Manchester.  In their view, the sound came not from the bubble's birth, but from its collapse.  Their scenario went like this.  The joint is pulled, the pressure drops, and a bubble forms in the fluid.  Then, as the joint capsule stretches and flexes, the pressure begins to rebalance and the bubble  collapses in on itself.  This collapse creates a shock wave, and the crack we hear is actually that wave.  This idea rested on a well-known phenomenon in the physics of cavitation.  The bubbles that form behind a ship's propeller produce shock waves so powerful when they  collapse that, over time, they can wear pits into solid metal.  The Leeds team believed something similar was happening inside the joint.  Their interpretation proved so convincing that in the decades that followed it made  its way into the textbooks.  The notion that a knuckle crack was a bubble bursting came to be accepted almost without  question, both in medical training and among the general public.  Another of the Leeds team's observations still holds true today.  Once you have cracked a joint, you cannot crack it again right away.  You have to wait a while, and the team measured that interval at roughly 20 minutes.  You may have noticed this yourself, after cracking a finger once, no matter how hard  you try, the second pop simply will not come.  During this waiting period, it was thought, the gas had to dissolve back into the fluid.  In other words, the joint was waiting for the cap to be screwed back on the soda bottle,  and for the bottle to settle.  But one knot remained untied.  If the sound was the collapse of a bubble, and the bubble had collapsed and vanished,  then what was the gas-filled space that Rosten and Haynes had seen on their X-rays after  the sound?  According to the Leeds team, it might be smaller bubbles left behind once the large one had  collapsed.  Both explanations fit the data in part, and neither could definitively rule out the other.  The problem was this, an X-ray gives you only snapshots.  There was no way to see what was happening inside the joint at the exact split second  of the sound, or precisely when the bubble appeared or disappeared.  Untying that knot would have to wait until 2015.  A team led by Greg Kouchuk, a physiotherapist and researcher at the University of Alberta  in Canada, decided to film the inside of the joint at the very moment the sound was  produced.  The tool they used was magnetic resonance imaging, or MRI.  But this was no ordinary MRI scan, they used a dynamic imaging method capable of capturing  several frames per second.  About three frames per second may seem painfully slow by today's video standards, but for watching  the inside of a joint in real time, it was a first.  The hardest part of the experiment was finding the right volunteer.  Not everyone can crack their knuckles whenever they please.  The team worked with someone who could crack his joints almost on demand, the chiropractor  Jerome Fryer, one of the study's co-authors.  Fryer's fingers were placed, one at a time, inside a flexible tube.  The tube was attached to a cable that ran out of the MRI machine.  The researchers pulled the cable slowly, with steady force, and the finger was stretched  inside the MRI, right before the camera's eye.  The images reopened a debate that had lasted for decades.  As the pulling force increased, the two ends of the joint stayed stuck together for a time.  Then came the sound, and at that very instant a dark cavity appeared inside the joint.  This cavity did not vanish after the sound.  On the contrary, it persisted.  In other words, the sound coincided not with the bubble's disappearance, but with its appearance.  Kachuk and his team explained this through a process called tribonucleation.  It may sound complicated, but the idea is simple.  Imagine pressing two wet panes of glass together and then trying to pull them apart.  The thin layer of liquid between them holds the panes together with surprising strength.  They resist up to a certain force, and then, all at once, they separate.  At that moment of separation, a cavity rapidly opens in the liquid between them.  Joint surfaces behave the same way.  The adhesion created by the slippery fluid builds up tension as the finger is pulled,  until it is suddenly released.  A cavity is born, and the sound is born along with it.  This result seemed to support the interpretation put forward by the first team, in Manchester,  in 1947.  But the story did not end there either.  In 2018, Vineeth Chandran Suja of Stanford University and Abdul Barakat of the École  Polytechnique in France approached the problem with pen and paper, that is with mathematics.  They built equations that took into account the geometry of the joint, the properties  of the fluid, and the changes in pressure.  Then they checked whether the sound these equations predicted matched real recordings  of knuckle cracking.  Their conclusion surprised both camps.  According to their model, the sound might come from the partial collapse of the bubble.  That is, the bubble forms, part of it collapses immediately afterward, and that collapse produces  the sound, while the rest of the bubble remains in the joint.  This was a proposed compromise, one that could explain within a single framework both why  the cavity remained visible on the MRI after the sound and the Leeds team's idea of collapse.  Here is where things stand today.  Scientists agree that the sound is linked to the sudden formation of a cavity in the  joint fluid.  But whether the moment that produces the sound is the opening of that cavity itself, or the  collapse of part of it immediately afterward, is still debated.  Settling the question for certain will require imaging far faster than anything MRI can capture,  because the entire event unfolds within a few milliseconds.  In other words, the final word on this most ordinary of sounds has yet to be spoken.  So let's set aside the source of the sound, and turn to the question on everyone's mind.  Perhaps, as a child, an older relative told you something like this, stop cracking your  knuckles, or your joints will wear out when you grow up, and you'll end up with arthritis.  That warning has been handed down from generation to generation, and many people still believe  it.  So what does science have to say?  One of the most unusual answers to this question came from a California physician named Donald  Unger.  Unger recalled that as a child, his mother, his aunts and other elders gave him exactly  this warning.  But he wondered whether they were right, and he turned his own body into an experiment.  For more than 60 years, at least twice a day, he cracked the knuckles of his left hand only.  His right hand he almost never cracked.  The right hand became the experiment's control group.  By Unger's own count, the knuckles of his left hand had been cracked at least 36,000  times over that period.  When he compared his two hands years later, he found no sign of arthritis in either one,  that is no joint inflammation and no joint degeneration.  There was no noticeable difference between them.  He shared this result in 1998, in a short letter to a rheumatology journal.  In it he noted that this one-person experiment, at least in his own case, did not support  his elders' warning.  Then, in 2009, this stubborn and witty piece of work earned him the IG Nobel Prize in Medicine,  an award that honors research that first makes people laugh, and then makes them think.  Unger's experiment is a charming story, but its scientific limitations are obvious.  A single person, a single hand, a single observer.  Perhaps Unger was genetically resistant to arthritis, perhaps in someone else the result  would have been different.  That is why the real weight of evidence lies in studies of larger groups.  In a study published in 1990, Jorge Castellanos and David Axelrod examined 300 people.  They found no difference in the rate of arthritis between those who cracked their knuckles and  those who didn't.  They did however, report that habitual knuckle crackers had somewhat more hand swelling and  reduced grip strength.  Interesting as this finding was, it was not consistently replicated in later studies,  and it remained unclear whether the relationship was one of cause and effect.  People whose hands are already tense or tired, for example, might simply crack their knuckles  more often to find relief.  Then, in 2011, Kevin deWeber and his colleagues studied 215 people who had had hand x-rays  taken.  They asked the participants how often, and for how many years, they had cracked their  knuckles, and then looked at the x-rays for signs of degeneration in the hand joints.  Once again, the result was the same.  No meaningful link was found between knuckle cracking and degeneration of the hand joints.  Even among people who had cracked their knuckles a great many times over the years, there was  no increase in risk.  There is also a sensible explanation behind this consistent picture.  Joint degeneration, known in medical terms as osteoarthritis, is a disease that develops  as the cartilage in a joint wears down over the years, and as the joint responds to that  wear.  It is associated with factors such as age, genetic predisposition, excess weight, old  injuries and prolonged heavy loading of the joint.  When you crack your knuckles though, the bones don't strike each other, and the cartilage  isn't crushed.  What happens is a change in pressure within the joint fluid, followed by the formation  of a cavity.  That is not the kind of mechanical stress that wears away cartilage.  But this doesn't mean that knuckle cracking is completely insignificant under all circumstances.  If you feel pain when cracking a joint, or if the joint swells or its movement becomes  restricted, the issue may not be the sound itself, but some other problem that the sound  accompanies.  Such as a ligament injury or damage inside the joint.  In that case, what deserves attention is not the habit itself, but the cause of the pain.  Paints cracking sounds, whether they happen on their own or on purpose, are considered  harmless according to the current evidence.  Then there is the question of why cracking feels so relieving.  Many people say that after cracking, they feel a loosening, a lightness in the joint.  The exact mechanism behind this is not fully understood.  One possibility is that when the joint capsule is stretched, nearby nerve endings are stimulated,  creating a brief sense of relaxation.  Another is that the joint's range of motion increases slightly for a while after cracking.  The waiting period of roughly 20 minutes measured by the LEADS team suggests that this loosening  may also last for some time.  These interpretations, however, are not yet settled, and researchers present them only  as possibilities.  Perhaps the most valuable thing the story of knuckle cracking leaves us with is not  so much the source of the sound as a lesson in how science works.  An explanation that one generation wrote into textbooks as established fact can be questioned  anew once a better tool comes along.  From the X-ray films in Manchester to the sound recordings in LEADS, from the MRI images  in Alberta to the equations at Stanford, each step sharpened the question left behind by  the one before, but never fully closed it.  Likewise, a warning handed down from generation to generation revealed its true worth only  when someone took it seriously and put it to the test.  Donald Unger's quiet comparison between his left hand and his right, and the studies involving  hundreds of people that followed, remind us that our intuitions are not always reliable  guides.  A sound that seems harmful can turn out to be harmless, while a phenomenon that sounds  simple can grow into a scientific debate lasting more than half a century.  And now, the next time you interlace your fingers and push, you'll know what lies behind  that brief sound.  The slippery fluid inside the joint gives up its resistance for just an instant.  The pressure drops, a cavity is born, and the echo of that birth, and perhaps of a partial  collapse that follows right after, reaches your ear.  Lasting mere milliseconds, this event is a tiny physics experiment that, even today,  has not been fully solved, and it repeats itself every day in millions of hands.  We've reached the end of today's episode of neuronballom.  Until we meet again in a new story.
