In November of eighteen twelve, more than a hundred thousand people stood waiting on the bank of a river. Ahead of them ran a current carrying blocks of ice. Behind them and on both flanks were three separate Russian armies. The fate of an empire now rested in the hands of a few hundred men who waded chest-deep into the freezing water, and most of those men would never be warm again.

In mid-September of eighteen twelve, Moscow was burning. The first smoke rose only a few hours after Napoleon's soldiers entered the city, and within a few days whole districts of wooden houses had been reduced to ash. Who started the fire is still debated today, but most historians believe that the city's governor, Rostopchin, personally arranged for arsonists and for the removal of the fire-fighting equipment, so that everything left behind for the French would be destroyed. From the windows of the Kremlin, Napoleon watched the flames paint the sky red. He had captured the heart of Russia, but that heart was no longer beating.

The army that had crossed the Neman River at the start of that summer, in June, was one of the largest Europe had ever seen. Counting its reserves, this host of nearly six hundred thousand was called the Grande Armée, but it was not made up of Frenchmen alone. In its ranks marched Poles, Germans, Italians, Dutchmen, Swiss, Croats and Portuguese. All the peoples of an empire had marched east at the will of a single man. By the time they reached Moscow, their numbers had already melted away to a little over a hundred thousand. Disease, heat, hunger and the bloody pitched battle at Borodino had exacted a price for every kilometer of the road.

Believing that Tsar Alexander would sue for peace, Napoleon waited in Moscow for five weeks. He sent letters, and no answer came. At last, on the nineteenth of October, the army marched out of the burned city. The wagons were laden with plunder: gold-embroidered fabrics, church treasures, furs, paintings, candlesticks. The soldiers' knapsacks held more silver than bread. Before long, these burdens would be abandoned one by one at the roadside, in the snow.

Napoleon first tried to head south, into lands that had not been plundered. But after clashing with Russian forces at Maloyaroslavets on the twenty-fourth of October, he was forced to change course, and the army began to retreat along the very road it had come by in early summer: the old Smolensk road, already stripped bare, the villages around it burned. That decision sealed the fate of the retreat. There was no food along the way. The horses were the first to die; the soldiers butchered them and roasted the meat over their fires, sometimes fighting over an animal that had not yet gone cold. As they passed Borodino, they walked among thousands of dead who had been left unburied two months before.

In the first days of November, the snow began. From then on, the cold joined forces with hunger and gnawed at the army from within. Each morning, around the campfires, men were found who had frozen to death in the night. Discipline collapsed. Every day, more soldiers threw away their weapons, abandoned their units and struck out on their own. This leaderless mass became a human flood flowing behind and alongside the regular troops. Among them were the wounded, the sick, and the merchants, cooks, women and children who followed the army. The Cossacks constantly tore away at the edges of this flood, taking prisoner or killing whoever fell behind.

When the army reached Smolensk, the great supply depots it had been counting on had already been emptied by the looting of those who arrived first. Then, around Krasnoi, in the middle of November, came days of fighting with Russian forces. Marshal Ney fell so far behind with his rearguard that for a time he was believed dead; in the end, with only a handful of men, he crossed the icy Dnieper by night and rejoined the army. The number of regular soldiers still bearing arms at Napoleon's side had now dwindled to a few tens of thousands. But ahead of the army lay a river that sat on the map as a thin blue line, one that hardly anyone had given much thought to: the Berezina.

The Berezina flows from north to south through what is today Belarus, before joining the Dnieper. On its own, it is not considered a great river; at its crossing points it is only about one hundred meters wide. The real obstacle is the marshland that covers both its banks. Reaching the river, and getting away from the far bank, was possible only along long raised causeways built from tree trunks. The most sensible route for Napoleon's retreat westward, toward Vilnius, ran across the long wooden bridge at the town of Borisov. That bridge was the gateway to salvation for the entire army.

What Napoleon did not know, or could not prevent even if he did, was that the Russians were closing in from three directions to shut that gateway. From the east, following on the army's heels, came Marshal Kutuzov's main army. The old commander preferred to let hunger and cold wear down his enemy rather than risk a great pitched battle, and he kept his distance as he pursued. From the north, General Wittgenstein's corps was marching down. And from the south, from a quarter no one had expected, came the Army of the Danube under Admiral Pavel Chichagov.
There was a reason this army was here. Until the spring of that year, Russia had been at war with the Ottoman Empire. When the Treaty of Bucharest, signed in May of eighteen twelve, brought that war to an end, the Russian forces along the Danube were freed up and sent north. Chichagov, a naval man, was considered inexperienced in land campaigns, but the plan guiding him was clear: link up with Wittgenstein at the Berezina and trap Napoleon between the river and Kutuzov.

On the sixteenth of November, Chichagov took Minsk. That alone was a heavy blow to Napoleon, because Minsk held vast supply depots stockpiled for the retreating army, and every one of them fell into Russian hands. The admiral then turned toward Borisov. The western end of the bridge was held by a Polish division under General Dąbrowski. On the twenty-first of November, there was a fierce clash. The Poles suffered heavy losses and fell back. The Russians took the bridgehead and the town.

Napoleon learned the news on the road, as he marched with his army. He immediately sent Marshal Oudinot's corps to Borisov. On the twenty-third of November, Oudinot fell upon Chichagov's advance guard and drove them out of the town and across to the far bank of the river. But as the Russians withdrew, they set fire to the bridge behind them. The wooden bridge burned, along with the sections that stretched across the marshland. The town was back in French hands, but it no longer meant anything. On the opposite bank, Chichagov's army stood dug in and waiting.

Now picture Napoleon's situation on a map. Behind him, Kutuzov, a few days' march away. To the north, Wittgenstein, poised to fall on his right flank at any moment. Ahead of him, a river whose bridge had burned, and beyond it, a Russian army. With him, perhaps forty to fifty thousand men still able to carry arms, and trailing behind them perhaps as many again who were unarmed, starving, and sick. Military history offers few pictures of encirclement as stark as this one. At Russian headquarters, the capture of the French emperor was now being discussed as merely a matter of time.

And nature, too, was playing an unexpected trick. In the last week of November, the weather briefly turned milder, and the bitter cold gave way to a thaw. This kept the river from freezing. Had the Berezina frozen over in the hard frosts of a few weeks earlier, the army might simply have marched across it. But now the water was flowing, carrying great chunks of ice downstream. The ice was not solid enough to walk on, and the water was not warm enough to swim. The army needed a bridge. And only a few days earlier, they had burned the very materials to build one with their own hands.

Let us go back a few days, to the town of Orsha. By the time Napoleon arrived there, around the twentieth of November, the heavy loads dragging along behind the army had become impossible to carry. Horses were dying, and cannon were being left by the roadside. The emperor made a decision: the bridging train, which carried the pontoons used for river crossings, would be burned, and the horses that pulled those wagons would be handed over to the artillery. The logic was understandable. In the dry days of summer, hauling heavy pontoons down the middle of the road had seemed like a luxury. The guns, on the other hand, were the army's last means of defense.

In charge of the bridging train was General Jean-Baptiste Eblé. He was fifty-four years old, an artilleryman by training, a quiet and meticulous man. Under his command were the pontoniers, specialist soldiers trained to build pontoon and trestle bridges across rivers. They numbered around four hundred, and a large share of them were Dutch. These Dutch pontoniers had grown up in a country laced with rivers and canals, men well used to working with water. One of their companies was led by Captain George Diederich Benthien.

Eblé obeyed the order. The pontoons were set on fire. But according to those same accounts, there was one thing he did not burn. On his own initiative, perhaps sensing that the rivers were not yet behind them, he held back two portable forges, two wagons of charcoal, and six wagons of tools. In those wagons were axes, adzes, saws, picks, nails, iron clamps, and ropes. He is also said to have ordered each of his men to carry a tool and a quantity of nails on his back. The pontoons were gone, but the hands that knew how to build a bridge, and the iron those hands would need, were still with the army. The whole story of the Berezina was built on this small act of disobedience, or perhaps of foresight.

Once the Borisov bridge had burned, the question facing Napoleon was this: where to cross? The answer came from General Corbineau, one of Oudinot's advance cavalry commanders. Corbineau's brigade had earlier been operating in the north alongside another force, and had reached the river while searching for a way to rejoin the main army. A local peasant showed them a ford north of Borisov, near the village of Studianka, where horses could cross. Corbineau's cavalry rode across the river at that spot and sent word to Oudinot. At Studianka the river was narrow, and the far bank was somewhat higher and relatively firm. What is more, on the opposite side, a road ran west through the marshes toward Zembin.
But there was a problem: Chichagov was also watching the points where the river could be crossed. If he realized the French had chosen Studianka, he could shift his army there and strangle the crossing before it had even begun. What Napoleon did at this moment is remembered as one of the cleverest deceptions of his career. He ordered Oudinot to make showy preparations south of Borisov, near Ukholodi. Soldiers were marched there, trees were cut down, and a great deal of noise was made, as if a bridge were being built. Among the local people, a rumor that the French would cross to the south was deliberately spread. According to some accounts, a few Jewish merchants were given false information about the crossing point, in the expectation that it would reach the Russian side.

The deception worked. Chichagov was already inclined to believe that Napoleon would want to cross to the south, toward Minsk, and the warnings coming from Kutuzov pointed the same way. The admiral shifted his main force south. Opposite Studianka, around the village of Brili, he left only a small force under General Chaplitz, and most of that was later recalled as well. And so, across from the very point where Napoleon meant to cross, the far bank was left almost empty.

On the evening of November twenty-fifth, Eblé and his men reached Studianka. They began tearing down the village's wooden houses. Beams, planks and posts were to be turned into bridge trestles. All through the night, in the darkness, they worked without lighting fires, so that the Cossacks on the far bank would not notice. Clamps and nails were hammered out at the blacksmiths' forges. The pontoneers lashed timbers together to build trestles: triangular or rectangular wooden frames that would stand upright in the water, with planks laid across their tops. The material was wet, warped and uneven; logs pulled from village houses had never been cut for a bridge. But there was nothing else.

The next morning, Napoleon came to Studianka himself. From a small rise on the riverbank, he watched the opposite shore. When he saw that the main weight of the Russian forces had shifted south, he ordered the crossing to begin.

On the morning of November twenty-sixth, Corbineau's cavalry and a group of volunteers crossed to the far bank on rafts and on horseback and drove off the Cossacks there. Artillery batteries were set up on the shore. And the pontoneers went into the water.

Try to picture what these men did. The water came up to their shoulders in some places, to their chests in others. The current carried chunks of ice that slammed into the trestles and into the men's bodies. The trestles had to be fixed to the riverbed, kept level, and then the beams had to be set on top and nailed down. The bottom was muddy, and the trestles kept sinking. The men would climb out of the water, shiver for a few minutes beside a fire on the bank, and then go back in. Some were swept away by the current. Some fainted from the cold and drifted off. But the work never stopped. According to witnesses, Eblé was on the bank among his men, encouraging them one by one.

At around one o'clock, the first bridge, the northern one, built for infantry and cavalry, was finished. Oudinot's corps began crossing at once and established a bridgehead on the far bank. At around four in the afternoon, the second bridge, built more sturdily for guns and wagons, was also complete. The two bridges stood a few hundred meters apart. In the middle of an encirclement, in a single day, Napoleon's army had opened a road across the river.

But the bridges were not permanent structures. The bridge carrying the heavy guns and wagons in particular was constantly being crushed under the load. That night, at around eight o'clock, some of the trestles collapsed. The pontoneers went back into the water, in the dark, in a current full of ice, and repaired them within a few hours. The following night, at two o'clock, the bridge broke again; toward morning it was repaired again. On the afternoon of November twenty-seventh, it collapsed once more. Eblé had divided his men into shifts; while one group worked, the other tried to recover by the fire. From every shift, there were men who never made it back for the next one.

On November twenty-seventh, Napoleon crossed over with the Imperial Guard. In these first days the crossing was relatively orderly, because the bridges were reserved first for armed units. The real problem was the unarmed crowd. Thousands of people had gathered on the eastern bank, around Studianka, huddled around campfires. Many of them could have crossed at night, when the bridges stood empty, but they did not move, out of exhaustion, out of apathy, or out of fear that they would freeze if they left the fire. And when morning came, they all surged onto the bridges at once.

Meanwhile, danger appeared from the north as well. Wittgenstein's troops had made contact with Marshal Victor's corps, which was serving as the rearguard on the eastern bank. On the night of November twenty-seventh, one of Victor's divisions, the division under General Partouneaux, lost its way in the dark while falling back from Borisov to Studianka and blundered into the middle of the Russian lines. Most of the division was surrounded and forced to surrender.
November twenty-eighth was the bloodiest day of the Berezina. The battle raged on both banks of the river at once. On the western bank, Chichagov had realized he'd been deceived and had turned back from the south; in the forest between Brili and Stakhov, the troops of Oudinot and Ney tried to stop him. Oudinot was gravely wounded. In this fighting, the Swiss regiments of Oudinot's corps held a defense that lasted the entire day and turned again and again into bayonet charges; even when their ammunition ran out, they did not fall back. A charge by Polish troops and French cuirassiers along the edge of the snow-covered forest halted the Russian advance.

On the eastern bank, meanwhile, Victor was trying to keep Wittgenstein away from the bridges with the German troops he had left, soldiers from Baden, Hesse and Berg. Just behind the ground where these men were fighting, a crowd tens of thousands strong was massed in front of the bridges. When the Russian guns came within range and opened fire on that crowd, the panic became a full catastrophe. People trampled one another trying to reach the bridges. Horses, wagons, the wounded, women and children were all forced toward the same narrow crossing. Those who fell from the edge of the bridge were swept away by the icy current. Some tried to swim across the river; very few made it. At the approaches to the bridges, heaps of dead people and animals made the crossing even harder. By evening, even Victor's troops had to clear a path through those heaps to reach the bridge.

That night, what remained of Victor's corps crossed over. Still, thousands of people stayed on the eastern bank. Around the fires, beneath the wagons, they waited for morning in a numb stupor.

Eblé had been ordered to set the bridges on fire early in the morning. If the Russians reached the bridges on the eastern bank, it would only be a matter of time before they were on Napoleon's heels. But the general put off carrying out that order. He sent his men into the crowd on the eastern bank, trying to wake people and persuade them to walk toward the bridges. Many did not get up. Some did, but too late.

On the morning of November twenty-ninth, between eight and nine o'clock, Russian advance guards appeared on the heights of Studianka, and Eblé could wait no longer. The bridges were set alight. As the flames began to wrap around the planks, the people on the eastern bank began running toward the bridges. Some tried to cross over the burning bridges. Some threw themselves into the water. The thousands left behind fell into the hands of the Cossacks. The pontoneers had built those bridges with their own hands and kept them standing for three days from inside the water, at the cost of their lives. Now the same hands destroyed them.

The losses at the Berezina are not known for certain. Historians' estimates, counting armed soldiers and the unarmed crowd together, range from twenty-five thousand to forty thousand. Russian losses are estimated at around ten thousand. It is said that when spring came and the ice on the river melted, hundreds of bodies, broken wagons and chests full of plunder emerged all along the banks.

So was the Berezina a defeat, or a victory? The question has been argued for two hundred years. The Russian plan had been to capture Napoleon, and that never happened. The Emperor, his Guard and the armed core of his army slipped out from between three Russian armies and made it west. In Russian public opinion, the blame for this escape fell largely on Chichagov; the admiral spent the rest of his career in the shadow of that accusation, and in the end chose to leave Russia and live in Europe. The Grande Armée, on the other hand, largely ceased to exist as an army after the Berezina. Right after the crossing, the real winter set in. In the first days of December the temperature plunged to brutal lows, and whatever order was left on the road fell apart.

On December third, on the road at Molodechno, Napoleon dictated his famous Twenty-Ninth Bulletin. It was the first official text to openly admit to the French people that the campaign had ended in disaster. When it was published in Paris, families across the country began to wonder what had become of their sons and husbands. The bulletin's final sentence would be remembered for years with bitter irony: it announced that the Emperor's health had never been better. On December fifth, at Smorgon, Napoleon left his army and set out secretly for Paris. When those left behind crossed back over the Neman in mid-December, they were a small remnant of the multitude that had crossed the same river in June.

General Eblé did not live even a month after the Berezina. The days spent in the water, then the cold and the exhaustion that followed, took him just as they had taken his men. He died on December thirty-first, eighteen twelve, in Königsberg, today's Kaliningrad. Most of the roughly four hundred pontoneers under his command also lost their lives, either at the Berezina or in the weeks that followed. Many of them paid for the hours they had spent in the water by freezing to death soon after they climbed out onto the bank. Today Eblé's name is inscribed on the Arc de Triomphe in Paris. And in the Netherlands, the pontoneers of the Berezina are remembered as part of the nation's military memory, with commemorations held every year.
Berezina's memory found a home not only in the countries of those who died there, but in other places too. In Switzerland, the memory of the regiments that fought in the Brili forest on November twenty-eighth lives on in a song. According to one account, that morning a Swiss lieutenant had the men around him sing an old German hymn before the battle, one that likens life to a journey made by night. In Switzerland the song is known as the Berezina Song, and it is still sung today at military ceremonies and funerals.

But Berezina's most lasting legacy is a single word. In French, even today, to say that something has collapsed completely and that no hope remains, people say "c'est la Bérézina," meaning "this is a total Berezina." A crushing defeat in a football match, a company going bankrupt, an election campaign gone badly wrong: any of these can be a Berezina. Most of the French people who use the expression may not know that they are speaking the name of a distant Eastern European river, or what happened on its banks two hundred years ago. What is striking is that the expression carries only one side of the event. Napoleon himself, and some historians on his side, saw Berezina as a military success, because the encirclement had been broken and the army had escaped annihilation. Language refused to accept that. The people's memory did not hold on to those who escaped. It held on to the crowds before the bridges, the people running across burning planks, and the icy water.

Today in Belarus, between Studyanka and Brili, near the place where the crossing was made, there are monuments. The river flows calmly, and the banks are still marshland. In twenty twelve, on the two hundredth anniversary of the event, people from France, Russia, Switzerland, the Netherlands and Poland held commemorations here together. Peoples who were once enemies bowed their heads on the banks of the same river, for the same dead.

The story of Berezina has emperors, marshals and grand strategic moves. But none of these made the crossing possible. What made it possible were logs pulled from dismantled village houses, six wagons of tools that one commander refused to burn, and a few hundred men standing in a river full of ice, submerged up to their shoulders, clinging to one another's bodies to hold the trestles upright. Most of those men's names appear in no record. The bridges they built held for three days, and then they burned. But those three days were enough to change the fate of an army, an emperor, and tens of thousands of people.

We have come to the end of today's episode of Nöron Tarih. Until we meet again in a new story.
