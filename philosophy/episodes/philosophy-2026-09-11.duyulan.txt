A thousand years ago in Basra, a group of thinkers wrote a strange story, the animals  take human beings to court and ask.  Why do you imagine that we are your property?  The human defense is the very one we still use today, reason belongs to us, and therefore  the world is ours.  But is that defense really sound, or do we win only because we are both the judge and  the accused in this case?  In the middle of the 10th century, in Basra, there worked a community of thinkers who had  made a principle of concealing their names.  They called themselves the Ikhwan al-Safa, the Brethren of Purity.  They left behind an encyclopedia made up of 52 epistles, numbers musical intervals, the  movement of the stars, the rules of logic, the descent of the soul into the body and  its rise again.  Among those 52 texts, one resembles none of the others.  It is neither a textbook nor a treatise.  It is a court record.  Its subject is the case the animals bring against humankind, and the place where the  case is heard is the court of the king of the jinn.  The scene is set like this.  In the middle of the green sea, somewhere near the equator, lies the island of Samhain.  Its ruler is Biwarasp the Wise, king of the jinn, the text presents him as a sovereign  who is just, generous, a refuge for the stranger.  A ship breaks apart in a storm and human beings are cast up on this island.  The island is fertile, they stay, they settle, they sow and reap.  And then they lay hands on the island's older inhabitants.  Ropes go around necks, loads onto backs, pens are built, gates are closed.  The animals, who had roamed freely on those plains since the time of their ancestors,  one day speak among themselves and resolve to choose a delegation and come before the  king.  Something happens that is rarely found in the history of literature.  The animals take the plaintiff's seat, and the human being takes the chair of the accused.  The mule speaks first.  It describes the iron forced into its mouth, the loads piled on its back beyond what it  can carry.  The whip that comes when it slows, and being fed not when its belly is empty but when its  work is done.  The ox and the donkey describe a working day that begins before sunrise and ends in the  dark.  The ram speaks less of its wool being taken every year than of the thing that waits for  it at the end.  The camel speaks of thirst, the elephant of chains, the hare of being chased for sport.  When the bee takes the floor and lays out the finest point in the whole case, it made  its honey for its own young, not to be given to anyone.  When a human being comes and takes it, he is not accepting a gift, he is emptying out  a property.  The parrot is left for last, and it imitates the sentences human beings use to explain  their own superiority, heard from its beak, those sentences ring a little strangely.  The human side makes its defense in an orderly way, not a scattered one.  But men of every nation rise and turn and build the same skeleton of argument, we are  the masters, you are the servants.  As proof they point to their upright posture, the gripping power of their fingers, speech,  reasoning, the arts they practice, the cities they have built.  They recall the trust that the sacred texts placed in humankind.  Then they come to the proof they rely on most, your meat, your milk, your wool, your strength  serve us, and if the existence of one thing serves another, then it was created for that  other's sake.  The animal's answer cuts that last proof straight through the middle.  Benefit is no proof of ownership.  The lion too, eats human beings, does that mean the human being was created for the lion?  The mosquito feeds on human blood, does the human being become the mosquito's property?  They object to the matter of upright posture as well, but not with pride, with an inventory,  our bodies are born complete.  Our fur is on our backs, our weapons are on our faces, we find our way with no one to  show it to us, and we run within a short time of coming into the world.  The human infant, meanwhile is born the most helpless of all living creatures, for years  it must be carried, clothed, fed.  Your arts, then, are the product not of your superiority but of your deficiency.  You invented weaving because you had no fur, the house because you had no shelter, the  weapon because you had no teeth.  And with that same ingenuity you also invented the bit, the whip, the cage and the butcher's  hook.  These did not exist in nature, they arrived with you.  The most striking thing about this trial is perhaps not what the two sides say, but who  sits on the bench.  The judge is neither human nor animal.  He is a jinni, a third intelligence outside both parties bound to the interest of neither.  The writers in Basra can hardly have chosen this by accident.  The hearing lasts for days, the court disperses, and Bewarist does not announce his verdict  that day.  The ground of the argument in Basra was this, are we doing the animal an injustice, and  if so, how great an injustice.  Seven centuries later, in Europe, the defense made an entirely different move.  It stopped debating the degree of the injustice, and opened up for debate the very existence  of the plaintiff.  In 1637, in Holland, Descartes published his Discourse de la Maîtode, the Discourse on  the Method.  In the fifth part of the book he describes the animal body, from beginning to end, as  a mechanism, the heart is a pump, the vessels are pipes, the nerves are pull cords, and  the muscles are bellows that swell and shorten as fluid flows through them.  This description did not startle the people of his day, the way it startles us.  In Descartes' youth there were water-driven grotto machines in the royal gardens of France,  at Saint-Germain-en-Laye, when a visitor stepped on a certain stone figure's playing instruments  would begin to move.  A goddess would hide herself, a sea god would come forward.  And the clock towers of the cities demonstrated daily that a gear and a spring, without knowing  what they were doing, could produce an unerring order.  From this Descartes draws two criteria.  The first is language, though not in the sense of making sounds.  A machine makes sounds too, touch it and it may cry out.  What distinguishes us is that the one in front of you can arrange words afresh so as to reply  appropriately to everything you say.  The second is the difference between doing something through knowledge and doing it through  the arrangement of one's organs.  A clock keeps time better than we do, but the clock has no notion that there is such  a thing as time.  For Descartes the animal is like this, an automaton made by nature, far surpassing every  device that has come from human hands.  Here one has to be careful, because Descartes himself was more cautious than his students.  In a letter of 1649 to the English thinker Henry Moore, he says it plainly, he is speaking  of thought, not of sensation, and in so far as sensation depends on a bodily organ, he  denies it to no animal.  In the same letter he goes further, writing that in his view it cannot be proved that  there is thought in animals, but neither can it be proved that there is none, because the  human mind cannot reach inside them.  That sentence, written in the last year of his life, is not a claim.  It is an admission that he does not know.  Those who came after did not carry that caution with them.  It was a period in which the method was less attractive than the conclusion.  One of the memoirists of the Port Royal Circle records that living animals were cut open  and examined there, and that the sounds they made were received as the sound of an organ  pipe or of a stretched spring.  According to Fontenelle, the philosopher Malebranche, kicked a pregnant dog that had brushed against  his leg, and when the man beside him recoiled, answered, Don't you know that it feels nothing  at all?  The story itself is an anecdote, but the mentality it describes is a mentality on the record.  Put in the language of the courtroom, this is the strongest strategy the defense can  find.  Easier than proving that you are in the right, is proving that there is no one across from  you.  If the scream is the noise of a spring, there is no pain, if there is no pain, there is  no victim, if there is no victim, the case is dismissed.  The sharpest answer to this defense within its own century came from Voltaire.  In the article on animals in his philosophical dictionary he describes a dog that has lost  its master, searching every room, pacing restlessly, and at last finding him and showing  its joy with its whole body.  Then he asks his question, to the man who nails this dog to a table and cuts it open  alive, what are those nerves, the very same as your own, therefore?  To feel nothing.  The machine defense lost its standing in that century, but it did not disappear, it only  changed clothes.  Today nobody says, in the crude form, that an animal does not feel.  What is said is this, we cannot really know what is going on inside it.  The strangeness of that sentence is that it works in one direction only.  The same we cannot know is never raised about the person at the next desk.  In 1789, in England, a philosopher of law broke this closed circle with a footnote.  In the last chapter of Jeremy Bentham's An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and  Legislation, squeezed in at the bottom of a page, there are a few lines.  Bentham begins by showing the inconsistency of the criteria.  A full-grown horse or dog is, beyond comparison, a more rational and more communicable creature  than an infant of a day or a week old.  So if reason and speech are taken as the measure, that measure does not fall where it is wanted,  it leaves the infant outside and the horse inside.  Then he writes the famous sentence.  The question is not, can they reason, nor is it, can they talk?  The question is can they suffer?  And right beside it he sets a comparison.  The blackness of the skin can be no reason for abandoning a human being to the whim of  a tormentor.  He was writing this in a world where slavery was still legal.  What Bentham did, technically, was to shift the boundary.  Until then the circle of morality had been drawn according to capacity.  What can a being do?  Can it reason?  Can it speak?  Can it make a promise?  Bentham drew the circle according to vulnerability.  What can be done to that being?  The first criterion takes in the strong.  The second takes in the one who suffers.  Bentham's own limits are worth stating, in the interest of honesty.  He went on eating meat, and argued that a painless death did no wrong to the animal,  his reasoning was that the animal does not anticipate its death in advance, that the  end awaiting it in nature is usually far worse.  Whether that calculation is correct still sits at the very centre of the debate today,  but the ledger in which the account was opened is his.  In those same years, in Konigsberg, Kant was arriving somewhere else entirely.  In his view we have no direct duties toward animals, because duty arises only toward rational  beings who are ends in themselves.  Cruelty to an animal is wrong all the same, but only indirectly it dulls the capacity  for pity within a person and hardens them toward other people as well.  The whipped animal, in other words is not a victim, it is a wound opening in the character  of the one wielding the whip.  Schopenhauer could not stomach this view, he said that Kant's conception of morality  treated the animal as a thing, and wrote that European philosophy had a great deal to learn  from Asian thought on this question.  In the 20th century the footnote turned into a program.  In 1970, Richard Rider gave a name to an attitude that mirrors the structure of racism.  Speciesism, discounting a being's interests simply because it belongs to another species.  In 1975 Peter Singer's Animal Liberation appeared, Singer argued not that animals should be granted  human rights, but that where pain and pleasure are concerned, then equal interest should  be given equal weight.  In 1983 Tom Regan went further, some animals are the subject of a life, they have memories  , expectations, preferences and a future of their own, and a being like that cannot  be a mere instrument for someone else's purposes.  Meanwhile, the question was also being worked on in laboratories.  Researchers measured how widespread pain receptors are among vertebrates, how fish, once harmed,  do not merely flee but alter their behavior for days afterwards, and how that alteration  reverses when painkillers are administered.  In 2012, neuroscientists gathered at Cambridge signed a declaration on consciousness.  The neural structures that generate consciousness are found not only in humans, but in all mammals,  in birds, that and in certain other creatures such as the octopus.  A similar declaration published in New York in 2024 pushed the boundary wider still, stating  that the scientific grounding is strong for birds and mammals, and that the possibility  of conscious experience must be taken seriously for reptiles, fish, cephalopods, crustaceans,  and even insects.  The upshot rests in a strange place.  What the mule of Basra had to say can now be measured.  The existence of suffering is documented with a certainty unimaginable 200 years ago.  But evidence is not standing to sue.  A being whose suffering has been proven still has nowhere to file a petition.  Other ways of living with that silence had been tried as well.  In Istanbul, in Bursa, in the old cities of Anatolia, there are little stone houses carved  into the walls of mosques, caravanserais, madrasas and fountains, bird palaces, complete  with eaves, windows, sometimes even columns.  It is architecture at the scale of a bird, something built into a wall for a bird, and  not placed there as a display for anyone.  In the records of charitable foundations, one finds line items set aside for birdseed,  and revenues left to buy meat for the street animals.  In 1928 Amit Hashim wrote about a place he saw in Bursa, the Garabahani Ilakhlakhan,  the house of forlorn storks.  It was a place where storks with broken wings, unable to migrate, were cared for.  There is a clear foundation of ideas behind this behavior.  The Quran says that the animals walking the earth and the birds flying on their wings  are communities just as humans are.  The body of hadith tells of a person who drew water from a well for a thirsty dog and was  forgiven, and of a person who shut a cat away and let it starve and was held to account  for it.  In Ibn Tufel's Hayy ibn Yaqsun, the man on the island reorders his own eating as he matures,  he turns to fallen fruit and to seeds that have already multiplied themselves, trying  as far as he can, to take nothing living out of the world.  But the registers of that same city hold another entry.  In 1910, the street dogs of Istanbul were rounded up and left on a bare rock in the  Sea of Marmara, Sivriyada, known in common speech as Hayyir Sizada, the island of no  use.  There was no water there, no shade, no food.  Tens of thousands of animals died, by some accounts, when the wind blew from a certain  direction the sound carried to the city.  The hand that carved the bird palace and the hand that loaded that boat belonged to the  same culture.  The issue is not hypocrisy, but category one was counted as a subject of compassion, the  other as a line item of municipal administration, which column an animal is written into, determines  what will be done to it.  In the last century, a handful of animals came very close to shifting that column.  An organization called the Non-Human Rights Project petitioned the courts on behalf of  large animals held in zoos, what it sought was neither damages nor punishment.  But that old legal remedy demanding that a person deprived of liberty be brought before  a judge.  In June of 2022, the New York Court of Appeals rejected one such petition, filed for happy,  an elephant at the Bronx Zoo, by a vote of 5-2.  The majority wrote that this remedy was open to persons, and that widening its scope would  bring consequences impossible to calculate.  The judge who wrote in dissent said that the conditions in which the elephant was held  amounted to an injustice.  In Argentina, two rulings went the other way.  In Buenos Aires, the description non-human person was applied to Sandra, an orangutan,  and in 2016, in Mendoza, a petition was granted for Cecilia, a chimpanzee.  Dew was sent to a sanctuary in Brazil.  In 2020, in Pakistan, the Islamabad High Court, ruling on the case of Kaavan, an elephant  kept in chains for years, grounded its decision in Islamic sources governing the treatment  of animals and ordered the elephant released.  There is another line of development that proceeds without entering the debate over  personhood at all.  The European Union, in the 13th article of the Treaty of Lisbon, which came into force  in 2009, defined animals as sentient beings.  In 2022, the United Kingdom passed a sentience law covering octopuses and lobsters alongside  vertebrates.  In Turkey, the Animal Protection Law, number 5199, adopted in 2004, was amended in 2021  to remove animals from the category of objects and property.  Acts of torture and killing were tied to prison sentences.  While all of this was happening, the scale of the case grew to a place never seen before  in history.  Today around 80 billion land animals are slaughtered worldwide every year.  The number taken from the water is so vast that no count by head is even kept.  It is measured by weight.  And yet, in the daily life of cities, none of this makes a sound.  The slaughterhouse moved out of the city center to the edge, then beyond the edge of town,  it went into windowless buildings, and the trucks began moving at night.  The silence on the plate is not the absence of a scream, it is distance.  This may be the only working form the machine defense has left today, instead of saying  the animal does not feel, keeping the place where it feels outside the field of vision.  The question of what that court in Basra decided finds its meaning here.  Bewarasp ended the case in favor of the human beings.  But it is worth looking at how he ended it.  Not by refuting a single one of the things the mule, the ox, the ram and the bee had  said.  The final word from the human side was this.  We are beings who will answer for what we do.  A responsibility has been laid upon us.  There is a test and there is a reckoning.  The judge accepted that claim.  Which means the reasoning behind the ruling was not that humanity is innocent, but that  humanity is responsible.  A verdict that reads like an acquittal, and works like a sentence.  Nearly everyone who reads the text notices the same thing, on every question of fact,  the animals had won.  If the same case were brought today, we are the ones who would convene the court.  The party writing the law, appointing the judge, choosing the expert witness and making  the defense, is one and the same party.  No one can be the judge of their own case.  That is precisely why, a thousand years ago in Basra, a jinn was seated on the bench.  The only honest thing we have to put in the place of that third mind is the question we  hear inside our own head standing in front of a shelf at the market, or in the moment  we sit down at the table.  The record is closed and the judgment handed down, the only line left blank is the one  that says how the responsibility is to be paid.
