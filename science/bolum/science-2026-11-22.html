Swirl a glass of wine, set it down on the table, and look at the inside of the glass. Untouched, the liquid climbs the wall and then slides back down in heavy drops. What drives this small display of defiance against gravity is not the quality of the drink. It is a quiet battle of evaporation going on at the rim of the glass.

Set a glass of red wine on the table, swirl it a few times, and wait. Within a minute or two, a thin wet band appears on the inner surface of the glass, a few centimetres above the level of the liquid. The lower edge of this band thickens and ripples, and then drops break away from it. The drops slowly slide down to the bottom of the glass, leaving thin trails behind them. The band stays where it is, and new drops keep forming. This sight is called the tears of wine. In the English-speaking world they are called the legs of wine, and in Germany they are known as church windows, because the arches left between the drops look like Gothic stained glass.

Something in this scene seems to go against gravity. The drops sliding down the glass are not surprising; gravity expects exactly that. What is surprising is how the liquid that feeds those drops got up there in the first place. The wine climbs the wall from the bottom of the glass, collects at a certain height, and only then falls. Why would a still liquid flow upward on its own?

People must have been watching this sight for as long as they have been drinking wine. Some commentators believe the motion is mentioned in the Book of Proverbs, in the warning against wine that turns red in the cup and "goes down smoothly." That reading is disputed, but it shows how old the observation might be. The date of a scientific explanation, however, is certain: eighteen fifty-five. That year, the Scottish engineer and physicist James Thomson published a short paper on the "curious motions" seen on the surface of alcoholic drinks such as whisky and wine. Thomson was the elder brother of William Thomson, who would later become known by the title Lord Kelvin. He was a practical-minded engineer who worked on flowing water, turbines, and wheels.

What caught Thomson's attention was that the motion on the surface had nothing to do with the liquid in the glass as a whole. It was happening along the line where glass and liquid meet, in a zone just a few millimetres wide. Thomson saw that the answer lay in surface tension. More importantly, he realised that surface tension alone did not explain it; the real driver was surface tension changing from one place to another. Wine is not a single liquid but largely a mixture of water and alcohol. These two components behave very differently from each other. And the secret of the tears lies hidden in that difference.

To understand the idea Thomson put forward, we first need to ask a question: what exactly does the surface of a liquid pull on, and in which direction?

Every molecule inside a liquid is pulled equally in all directions by its neighbours, so the forces balance out. Molecules at the surface, though, have no neighbours above them; they are pulled only from the sides and from below. This imbalance gives the surface a tendency to behave like a taut membrane. The surface wants to shrink as much as it can. That is why drops are round, and why some insects can walk on water. We call the measure of this tautness surface tension.

Water has one of the highest surface tensions of any ordinary liquid. At room temperature it is about seventy-two millinewtons per metre. The surface tension of ethyl alcohol is a little less than a third of that, about twenty-two millinewtons per metre. When you mix the two, the resulting value depends on the proportion of alcohol in the mixture: the more alcohol, the lower the surface tension; the less alcohol, the closer it gets to that of water. A typical wine, containing between twelve and fourteen percent alcohol by volume, has a surface tension noticeably lower than that of pure water.

Now suppose that the surface tension is not the same everywhere on a liquid's surface. Let one region be tauter and a neighbouring region slacker. You can think of it as a tug of war. The region with higher surface tension pulls the surface toward itself more strongly. The surface slides, dragging the liquid just beneath it along. So the liquid flows from the region of low surface tension toward the region of high surface tension. You can easily see this at home. Sprinkle a little black pepper on the water in a plate and touch the middle with a soapy finger. Where the soap touches, it lowers the surface tension. The tauter water surface around it pulls itself outward, and the pepper grains instantly rush to the edge of the plate.

These flows, driven by differences in surface tension, take their name from the Italian physicist Carlo Marangoni. In eighteen sixty-five, in his doctoral thesis at the University of Pavia, Marangoni studied how a drop of one liquid spreads when placed on the surface of another. In the years that followed, he systematically investigated how differences in surface tension set liquid surfaces in motion. Thomson had laid out the basic idea ten years earlier. For this reason, some sources refer to the phenomenon as the "Thomson–Marangoni effect," but the name that stuck was the Marangoni effect. Let's apply this principle to the glass. When you swirl the glass, a thin layer of wine coats the inside surface. And where the liquid meets the glass, there is a meniscus that curves gently upward along the wall. This meniscus comes from the attraction between the glass and the liquid. In these thin regions the layer of liquid is very shallow, and the surface exposed to the air is very large compared to its volume. That is why evaporation happens so quickly there.

Here is the crucial point: alcohol is more volatile than water, which means it evaporates more easily. In the thin layer, alcohol escapes into the air faster than water does. So the thin film of wine on the wall becomes slightly more "watery" than the wine in the middle of the glass. And because there is less alcohol, the surface tension rises. The result is a gradient on the glass, with higher surface tension above and lower surface tension below.

Remember the tug-of-war. The tauter surface above pulls the slacker surface below toward itself. The surface slides upward and carries the liquid beneath it along. So the wine in the bowl of the glass climbs up the wall as a thin film. The energy for this climb comes from evaporation. Evaporation keeps drawing alcohol from the tip of the film, and so the difference in surface tension is maintained. The fresh wine arriving from below is rich in alcohol. As it rises, it too loses its alcohol and helps keep the difference going.

But the liquid cannot climb forever. As it rises, gravity pulls it back. The liquid carried upward collects at the top edge of the film and forms a thick ridge. Once this ridge reaches a certain thickness, it becomes unstable. Instead of staying a straight line, it begins to ripple and breaks up into a series of separate bulges. When the bulges grow heavy enough, gravity overcomes the Marangoni pull, and droplets slide back down. These are the tears. The falling drop returns to the glass, but the ridge stays where it is, because the flow that feeds it carries on.

There is a simple way to test this explanation. Cover the mouth of a glass with tears running down it, using a plate or a lid. Before long, you will see the tears slow down and then stop. The air inside the glass becomes saturated with alcohol vapor, evaporation almost stops, the difference in alcohol disappears, and the driving force behind the Marangoni flow is gone. When you lift the lid, evaporation starts again and the tears return. You can also compare it with a single non-alcoholic drink: grape juice swirled in the same glass simply runs down the wall and does not form a thick climbing band.

Evaporation has a side effect as well. The evaporating liquid absorbs heat, so the thin film cools slightly. Cooler liquids generally have higher surface tension. This temperature difference adds a weak extra pull in the same direction. Even so, most researchers agree that the dominant factor in the tears of wine is the difference in alcohol content.

For a long time, it seemed the matter was settled. Throughout the twentieth century, the idea of Thomson and Marangoni appeared in textbooks as an elegant example of surface forces. But it was also known that the explanation was not detailed enough. Why does the film stop at a certain height? Why does the ridge break into droplets at regular intervals? What role does swirling the glass play? The answers to these questions came from a rather exotic corner of fluid mathematics.

In two thousand one, Anette Hosoi and John Bush of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology published a study on climbing films driven by evaporation. They showed in detail that the droplets arise from an instability that develops in the ridge at the front of the film. But the most striking part of the story came about twenty years later, from the applied mathematician Andrea Bertozzi and her team at the University of California, Los Angeles. Together with Yonatan Dukler, Hangjie Ji and Claudia Falcon, Bertozzi built a mathematical model of the climbing film. They presented their results at the end of twenty nineteen at the American Physical Society's meeting on fluid dynamics, and published them in twenty twenty in the society's fluids journal. Their conclusion was this: the leading edge of the wine climbing the wall of the glass is actually a shock wave.

The phrase "shock wave" brings to mind the boom of a plane breaking the speed of sound, or a supernova. In mathematics, its meaning is broader. A shock is a front where the properties of a medium change almost in a leap, within a very narrow region. In the glass, this is the sharp transition where the thin film is very thin on one side and much thicker on the other. In equations of this kind, shocks also move with a speed and a direction, and there are rules that govern them. In ordinary shocks, information flows into the front from both sides: the regions ahead of and behind the wave "collide" with the front and compress it. The shock that Bertozzi's team found in the wine film was of a different kind. It was what mathematicians call an "undercompressive" shock. In these shocks, information does not arrive at the front from both sides. The front advances behind a ridge of its own making, and its structure depends not only on the conditions on either side, but also on the delicate physics within the front itself, namely surface tension and the curvature of the film. Shocks of this kind are rare in nature and are especially intriguing structures from a mathematical point of view. Bertozzi's group had studied them before, in earlier work on thin films driven up an inclined surface by Marangoni stress. This time, they showed that the same kind of structure forms every evening at dinner tables. According to the team's model, the shock in the wine's climb is a front that advances upward, against gravity. The droplets form when the ridge ahead of this front becomes unstable.

The model also explained another everyday detail. Swirling the glass is not just a tasting ritual; it is part of the physics, too. Swirling pre-wets the inside of the glass with a thin film of wine. Bertozzi's team showed that the behavior of the climbing front depends on the thickness of this pre-coated film and on how high the liquid rises. The team also ran experiments: they swirled mixtures with different alcohol contents in glasses, watched the climbing front, and found that their observations matched the model's predictions. The sight in an ordinary wine glass was a living demonstration of one of the subtlest consequences of thin-film equations.

This scientific picture also has an everyday consequence, and it contradicts a belief that has long lived on in wine culture. Many wine lovers believe that a wine leaving pronounced, slow-moving tears in the glass is "fuller," or of higher quality. In some tasting settings, the tears are even read as a clue to a wine's body and age. Another common explanation is that the tears come from the glycerol or sugar in the wine. Glycerol is a syrupy, viscous compound produced as a by-product of fermentation.

Physics does not support these beliefs. The engine that creates the tears is the difference in evaporation and surface tension between alcohol and water. So what most determines how strong the tears are is the wine's alcohol content. As the alcohol rises, the surface tension difference driven by evaporation generally becomes more pronounced, and the tears become more visible. The surroundings matter as well: in a dry, warm room, evaporation is fast; in a damp, cool cellar, it is slow. How clean the glass is, and whether it carries traces of dish detergent, also changes the outcome. Glycerol and sugar can alter the liquid's viscosity slightly and slow the flow of the droplets, but they are not the source of the tears. A mixture of water and alcohol, entirely free of glycerol, also produces flawless tears. Bertozzi underlined this point when explaining the results: the tears tell you about the alcohol, not about the quality of the wine.

This does not mean that tasting is meaningless. A wine's color, aroma, and structure on the palate say a great deal about it. The tears tell you something too, but that something is not quality; it is alcohol content. A cheap wine with high alcohol can leave more impressive tears than an expensive but light one. The sight in the glass is beautiful, but it is no certificate of quality.

Perhaps the most striking part of this story is how widely the principle behind this little show in the glass is put to work. Today the Marangoni effect is not merely a curiosity, but a tool that engineers use deliberately. In microchip manufacturing, silicon wafers must be dried perfectly after every rinsing step, because even a single droplet drying on the surface can leave marks and contamination. In a method that became widespread in the nineteen-nineties and takes its name directly from Marangoni, isopropyl alcohol vapor is directed onto the wafer's surface as it is drawn out of the water. This vapor lowers the surface tension along the line where the wafer meets the water. The resulting Marangoni flow pulls the water back off the wafer's surface, toward the bulk of the liquid, and the wafer emerges almost bone dry. This is the phenomenon in the wine glass applied in reverse: in wine, the surface tension difference pulls the liquid up along the glass, while in wafer drying, it draws the water away from the surface.

The same principle is also being studied to understand the stains that drying droplets leave behind. In inkjet printing, coating technologies, and medical diagnostic tests, the way a droplet dries determines how uniform the final product will be. Steering these flows by manipulating surface tension differences is an active area of engineering research. Marangoni flows driven by temperature differences, meanwhile, are being studied as an important factor shaping the pool of molten metal during welding, and the behavior of liquids aboard space stations, where gravity is barely felt. There, because gravity-driven flows all but vanish, surface tension differences run the show on their own. When James Thomson gazed into his glass in eighteen fifty-five, he could not have foreseen any of this. He had no computer model, and no mathematics of shock waves. But he refused to treat an ordinary sight as ordinary. He saw that the small rebellion on the wall of the glass was born of an imbalance between the two components of the mixture. Marangoni gave the idea a solid experimental foundation. Hosoi and Bush revealed the instability from which the droplets are born. And Bertozzi and her team showed that the climbing front carries one of the most subtle structures in all of applied mathematics. Over more than a century and a half, the same glass posed a different question to four generations of scientists.

The next time you swirl your glass, that thin band climbing the wall will carry the trace not only of the wine, but of evaporating alcohol, of water pulling the surface toward itself, and of a shock front advancing against gravity. Cover the mouth of the glass and the show stops; uncover it and it begins again. You don't need a laboratory to see it with your own eyes.
