One morning, in the very middle of the most crowded act of worship on earth, a group of  men who called themselves pilgrims drew their swords.  When they left, they left behind a Kaaba with its door torn off, a well stuffed with corpses,  and an empty hollow in a wall.  That stone, from the heart of the Islamic world, would stand for 21 years in the wall  of another mosque, a thousand kilometers away.  The coastline of eastern Arabia, that thin band of the Persian Gulf wedged between sand  and salt, is a place that has rarely attracted the interest of great empires.  Date groves, springs of fresh water, a few harbors where pearl divers lived, and beyond  them, emptiness stretching inland.  At the end of the 9th century, something took shape in that emptiness that the Caliphal  court in Baghdad never saw coming, a state that minted its own coinage, enforced its  own law, that and carried its own calendar and its own expectation of a savior.  The roots of the story reach back to the barren villages around Kufa.  In the 870s, traveling missionaries of the Ismaili call were moving through this region.  What they preached was profoundly appealing to peasants crushed under heavy taxes and  to the Bedouin at the desert's edge.  The present order is temporary, oppression will not last forever, the awaited Imam is  near.  And when he comes, everything will be turned upside down.  The man who organized this call in the region was named Hamdan Karmat.  The movement took its name from his, they came to be called the Karmatians.  Hamdan Karmat vanished from the historical record around 906, but the network he built  lived on.  The furthest and most durable branch of that network, took root in Bahrain.  This Bahrain is not today's island state, it is the broad coastal region stretching  from Qadif to Al-Asa, which the Arabs of that age called Bahrain.  The missionary sent to the region was named Abu Sayyid al-Janabi.  His title came from the town of Janaba in Iran, where his family originated.  Abu Sayyid began his work disguised as a flower merchant, won over the tribes one by one,  and in 899 seized Al-Asa and made it his capital.  That same year, a great rupture tore through the movement, the leadership in Syria proclaimed  that the awaited Imam had now appeared, and that this man was Ubaid Allah al-Mahdi.  Who would later found the Fatimid dynasty in North Africa.  The Karmatians of Bahrain refused to accept the claim.  As far as they were concerned, the awaited savior had still not come, and come he would.  That refusal was a fracture that would determine a great deal in the years ahead.  They now owed allegiance neither to the Sunni Caliphate in Baghdad, nor to the Ismaili Caliphate  in North Africa.  To no one.  Abu Sayyid was assassinated in 913, the sources report that he died in his bathhouse, at the  hand of a servant.  Rule passed to his sons.  What made this state truly extraordinary was less its capacity for war than its internal  order.  A century later, in 1051, the Persian traveler Nasir-e-Khusraal, passed through Al-Asa and  described what he saw with astonishment.  No taxes were levied on the people of the city.  Anyone in difficulty was given an interest-free loan.  Local trade was conducted not in gold or silver but with tokens made of lead, these tokens  were valid only inside the city, which meant that wealth could not seep out.  The state's mills ground the people's grain free of charge.  Houses that fell into ruin were repaired at public expense.  Government did not rest in the hands of a single ruler.  A council of six, drawn from the descendants of Abu Sayyid, took the decisions, and in  disputes an advisory assembly stepped in.  There was no mosque in the city where the Friday prayer was held.  The call to prayer was not sounded.  The savior had not come, and so, in their view, the older forms of worship were suspended.  It is easy to read this picture as a medieval utopia, but the foundation beneath it was  far darker.  According to the same account by Nasir-e-Khusraal, those vast date and grain estates around Al-Asa  and Qadaf were worked by the labor of some 30,000 black slaves.  The absence of taxes, the interest-free credit, the free milling, all of it belonged to the  community of property owners, the hands that filled that community's common treasury  owned nothing at all.  This state built in the desert was an egalitarian machine for its own members and a merciless  one for everyone outside.  And there was a second source of revenue, the raid.  Because the Karmatians regarded themselves as standing outside the existing order, they  treated that order's caravans, cities and pilgrims as legitimate targets.  The storehouses of Al-Asa were also filled with plunder taken on the roads of Iraq.  And in time, these raids would move far beyond an ordinary economy of pillage.  To think of the Karmatians merely as a desert state of brigands is not enough to explain  what happened in 930.  What brought them to that point was a belief about time itself.  This belief held that history moved in closed cycles.  Every great prophet had his era, and every era had its end.  The order Muhammad had brought would one day run out its term as well, then the awaited  redeemer would come, what was sealed would be opened, and outward law would give way  to inner truth.  With the break of 899, the community in Bahrain had chosen to keep that expectation alive.  For them, in other words, the question was not whether to obey religious law, but whether  that law still held it all.  According to the sources, this reckoning drew support from the heavens as well.  The great conjunction of stars that astrologers placed at the turn of the century was read  as the date when the age of Islam would close and a new age would open.  The man who showed how destructive that expectation could be is Abu Tahir Sulayman al-Jannabi,  who took power in 923.  He was one of the sons of Abu Said, he pushed his elder brother aside, seized the leadership,  and entered Basra that same year.  In 927 he sacked Kufa and routed the Caliphal army sent against him.  In 928 he advanced to the very threshold of Baghdad.  He could not enter the capital, but he ranged across most of Iraq and plundered it.  Before all these raids, back in 906, the pilgrim caravan returning from Mecca had been ambushed,  and by the figure the sources give, close to 20,000 pilgrims were put to the sword.  The Karmatians, then, were not a community that shrank from laying hands on the sacred  roads, on the contrary, laying hands on those roads was itself their message.  To grasp where this mindset could lead, we need to look one year ahead of Mecca, to 931.  Because that was the year the Karmatian state came face to face with the logical conclusion  of its own faith.  In 931 Abu Tahir gave his allegiance, as to the awaited redeemer, to a young man from  Isfahan, Abu Lufadl al-Isfahani, who counted himself a descendant of the Sasanian kings,  and handed the government over to him.  The one who for long years had been called, the one who is to come, had come.  The result was a devastation that not even the community's own leaders had reckoned with.  The new Mahdi abolished the whole of religious law.  The obligations of prayer and fasting fell away.  Books were burned.  The prophets were openly cursed, not Muhammad alone, but an entire line stretching from  Abraham to Jesus.  The man rejected the supremacy of the Arabs, began to exalt the legacy of the Persian kings,  and had a number of the Karmatian notables executed.  This was an age of salvation that began with the blood of its own leaders.  Abu Tahir endured it for 80 days.  Then he declared that the Mahdi he had raised to the throne with his own hands was an imposter,  and had him killed.  The Karmatian state never fully recovered from those 80 days, Abu Tahir's spiritual  authority within the community was gravely wounded, and the movement's branches in other  regions lost their trust in the center at Bahrain.  This episode shows the mental climate in which what happened at Mecca a year earlier was  possible.  Because for someone who genuinely believes the old order has run out its term, the holiest  object of that order is no longer untouchable.  It is a portable thing.  It can be bargained over.  It can even be prized from its place and set in the mosque of another city, with the expectation  that people will turn toward it there.  That is precisely what Abu Tahir attempted.  And the day he attempted it was the most shattering morning in the 10th century Islamic world.  The 8th day of Dhu al-Hijjah, in the year 317, the 11th of January, 930.  It was the most crowded moment of the pilgrimage season.  The pilgrims were in a state of ritual consecration, they wore nothing but two seamless pieces  of white cloth, and they were forbidden to carry weapons.  Most were preparing to set out for Mina the next day.  The city was filled with people who had come from every corner of the world, from Khurasan,  from Egypt, from Al-Andalus, from Yemen.  When Abu Tahir arrived at the city gate with his horsemen, he did not force his way in.  He said that every Muslim had the right to enter this city, and gave his assurance that  he would harm no one.  Mecca's resistance was fragile, the assurance was accepted, and the gates were opened.  Then the swords were drawn.  The sources recount the days that followed with differing details, but with the same  horror.  Qarmatian cavalry rode through the courtyard of the sacred mosque, into the consecrated  and defenseless crowd.  It is reported that as they killed, they recited verses of the Quran, mocking their victims  with the words of their own scripture.  At night they left the city for their camps outside, and in the morning they came back  in.  This went on for 8 days, in some accounts, 11.  The sources do not agree on the number of the dead, some say thousands within the bounds  of the sanctuary, others put the total as high as 30,000.  This much is certain, the bodies went unburied for days, left lying in the streets and in  the courtyard.  Then something heavier still was done.  Some of the dead were thrown into the well of Zamzam.  Mecca's lifeblood, the well whose water pilgrims drank as a healing, was filled with corpses.  This was not a military act, it was a message.  A message that said, nothing here is sacred.  The Kaaba itself became a target.  Its covering was pulled down, cut into pieces, and divided up as spoils.  Its door was torn from its frame.  It is recorded that the Kaaba was stained with the blood of slaughtered pilgrims, and  that the building was set alight.  The scale of the plunder is described in such terms that more than 50 camels were said to  be needed to carry it away, modern historians regard that figure as an exaggeration.  The governor of Mecca, Muhammad ibn Ismail, was killed while trying to defend the city.  A few things were saved, the station of Abraham was smuggled out of the city, and the golden  water spout on the roof remained in place.  But what Abu Tahir really wanted was something else.  In the eastern corner of the Kaaba, set into the wall at a height just below that of a  man, was a dark colored stone.  The Black Stone.  In Islamic tradition it is held to have been placed there from the time the Kaaba was built.  It marks the starting point of each circuit during the circumambulation, and it is customary  to kiss it.  Doeth Salwar to salute it from a distance.  The one physical object believed to have been touched by the Prophet's hand.  According to the account, when the people of Mecca invoked the sanctity of the Kaaba  against him, Abu Tahir answered that this house did not belong to God, and that God  would not choose a house for himself.  As the stone was prized from the wall with pick and iron, it broke.  The piece was already cracked, and under this assault it came apart into several fragments.  The sources speak of seven pieces.  The fragments were gathered up, loaded onto camels, and the caravan set out eastward,  towards Hajar.  What was left behind was an empty hollow.  In the corner of the Kaaba, where the stone had been torn out, a cavity opening into the  wall.  The point where, eight days earlier people from the four corners of the world had queued  and reached out to touch, was now a wound.  Abu Tahir's plan was plain.  He installed the stone in the mosque of his own country.  His intention was to redirect the pilgrimage, to tear people away from Mecca and draw them  to Hajar.  He believed that as long as he held the stone, he would hold its sanctity too.  This was the beginning of a delusion that would last 21 years.  When the news reached Baghdad, al-Muqtadir sat on the caliph's throne.  Raised to it in the year 908, while still a child he was a caliph tossed about throughout  his reign among commanders, viziers and palace factions.  The treasury was empty, the army preoccupied with mutinies over pay.  One of the caliph's official titles was guardian of the sanctuary and of the pilgrimage road.  After the morning of the year 930, that title rang hollow wherever it was spoken.  There was little that could be done.  To mount a military campaign meant hunting the karmations down in their own desert, and  armies that had tried this before had been routed.  So what remained was diplomacy and money.  When boys were sent to secure the stone's return, vast sums were offered.  Abu Tahir refused them all.  What he held in his hands was worth more than any amount of gold that could be offered,  because the legitimacy of the side offering that gold rested on precisely that corner  where the stone had sat.  The outrage did not come from Baghdad alone.  The event provoked fury throughout the Islamic world, across sectarian lines.  In North Africa, the Fatimid caliph, Ubaid Allah al-Mahdi wrote letters full of reproach  to the karmations of Bahrain, demanding the stone's return.  This detail matters.  The karmations are often lumped together with the Fatimids, yet the raid on Mecca was a  catastrophe for the Fatimids too.  A dynasty trying to present itself as the rightful leadership of Islam gained nothing  whatever from being charged with the plundering of the Kaaba, by a movement sprung from the  same root as itself.  The gravest consequence, though, was felt on the roads.  For years, the pilgrimage could not be made.  The great caravans setting out from Iraq, those crowded convoys traveling by way of  Kufa and Medina, came to a halt.  To send out a pilgrim caravan meant putting thousands of people within range of Karmatian  cavalry, and no one wanted to take that responsibility.  For years only small groups reached Mecca, those willing to accept the risk themselves.  Finally, in the year 939, an agreement was struck, the Karmatians would permit the pilgrim  caravans to pass in return for a payment made to them.  The road to the holy city was reopened by a toll paid to the very people who had plundered  it, that cut deeper into Baghdad's prestige than the loss of the stone.  Al-Muqtadir never saw this solution.  In the year 932, he was killed in a clash with his own soldiers.  He went to his grave as a man who had held the caliphate for 25 years and had been unable  to withstand the greatest humiliation of his age.  Meanwhile, in Mecca life went on in a strange way.  After the agreement, the pilgrims returned.  The circuits were performed.  But in the corner where each circuit began, there was no stone.  People turned towards that empty hollow, raised a hand and saluted the place, then began to  walk.  In the place of the sacred object stood the object's absence, and still people stopped  at the same point and made the same gesture.  This detail reveals the greatest error in Abu Tahir's calculation.  No one went to Hajar.  The stone sat in his mosque, but not a single person counted that mosque a place of pilgrimage,  no one changed direction.  The object had moved, the sanctity had not.  What people sought was not the stone, but the place where the stone belonged and the  meaning attached to that place.  The pilgrimage was made without the stone, and it went on being the pilgrimage.  Abu Tahir never saw the stone go back.  He died of smallpox in 944, he was about 38 at the time.  Pious sources prefer to describe his death in the grip of a hideous disease, rotting  away, as a punishment sent from heaven.  The more measured records we have point to an ordinary epidemic of the period.  Either way, the young leader who had still been in his mid-twenties when he sacked Mecca  was gone before he reached 40, and he left behind a stone that even he had not known  what to do with.  Authority passed to his brothers and to the council descended from Abu Said.  And that council, unlike his older brother, saw that the thing in their hands was not  a source of power but a burden too heavy to carry.  In the year 951, a sack was left in the great mosque of Kufa.  Beside it lay a note saying what was inside.  That one short sentence, as the sources report it, holds all the stubbornness of 21 years.  We took it by a command, and by a command we have brought it back.  No apology, no justification, no mention of a redeemer.  Only the dry notice of a hand that gives as it took.  What brought the Karmatians to this point is not precisely known, the sources offer  different reasons.  By one account, a large sum was paid in a bargain struck with the government in Baghdad.  By another, holding the stone no longer carried any political return at all, the toll taken  from the pilgrimage road was secure in any case.  While the stone did nothing but keep the hatred of the whole Islamic world alive.  For a movement that had lost its old fervor even among its own people after the upheaval  of 931, the empty space in the corner of the Kaaba was no longer a card to play, but a  wait.  It was no accident that the stone was left in Kufa.  The city was the soil in which the movement had been born, and at the same time a place  Baghdad could easily reach.  The sack was taken up, its contents were authenticated, and it was sent to Mecca.  Tradition attaches a story of its own to that moment of verification.  The stone, it is said, would not sink when thrown into water and would not grow hot in  fire.  And it was by this test that the returned fragments were known to be genuine.  A story of the same kind travels along the road as well.  The camel that carried the stone to Hajar is said to have perished on the way, while  the camel that brought it back grew stronger with every mile.  These tales were made in order to bear the sheer size of what had happened.  They are not historical documents, but they do show how deep a mark those 21 years had  left in people's minds.  The stone was set back in place in 951.  The hollow in the corner was closed.  But what was closed was not the same as what had been prized out in 930.  It had come back in pieces.  Setting it in place meant holding those pieces together, it was mounted inside a silver frame,  and the band that carried it was repaired and renewed.  The silver ring you can see today in the eastern corner of the Kaaba, and the few dark fragments  inside it, are the direct trace of that journey.  Over the centuries that frame has been mended many times, replaced, remade, but its function,  to hold the stone together, has never changed.  One of the most touched objects in the world has stood from that day to this, in a broken  state, dependent on a frame.  The Karmatian state outlived the stone, but it never recovered.  On the toll it took from the pilgrimage road, the tribute it gathered from the surrounding  regions and the date groves of Al-Asa, it stayed on its feet for another century.  That city with no taxes and free-running mills, which Nasser Khusraw described with such admiration  in 1051, was in truth a city living on its past.  The awaited Redeemer had not come.  Sportspeople still remembered what the one said to have come in 931 had amounted to,  and nobody dared make the same claim a second time.  Their rule in the region came to an end in 1067.  Today, on that coast of eastern Arabia, what remains of a state that once minted its own  coinage is a few lines of record, and the memory of a stone's absence.  The real question that remains is where Abu Tahir was wrong.  He thought holiness lay in the object.  He imagined that if he took the stone, the center would move with it, that people would  turn in a new direction, that the address of the thing called pilgrimage would become  Hajar.  The stone stayed in his hands for 21 years, and in all those 21 years, not a single pilgrim  caravan went to Hajar.  Even in the years when the stone was gone, pilgrims walked to Mecca, stood before the  empty hollow, raised a hand and began the circling.  What made the stone portable was physics, what made it sacred was the shared orientation  of a community, and that was not something you could load onto a camel.  Anyone who looks at the silver frame in that corner today sees more than an old stone.  The ring that holds the fragments together is the record of a mourning on which an empire  failed to protect its most sacred object, of a well filling with bodies of caravans  that for years could not set out.  Dandand of one silent sentence left in Kufa with a sack.  The object came back, broken, of the state built by the men who took it, not even a frame  remains.  That brings us to the end of today's episode of Neuron Tare.  Until we meet again in a new story.
