Do You Use the Tool, or Does It Use You?

The king of Egypt does not thank the god who invented writing; he says, "This is a drug not for remembering but for forgetting." Twenty-four hundred years later we read that same sentence as people who cannot find their way without navigation and cannot recall a single phone number without a phone. So does the tool merely make our work easier, or is it quietly rewriting us as well?

The story takes place in a city in lower Egypt, in an age when the gods were still assumed to speak with human beings. There was a god named Theuth; he was the one who discovered number and calculation, geometry, the map of the stars, even games played with dice. But there was one invention he boasted of above all the rest: letters. Writing.

One day he comes before Thamus, the king of the land. He lays out all his inventions one by one, and for each of them the king offers either praise or criticism. When the turn comes to writing, Theuth grows excited. Look, he says, this discovery will make the Egyptians wiser and strengthen their memory; this is a remedy for both recollection and wisdom.

The king looks at him for a long moment, and gives this answer: the person who invents a thing is the worst possible judge of its usefulness. Because you are the father of letters, you attribute to writing a power it does not have. Writing will not strengthen memory; it will produce forgetfulness. People will no longer remember from within themselves — they will trust to marks outside them. What you are giving them is not the remedy of memory, but of reminding. Your students will read a great deal and yet learn nothing; they will not be wise, they will merely seem wise.

The one who passes this conversation on to us is Socrates, sitting outside the city walls of Athens, in the shade of a plane tree, his feet in the cool water of a stream. It is an Egyptian tale he tells to his young companion in the dialogue of Plato's known as the Phaedrus. And here, buried inside the story, stands one of the most elegant ironies in human history: the only reason we know of this conversation today is that Plato wrote it down. The most powerful objection ever raised against writing has reached us in writing. Socrates wrote nothing; we know him only because he had a student who did.

This irony is not a contradiction but a diagnosis. Because the word Theuth uses is itself a trap. In Greek he says "pharmakon" — a word that means both remedy and poison. The same word, depending on the dose, says two things at once. The French thinker Jacques Derrida, in a famous text written in nineteen sixty-eight, dwells on precisely this word: in describing writing, Plato seems, without quite realizing it, to have stated the common law of all technologies. No tool brings benefit alone. What it brings and what it takes away are two faces of the same mechanism.

There's an easy misunderstanding here; let's clear it up at the outset. What Thamus is saying is not "writing is bad." The king is not banning writing, he is pointing to an accounting error. Theuth counts what his invention will win us, but not what it will cost us. And cost us it did. In oral cultures there were people who carried epics thousands of lines long entirely by heart; the poems of Homer lived for centuries without ever being written down, circulating only among trained memories. That muscle of memory slackened with the coming of writing. But that same writing made possible thought too large for any single head to hold; it set mathematics, law, and history on ground solid enough to build upon. What was lost was a real loss, what was gained was a real gain, and the two were not exchangeable.

Here is the heart of the matter: this trade has no contract. Nobody ever sat down and signed a document saying, "I hereby surrender part of my memory in return for the reach of my thought." The change happened through use, unnoticed, completed within a few generations. And by the time it was complete, there was no one left who remembered the earlier state. How the mind worked before writing is something we can only guess at today, indirectly, from anthropological traces. The loss took place without even announcing that it was a loss.

This is the most insidious aspect of the relationship a tool builds with a human being. A tool extends a capacity. But at the same moment it lays off the organ that carried that capacity. And in the early days, the name for being laid off is relief. Atrophy and relief are, at the beginning, indistinguishable from one another; both give the same feeling of lightness. The difference only emerges when the tool is taken out of your hands. On that day, you see what you have left.

Thamus's question is still waiting to be answered twenty-five hundred years later, because every new tool asks it again. And the question is not "is this tool good or bad?" The question is this: am I using this tool, or is it, without my noticing, turning me into a human being that suits it?

To pursue that question, we first have to notice something strange: tools are invisible for as long as they work.

Martin Heidegger, the German philosopher, explained this with a very simple example in his book Being and Time, published in nineteen twenty-seven: a hammer. When a carpenter is working with a hammer, he does not think about the hammer. He doesn't notice its weight, the grain of its handle, the coldness of the metal. His mind isn't even on the nail; it's on the cabinet as a whole, on the job he has to finish by evening, perhaps on something else entirely. The hammer has all but dissolved into his hand. Heidegger calls this readiness-to-hand. Precisely when a tool is working, it withdraws from consciousness; it becomes transparent.

So when does the hammer become visible? When the handle cracks. When the head flies off. Or when you can't find it at the very moment you need it. Breakdown brings the tool suddenly before your eyes; what was until then an extension of your hand turns all at once into an object standing opposite you. In other words, the moment we truly see a tool is not the moment we use it, but the moment we can't. That strange emptiness you feel when your phone's battery dies is exactly this: something that has melted into your hand and gone invisible over the years becomes visible again by breaking.

This transparency is a tool's greatest gift and its greatest danger. Because you cannot notice how something invisible is shaping you.

Years later, in a lecture given in nineteen fifty-three and published the following year, Heidegger reckons with technology itself. His claim here is far more unsettling. Technology, he says, is not merely a matter of instruments; it is a way of seeing. We begin to look at the world through a particular eye. He takes the Rhine as his example. For centuries the Rhine was a river poets wrote about, a river with cities built on its banks and bridges thrown across it. Once a hydroelectric plant is built on it, the river is no longer a landscape upon which the plant stands; it becomes a source from which the plant is fed. The river is redefined as a reserve of pressurized water. Heidegger calls this standing-reserve: everything reduced to stock, piled up and waiting to be used.

And this way of looking does not stop at the river. The eye that sees timber when it looks at a forest, ore when it looks at a mountain, yield when it looks at soil, sooner or later turns the same eye on human beings. Think about how natural the phrase human resources sounds. Inside that phrase lies the assumption that a person, too, is stock that can be stacked and stored — and nobody finds this strange anymore. This was Heidegger's darkest warning: in technology, the real danger is not the machines, but the way of seeing the machines teach us. Because that way of seeing makes us forget that any other way of seeing is possible.

Around the same period, in nineteen sixty-six, the American psychologist Abraham Maslow put his finger on the same point in a far plainer sentence: if the only tool you have is a hammer, it's very tempting to treat everything you meet as a nail. This line is usually passed along as a joke, but there is a serious claim inside it. A tool doesn't merely solve the problem; it also determines what counts as a problem in the first place. The man holding a hammer sees nails in the world. The manager holding a spreadsheet sees everything unmeasurable as unimportant. An institution holding an algorithm doesn't see the person who won't turn into a number.

In those same years the Canadian communication theorist Marshall McLuhan sharpened the matter to its finest edge: the medium is the message. On first hearing, the sentence sounds like wordplay. But what McLuhan meant was this: the content a medium carries is, most of the time, a bright lure that distracts us. The real effect comes from the way the medium itself changes the scale and rhythm of life. He takes the railway as his example. The railway did not bring humanity movement, or the wheel; both already existed. What the railway brought was entirely new cities, a new order of work, a new idea of leisure, clocks set to the same moment across a whole country. What was carried on the train is almost trivial next to this new world built by the manner of carrying.

Translate that into today. What you watch on the screen is secondary to how the screen itself divides up your day. While we argue about the content, we have already accepted the form. The whole quarrel turns on what kind of nail it is; nobody questions that the hammer is in our hand.

Eastern thought asked this question far earlier, and in a far calmer voice.

In the book attributed to Zhuangzi, a philosopher who lived in China during the Warring States period — known in older sources as Chuang Tzu — there is a small scene. Zigong, one of Confucius's students, is returning from a long journey in the south when he passes by a garden. An old man is watering his plot. But he is watering it in a strange way: each time, he climbs down the steps into the well, fills a heavy jar by hand, hauls it up on his back, empties the water into the channel, then climbs down again. All that effort, so little water.

Zigong can't hold back. Sir, he says, there is a device that can water a hundred fields in a single day. Little effort, much work done. Wouldn't you like one? What he's describing is a lever rigged at the wellhead, a weight at one end and a bucket at the other; light at the back, heavy at the front, carrying the water upward almost of its own accord.

The old man straightens up, smiles, and says: I heard this from my master. A man who owns a machine comes to see his work as machine-like. A man who sees his work as machine-like grows a machine-like heart in his breast. And in a man who carries a machine heart in his breast, purity and wholeness break down; his spirit can find no rest, and he becomes restless. It isn't that I don't know about that contraption. I don't use it because I would be ashamed to use it.

There is something harsh, almost unfair, about this story; but then, Zhuangzi's text has always been like that, written to provoke an argument. Yet the idea inside it is one of the most precise discoveries in the history of thought: the machine heart. The old man's objection isn't to the contraption. It's to the mark the contraption leaves on the breast. Once efficiency becomes a habit, a person begins to look not only at the field but at everything through the eyes of efficiency. A conversation turns into "a waste of time," a walk is judged "unproductive," a friendship gets weighed by "what it brings in." The tool leaves your hand; the measure stays in your head.

But it would be a serious mistake to think that the East was against machines; that is reading only half the story. One of the most brilliant engineers of Islamic civilization, Ismail al-Jazari of Cizre, in the book he completed at the Artuqid court in twelve oh six, set down dozens of mechanisms — water clocks, automatic handwashing devices, self-moving musician figures, float-operated valves — with drawings, with measurements, explaining step by step how each one was to be built. A human being can take that much delight in machines. The same civilization could take Zhuangzi's question seriously and still love gears; because the question was never against the machine, it was about the direction the machine pulls the human being.

Ibn Khaldun, in the Muqaddimah, completed in thirteen seventy-seven, approaches the matter from an entirely different place and says something that still startles us today. Crafts, he writes, settle into the human soul as a faculty. That is: a craft doesn't only leave calluses on the hands; it leaves a disposition in the mind, a point of view, a way of thinking. The reasoning of a man who knows carpentry carries the trace of carpentry. Ibn Khaldun describes this not as a loss but as a gain: a craft nourishes a person, multiplies his intelligence, gives him a new way of seeing.

And immediately after, he sees the other face of it as well. Once affluence and comfort take hold, people's capacity to do their own work for themselves grows weak; a life that leans on the backs of others, or on the system, wears down one's resilience. This sits at the very center of Ibn Khaldun's analysis of civilization: comfort is a gain, but it isn't free.

Put these three voices side by side. Zhuangzi's old man says the tool changes the heart. Al-Jazari shows that building the tool is, in itself, a pleasure and a form of knowledge. And Ibn Khaldun writes the sentence that unites the two: the tool leaves a faculty in the soul. What you use, even if it doesn't use you, leaves a disposition in you. So the question isn't "should I use the tool." The question is this: which faculty does this tool grow in me, and which one does it make unnecessary? Because it does both at once, and it only shows you one of them.

Now look in your pocket.

The thing sitting there is a tool without equal in human history. With it you can see the face of someone on the other side of the world. You don't get lost on a street you've never walked. Every question that crosses your mind finds an answer within seconds. These are not small things; to a person living a hundred years ago, every one of them would have counted as a miracle — and everything we complain about today is written on the bill for those miracles.

Here is the bill. In a study published in twenty eleven by the American psychologist Betsy Sparrow and her colleagues, people were given a series of facts. One group was told the information would be saved on a computer and they could reach it whenever they liked. The other group was told the records would be deleted. The result was clear: those who knew the information would be stored were markedly worse at remembering it. But there was one thing they remembered very well — which folder the information was in. The mind, in other words, had memorized the address, not the content.

Daniel Wegner, one of that study's co-authors, had proposed a concept for this years earlier: transactive memory. Human beings have always entrusted their memories to one another. In long marriages, one spouse remembers the dates and the other the names; in teams, who knows what is shared like a common map. Nobody calls that memory loss. But something new and different is happening here: the partner across from us is not a person. It doesn't tire, doesn't forget, has no limit — and most importantly, it doesn't ask us to remember anything in return. When a partnership becomes one-sided, it stops being a partnership.

The same is true of our sense of direction. A study by the neuroscientist Eleanor Maguire, published in the year two thousand, showed that in the brains of London taxi drivers the rear portion of the hippocampus was larger than in a control group. These drivers had passed a punishing examination that required them to memorise tens of thousands of streets and connections, and those years of study had etched themselves into the physical structure of their brains. The city had grown inside their heads. Imaging studies carried out in later years revealed something else: when people follow turn-by-turn navigation, that region of the brain goes almost entirely quiet. If you are not building the city yourself, no city grows inside your head.

And then there is the quietest clause of the contract. In the middle of the twentieth century, the American psychologist Burrhus Frederic Skinner discovered something in his laboratory: the reward that most powerfully reinforces a behaviour is not the one that arrives every time. It is the one that arrives sometimes, at moments you cannot predict. Uncertainty hardens a behaviour into something close to addiction. Every flick of your finger pulling a screen downwards today is the pull of a lever; sometimes nothing comes, and sometimes it is exactly what you were looking for. This is no accident. As the former design ethicist Tristan Harris has been explaining for years, these mechanisms were built not to capture our attention but to extract it — the way ore is torn out of a seam in mining. What Heidegger called "standing reserve" has finally come to take up residence in our waking hours.

The American critic Neil Postman opens his book, written in nineteen ninety-two, with that very scene from ancient Egypt, and he says this: every technology is a Faustian bargain; it gives and it takes at the same time. So the right question is not "what will we gain" but "what are we losing in the very same moment". Yet Postman's real warning is subtler still. The bargain is not struck on equal terms for every part of society. The winner and the loser are, more often than not, not the same person.

The picture that emerges from all of this is not a picture of catastrophe. Nobody is deceiving anybody, nobody is going to throw their phone in the bin, and nobody needs to. Thamus did not ban writing, Zhuangzi did not smash the well mechanism, Ibn Khaldun did not condemn the craft. Not one of them says "put the tool down". There is only one thing all of them are saying: do not sign the contract without reading it.

But how does a person actually do that? Perhaps by a single measure. You cannot tell whether a tool has taken hold of you while you are using it, because in use the tool is invisible; it has melted into your hand. You can only tell once you set it down. What you feel on an afternoon when you have left your phone on the table, on a journey you set out on without navigation, on a page where you have had to build your own sentence yourself — that is what tells you the truth. The discomfort is normal; habit always resists. What matters is what comes after the discomfort. Does the faculty return after a while? Or is there a hollow where it used to be?

Because the answer to the question — are you using the tool, or is it using you — is not in the tool. The answer is in what remains when you take your hand away.
