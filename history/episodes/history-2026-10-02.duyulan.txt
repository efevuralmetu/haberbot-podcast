The shogun's army could not get into a single castle, inside there were neither soldiers nor walls, only peasants about to starve to death.  And those castle walls were shelled by the Dutch, who wanted to trade with Japan.  The door of a country that would stay shut for 200 years slammed there, that winter.  In the southwest of Japan, there is a peninsula that hangs down from the coast of the island of Kyushu toward the Ariake Sea, built on the slopes of a volcanic mountain.  Its name is Shimabara, its soil is fertile but it is not wide enough.  The wind that blows off the sea is salty, the mountain is called Unzen, and near its summit there are pits where scalding water gushes up out of the ground as if boiling.  At the beginning of the 17th century, this peninsula was one of the most Christianized corners of Japan.  The lords of the peninsula, the Arima family, had been baptized, had opened their doors to the missionaries, and churches and seminaries had been established in the villages.  By some estimates, the greater part of the peninsula's population had embraced the faith brought by the Portuguese Jesuits.  Then everything was turned on its head.  In 1614, the shogunate banned Christianity across the whole country, sent the missionaries into exile, and had the churches torn down.  In 1616, the Arima family was removed from the peninsula and transferred to another province.  The man who came in their place was Matsukura Shigemasa.  The territory that fell into Shigemasa's hands was, on paper, a domain of 60,000 koku, that is, a place capable of producing 60,000 measures of rice a year.  For a samurai house, this was a middling income.  Shigemasa, however, had no wish to be a middling man.  In 1618, on the flat ground along the seashore, he began building a castle that far exceeded the means of his own domain.  It took seven years.  When it was finished, the circuit of the walls reached 3,900 meters, the walls and places rose 15 meters high, moats between 30 and 50 meters wide, encircled the castle.  Dot and 16 towers had been set at the key points.  The five-story main keep was visible from every part of the peninsula.  According to the reckonings of the time, the cost of this castle was twice what the domain could bear.  The difference was taken out of the peasants.  Tax rates were raised so high that they bore no relation at all to the domain's real productive capacity.  New items were added on top of the rice tax.  A hearth tax, a window tax, a tax for every child born, a burial tax for every person who died.  For those who could not pay, the penalty was not a fine.  According to the records of the period, a peasant unable to pay his taxes would be dressed in a straw raincoat, his arms bound behind him, and the raincoat set alight.  This torture was given the name the Straw Raincoat Dance after the movements of a man writhing in the flames.  The wives and daughters of those who could not pay were hung upside down and plunged into icy water in the middle of winter.  Taxation and religious persecution were joined in the same hand.  From 1627 onward, the Matsukura family began throwing those who refused to renounce Christianity into the boiling waters of Unzen.  Those who were caught would be taken up the mountain, held for days in the stench of sulfur, and brought one by one to the edge of the pit to make them give it up.  Some were crucified, some were burned alive.  Nor was Shigemasa content with that.  Believing that the source of Christianity had to be dried up, he drew up a plan for an expedition against the island of Luzon in Spanish hands.  That is, that the north of what is today the Philippines, and the cost of this expedition, too, he demanded from his peasants.  The expedition never took place.  Shigemasa died in 1630, in a hot spring town.  He was succeeded by his son, Matsukurakatsui.  He did not change a single line of his father's policy.  Meanwhile, in Nagasaki, to the north of the peninsula, a new practice had begun.  From 1629 onward, the people of the city were ordered once a year to step on a medallion or a plaque placed before them.  On the plaque was an image of the Virgin Mary or of Jesus.  This was called the trampled picture.  Merchant, monk, samurai, woman, child, they would line up, lift a foot before the eyes of an official, step on the image, and pass on.  Whoever stepped was cleared, whoever hesitated was written down on a list.  This method, which set out to measure a human being's inner world by the movement of a foot, was the beginning of a system of screening that would last in Japan for more than 200 years.  Then came the famine.  A run of bad harvests in the middle of the 1630s drove both the Shamabara Peninsula and the Amakusa Islands on the opposite shore into starvation.  The peasant's granary was empty, his seed grain eaten, and the tax collector was at the door.  Requests for tax relief were turned down.  The domain's collectors went on making their rounds from village to village.  On the 17th of December in the year 1637, in the village of Arima in the south of the peninsula, the district tax collector Hayashi Hiyozemon found himself surrounded by peasants and was killed.  This did not remain merely the news of a murder.  Word spread across the peninsula within a day, and the next day the neighboring villages rose up as well.  The peasants who brought out the spears, axes and sickles they had hidden along the coast were joined by former warriors who had lost their masters 30 years earlier and had lived by farming ever since.  The uprising began as a tax revolt.  Within a week, it had turned into something else.  The person who took charge of the crowd was 16 years old.  His name was Masuda Shiro Tokisada, but history remembers him as Amakusa Shiro.  His father, Masuda Jinbei, had once served a Christian lord, after that lord was destroyed, he was left a warrior without land, and he raised his son on the Amakusa islands, in a world shaped by missionaries.  The boy was baptized with the name Jerome.  He could read and write, he spoke well, and in the first days of the uprising he became both the rebel's council of command and their symbol.  It was not talent alone that carried him to that position.  For years a prophecy had passed from mouth to mouth across the peninsula.  One of the exiled missionaries, it was said, had foretold that 25 years after the persecution began, a youth would rise in the land, that he would be 16 years old, and that he would gather the people.  The arithmetic fitted.  To a population worn down by hunger and torture, this boy was more than a commander.  By some accounts, stories circulated around him that miracles came from his hands.  Whether or not they were true hardly mattered to the course of events.  The people believed, and that belief was a stubbornness sufficient to hold out for three months against an army of 120,000.  The rebels' first move was to march on two castles.  At Shimabara they laid siege to the five-story castle raised with the blood and labor of the peasants.  They could not take it.  There was no way to capture a structure with walls 15 meters high and moats 50 meters wide using farm tools and a handful of muskets.  On the opposite shore they besieged Tomioka Castle on the Amakusa Islands, at one point they were almost inside, and then reinforcements from the domain's forces arrived and they were forced to fall back.  Not a single castle had been won.  Meanwhile the shogunate's order to the domains of Kyushu, raise troops at once and crush the rising, had long since gone out.  And it was at that point that the rebels made one of the most obstinate defensive decisions in history.  Instead of looking for a castle they could take, they chose to shut themselves inside a castle nobody wanted.  On the southern coast of the peninsula, on a hill looking out to sea, stood an abandoned ruin.  Its name was Hara Castle.  It had been founded in 1496 by Arima Takazumi of the Arima family, and at the end of the 16th century it was rebuilt from top to bottom by Arima Harunobu.  In that work Harunobu drew on the castle architecture he had seen during the Korean campaigns, ringing the inner keep with stone walls.  Jesuit records confirm that the stone ramparts of the main inner keep were raised between 1599 and 1604.  But when the Arima family left the region in 1616, the castle was emptied, the roofs caved in, the timber fittings were stripped away, grass grew up between the walls.  For 20 years it was a heap of stone no one came near.  The rebels poured into it.  The villagers of the Shimabara peninsula, and those who crossed by boat from the Amakusa islands, climbed the hill, first with their families, then with their livestock, and finally with everything they could carry.  All together, counting women, the old and the children, there were some 37,000 of them.  Of these, perhaps a third could bear arms.  Within three weeks they turned that ruin into a fortress.  They shored up the collapsed walls with earth and with planks torn from village houses, they cleared the moats, they drove palisades into the paths leading up the hill.  The sheer face above the sea they left as a natural rampart.  Counted the rice they had brought inside and put it on ration.  This was the most critical calculation of the siege, and though no one said it aloud, the sum was bad, for 37,000 people, the stores were measured in months.  They also made a banner.  The man who painted it was named Yamada Imosaku, an artist who had learned painting from European missionaries and knew how to prepare pigments.  At the center of the standard he hung on the castle walls he set a chalice and two angels, and above them he wrote a sentence in Portuguese, praised be the most holy sacrament.  That banner is today one of the most striking objects preserved in Japan to have survived the siege.  And the story of the man who painted it would arrive at an unexpected place in the castle's fall.  On the shogunate side, the matter was being taken seriously.  The government in Ito ordered every domain in Kyushu to put troops in the field and placed an official named Itakura Shigemasa in charge of suppressing the rising.  Towards the end of January, the whole stretch of land on the castle's landward side was covered with army camps.  The siege formally began on the 22nd of January 1638.  Itakura Shigemasa underestimated what stood in front of him.  He asked himself how many days a crowd of mostly farmers crammed into a ruined castle could last against regular forces gathered from some of the greatest domains in Japan.  And he reckoned the answer at, not many. On the 3rd of February he launched the first assault.  The plan failed.  The rebels had narrowed the castle's few points of access, arranging their defenses so that an attacker could advance only along a narrow front.  The forces climbing the hill were met with stones, logs and boiling water hurled from above.  Among the defenders, musketeers opened fire at close range.  The first assault was broken.  Itakura tried again on the 14th of February.  The combined toll of the two attacks was more than 600 men lost, and not a single breach opened in the walls.  In the course of the second assault, the commander himself was shot, and died.  The first supreme commander the shogunate had sent to the peninsula had lost his life before a peasant uprising, on the slope of a ruin.  Ito's response to this news was a change of method.  Matsudaira Nobutsuna was appointed in Itakura's place.  The new commander did the exact opposite of his predecessor, he stopped attacking.  He had ditches dug around the castle, breastworks razed, watchtowers erected, and mine tunnels driven beneath the walls.  With soldiers flowing in from the surrounding domains, the strength of the camp passed 125,000.  He lined boats along the seaward side of the castle.  Now he had only one aim, that nothing at all should get inside.  He also asked for cannon.  Japan had cannon, but what Nobutsuna wanted was firepower heavy enough to pound a hilltop, and the nearest source of that was the Dutch trading post on the island of Hirado, north of the peninsula.  The Dutch wanted to strengthen their position against the Portuguese, the only other Europeans permitted to trade in Japan, refusing a request from the shogunate was simply unthinkable.  Nicolais Cucubacar, the man heading the Dutch East India Company at Hirado, first sent cannon and powder ashore, these were formed into a battery on the coast.  Then the shogunate asked for a ship.  Cucubacar personally took the 20-gun sailing ship named the Ripe, the Ripe, for short, out to the waters off Hera Castle.  The ship took up position on the 24th of February and bombarded the castle for 15 days, until the 12th of March.  In that time it fired roughly 426 rounds.  The result was very nearly nothing.  The castle stood atop a steep hill overlooking the sea, and most of the shot fired from the ship's deck sailed over the target and fell behind the hill, into the camp of the very Japanese army conducting the siege.  No serious breach was opened in the walls.  The rebels for their part, returned fire at the ship, two Dutchmen standing watch at the Ripe's lookout mast were shot and killed.  But the greatest damage the Ripe did to the siege came not from the shot it fired, but from its mere presence.  The rebels lowered mocking messages from the walls to the besieging army.  The gist of them was this, are there no brave men left in this country to fight us, that you must call foreigners to your aid?  For an army of 125,000 samurai that had come to put down a rebellion, this was a question that touched the tenderest spot of all.  The Japanese commanders asked the Dutch to withdraw.  The Ripe weighed anchor and left.  For the remainder of the siege, no foreign gun fired on the castle walls again.  Once the foreign ship was gone, what remained was Nobutsuna's real weapon.  That weapon was the siege itself, and it was working in silence.  Through the month of March, nothing much happened on the walls of Hera Castle.  What was happening was inside it.  37,000 people, on a hilltop sealed off from every direction but the sea, were living on rations they had calculated back in January.  In the middle of March the rice ran out.  Then the animals left to slaughter ran out.  Then the grasses on the hill were pulled up, and the bark was stripped from the trees.  In the end they began climbing down the steep face of the castle to gather seaweed from the rocks the tide had left bare.  The besiegers could see this, they watched the people coming down from a distance, and they knew what they were collecting.  At the beginning of April, the rebels attempted a night sortie.  A detachment came out of the castle and raided the siege lines.  The aim was both to seize provisions and to disrupt the siege works.  The raid was broken, and a large part of those who came out died at the lines.  And it was over those dead that the coldest scene of the whole siege took place.  Matsudaira Nobutsuna ordered the bellies of the corpses opened.  The point was not revenge, but intelligence.  The commander wanted to know how far the starvation inside the castle had gone, and the surest way to learn that was to look inside the enemy's stomach.  In the stomachs they opened there was no rice, no meat, no grain.  There was only seaweed, grass and tree bark.  That single finding answered every question Nobutsuna had.  The castle's provisions were exhausted, inside the number of people with any strength left to fight was falling by the day.  Preparations for the final assault began.  Before the preparations were complete, one more name entered the story.  Yamada Imosaku, the painter who had drawn the castle's banner, made contact with the besieging army.  He informed the besiegers of the situation inside the castle, which point was weak, and how the defense was coming apart.  When the rebels realized this, they seized him to be put to death.  The man was awaiting his execution as the castle fell.  On the 12th of April, 1638, the final assault began.  Even its beginning was unplanned.  A fire that broke out along the siege line, or a signal misread, dragged one domain's forces up the slope before their time, and when the others refused to be left behind.  Raline after line marched for the hill.  What emerged was not the orderly assault Nobutsuna had designed, but a scattered entangled rush.  At this time though, what faced them was a defense that had been living on seaweed for months.  The outer lines of defense fell on the first day.  Inside, they fought for three days.  Pressed in among the walls, using the last of their musket powder and the last of their stones, they resisted street by street.  The forces that broke into the castle found, alongside the fighters, women, the old and children.  The sources do not describe those three days in detail, the numbers describe them.  The siege army's losses across the whole siege, dead and wounded together come to around 20,000.  Of the 37,000 people inside the castle, almost no one survived the battle and the executions that followed it.  Amakusa Shiro was killed during the assault.  His head was cut off, carried to Nagasaki and displayed to the public on the point of a spear.  The severed head of the 16-year-old boy, who had become the symbol of the uprising was left at the entrance to the city for days, like a warning sign.  The castle fell completely on the 15th of April, 1638.  The stone walls were pulled down and the rubble was tipped into the ditches, leaving the site on the peninsula unusable ever again.  The only person recorded as surviving the siege of Hera Castle was the painter Yamada Imosaku, who had drawn the castle's banner and then passed its secrets to the outside.  The shogunate pardoned him.  The flag he had painted was recovered from the rubble and preserved.  Once the castle had fallen, it was the turn of the side that had provoked the revolt to answer for it.  Matsukura Katsuie had not been on the peninsula during the uprising, as the shogunate's system required, he was in Edo.  After the siege ended, the state of his domain was investigated, and the picture that emerged appalled the shogunate.  In May of 1638, his domain was stripped from him, during the investigation.  The discovery of a dead peasant inside his own residence was enough to document what had been said about his methods of tax collection.  Katsuie was summoned to Edo, tried, and on the 28th of August 1638 beheaded.  This punishment has no equal in Japan's recent history.  Across roughly three centuries of the Edo period there were many lords who fell from favor, lost their domains, were exiled, but a lord being put to death by beheading happened only once.  A samurai, even for the gravest crime, was granted the right to cut open his own belly, that right was the last door left open to a nobleman's honor.  For Katsuie, that door did not open.  In this way the shogunate was declaring something that a lord crushing his people hard enough to drive his own domain into revolt, was in the eyes of the ruling house, not an error, but a crime.  Shimabara was given to a new lord, Koriki Tadafusa, and the tax rates were brought down to a reasonable level.  But there was no one left on the peninsula to pay the tax.  Most of the population had died at Hera Castle, the villages were empty, the fields unplowed.  The shogunate resettled the peninsula by moving families to Shimabara from other parts of Japan.  What mattered in choosing the newcomers was plain, they would come from regions that were not Christian, because this was the real conclusion Edo had drawn from the uprising.  The shogunate read the people who had held out for three months on the walls of Hera Castle, feeding on seaweed, not as a tax revolt but as a problem of loyalty created by a foreign faith.  It had seen that, as long as that faith remained in the country, its own subjects could hold out this long against its own army.  And to remove that possibility, it closed the doors.  In 1639, Portuguese merchants were banned from entering Japan altogether.  The crew of one Portuguese delegation that tried to come despite the ban was executed.  In 1641, the Dutch, the only Europeans still permitted to remain in Japan, were taken from their settlement at Harado and moved to an artificial, fan-shaped island built in the middle of Nagasaki harbor.  The island was called Digima, and it was about the size of a football pitch.  For 200 years all of Japan's contact with the west passed across the bridge of that small island.  The annual ceremony of treading on the image, practiced in Nagasaki, turned after the uprising from a local check into a nationwide routine.  In the villages of Kyushu an official would come every year, lay the plaque on the ground, and have everyone, household by household, step on it in turn.  Even so, the faith did not disappear.  In these regions of Japan, communities left without priests, without churches, without books, passed their prayers down from memory, generation to generation, and lived in hiding for more than 200 years.  When missionaries came to Nagasaki again in the middle of the 19th century, villagers stepped forward to introduce themselves.  The Shimabara rebellion had one more consequence, and no one had planned it.  The fall of Hara Castle turned out to be the last major armed conflict Japan would see for 230 years.  From 1638 until the Civil War of 1868, no war on that scale occurred again.  Japan's famous Long Peace, in truth, begins on the slope of a ruin, with three days in which 37,000 people died.  As for the doors that closed, they were forced open in 1853, when American warships dropped anchor in Edo Bay.  Today there is no castle on that hill.  Because its rubble was dumped into the moats, the site was used as farmland for centuries.  In 1938 the area was declared a National Historic Site, and then the excavations began.  What came out of the ground gave a more tangible account of the siege than the written records did.  Musket balls, fragments of cannon shot, broken cooking pots.  Skeletons buried at the foot of the walls.  And among those skeletons, medallions, crosses, rosaries.  In an excavation in the year 2000, the remains of one of the largest castle gates in Japan were uncovered.  In 2018 the site was added to the World Heritage List, along with other places belonging to the hidden Christian heritage of the Nagasaki region.  Anyone who climbs that hill looking out over the Ariake Sea today sees only grass, a line of stones, and the horizon.  But to know that Japan's decision to close itself off from the world for 200 years was set in motion from this silent slope, changes the way you see the view.
