Your grandfather says, "In our day, people were more honest." Meanwhile you carry in your pocket a world he could never have imagined. Which of you is right: is humanity really moving forward, or is it only speeding up and mistaking speed for progress?

Picture a family table. It's a holiday afternoon, the tea has steeped, and Grandpa leans back and begins: In our day, people greeted one another. You never walked past a neighbor's door without knocking. Bread tasted like bread, and tomatoes smelled like tomatoes. At the other end of the table, a grandchild is looking at their phone, perhaps at that very moment texting a friend: Oh, we had the cartoons of our childhood, and nothing today even comes close. Two generations at the same table, two separate golden ages. One looks back sixty years, the other fifteen. And both believe it sincerely: things used to be better.

This feeling is not our invention. Some two thousand seven hundred years ago, the Greek poet Hesiod told the history of humankind through metals in his long poem, Works and Days. First came the golden race: people who lived like gods, who never tired, never aged, and met death as though drifting off to sleep. Then came the silver race, then the bronze, then the heroes. And last of all, the iron race, the poet's own generation. Hesiod laments that he wishes he had not been born into this age, that he had either died before it or been born after. Weariness by day, sorrow by night. Children are nothing like their fathers, and guests show no respect to their hosts. Sound familiar?

A few centuries later, the Roman poet Horace descends the same staircase. In one of his odes he writes: Our fathers' age, worse than our grandfathers', bore us, more wicked still, and we shall soon bring forth a generation even more depraved. Just think: if every generation since Horace had been worse than the one before, what kind of place would we be living in today? The math doesn't add up. Which means this complaint holds something that says more about people than about the world.

Interestingly, this feeling was once given a name as an illness. In sixteen eighty-eight, Johannes Hofer, completing his medical studies in Basel, examined a strange affliction among Swiss mercenaries fighting far from home. The soldiers lost their appetite, lost their sleep, and sometimes wasted away to the point of mortal danger. Hofer named the condition by combining the Greek words for homecoming and pain: nostalgia. So nostalgia began as a longing for a place. Over time the place faded away, and what remained was time. Now we long not for our village, but for our youth.

So why does the past always seem brighter? Psychologists attribute part of it to the way memory works. Over time, the mind files down painful details and polishes the good moments. Someone recalling their military service remembers not the cold on night watch, but the laughter in the barracks. And there is another reason: most of us remember the old days alongside ages when we carried no responsibility. Perhaps that tomato smelled better simply because we were ten years old when we smelled it. We weren't the ones paying the bills or worrying about illness; the world seemed flawless to us because keeping it flawless wasn't our job.

But we need to be careful here. Dismissing every nostalgia as an illusion would be the easy way out. Some things really are lost. A neighborhood is torn down, a language is forgotten, a craft is buried along with its last master. Grandpa's point about greetings may not be entirely imaginary either. The real question is this: when the losses and gains are tallied up, is the story of humanity climbing upward, sliding downward, or tracing a circle and turning in place? This is precisely the question around which the philosophy of progress is born. And that question takes on its sharpest form at a moment when a human life is up for bargaining.

Paris, autumn of seventeen ninety-three. The Revolution has begun to devour its own children. The guillotine is at work almost every day. On the Rue Servandoni, in the house of a widow named Madame Vernet, a man is hiding. His name is Nicolas de Condorcet. A mathematician, permanent secretary of the Academy of Sciences, an opponent of slavery, and one of the rare voices defending women's right to vote. He had applauded the Revolution and taken a seat in the assembly, but because he criticized the Jacobins' draft constitution, a warrant had been issued for his arrest. Every knock at the door must have made his heart leap into his throat.

What would you expect such a man to write, in days when death waited just one street away? A last will, perhaps a cry of rage, perhaps a bitter reckoning with how dark humanity can be. Condorcet does none of these things. He sits down and writes his Sketch for a Historical Picture of the Progress of the Human Mind. He divides human history into ten epochs. He begins with hunter-gatherer tribes and moves on to agriculture, writing, Greek philosophy, the printing press, and the scientific revolution. The tenth epoch, however, is the one not yet lived: the future. And that future, for a man looking out from the room where he lay in hiding, is astonishingly bright. Condorcet foresees that inequality between nations will diminish, that inequality within each nation will recede as well, and that humanity can truly perfect itself, both in reason and in morals. He writes that the inequality of rights between women and men will be abolished, that education will reach everyone, and that with the advance of medicine the human lifespan will stretch so far that no one will be able to fix its limit with certainty. In his view, there is no end to humanity's perfection; only its pace changes, never its direction.

In March of seventeen ninety-four, Condorcet leaves his hiding place so as not to endanger the woman who had sheltered him. South of Paris, worn out by hunger and fatigue, he enters an inn. He is in disguise, but his hands are not the hands of a peasant, and, so the story goes, he carries a volume of Horace in his pocket. Arrested on suspicion, he is locked in a cell at Bourg-la-Reine. Two days later, he is found dead in that cell. Poison or exhaustion? Even today, no one knows for certain. His book is published after his death, in seventeen ninety-five. Perhaps the most optimistic text ever written about the progress of humanity came from the pen of a man who fell victim to a revolution founded in the name of progress. That irony sits at the very heart of our subject.

Yet some half a century before Condorcet, another French-speaking thinker had dealt this same faith its first serious blow. In the summer of seventeen forty-nine, Jean-Jacques Rousseau of Geneva was walking to Vincennes to visit his friend Diderot, who was imprisoned there, when he read in the journal he carried an announcement of a prize competition held by the Academy of Dijon. The question was this: Has the restoration of the sciences and the arts contributed to the purification of morals? Rousseau would later say that the moment he read those lines, he saw another world and became another man. He says he collapsed at the foot of a tree and wept.

Rousseau's answer defies all the optimism of his age: No. In his view, the sciences and the arts had done nothing but weave garlands of flowers over the chains around humanity's neck. As society grew more refined, people lost their sincerity, learned to appear other than they were, and surrendered themselves to display and comparison. With this objection, Rousseau won the prize in seventeen fifty and became famous overnight.

A few years later, in his second great essay, which questions the origins of inequality, he deepens this idea further still. And here he introduces a very subtle concept: perfectibility. What sets humans apart from animals is the capacity to change themselves, to improve themselves. But Rousseau does not present this capacity as something to praise. He says that this very same capacity is what tore humanity away from the peace of its natural state and drove it toward endless desires and envies. If perfectibility is a ladder, that ladder reaches just as far down as it does up.

When we set these two men side by side, the skeleton of a debate from our own everyday lives emerges before us. Today, with the phone in our pocket, we can reach all the libraries of the world; the spread of knowledge that Condorcet dreamed of has perhaps come true in the most striking way. But with that same phone, we also live out every day the very thing Rousseau feared: polishing our image in the eyes of others, constantly weighing ourselves against everyone else. Which of them was right? Perhaps the point is that both saw two faces of the same instrument. Knowledge advances; but the heart of the person who uses it is under no obligation to advance at the same pace.

Within a hundred years, this quarrel in Europe had moved to an entirely different part of the world. This time, the question was not simply whether progress was good or bad; it was the question of whose progress it was.

In the middle of the nineteenth century, a new word gains weight in the language of Ottoman intellectuals: terakki. Progress, ascent, development. With the Tanzimat reforms, this word becomes more than a wish; it turns almost into state policy. But progress shakes a person when it is seen with one's own eyes, not on paper. And some of the generation who first saw it with their own eyes saw it along the roads of exile.

Ziya Pasha, one of the leading figures among the intellectuals known as the Young Ottomans, lives in Europe from eighteen sixty-seven to eighteen seventy-two; he sees Paris, London, Geneva. A couplet he left behind is still recited from memory today: Diyar-ı küfrü gezdim, beldeler, kâşaneler gördüm; dolaştım mülk-i İslam'ı, bütün viraneler gördüm. That is: I traveled the lands of the unbelievers, and I saw cities and mansions; I wandered the realm of Islam, and everywhere I saw ruins.

These two lines lay open the wound of an era. Because in the minds of this generation there is another powerful understanding of history as well. In the Islamic world, the best age was placed not in the future but in the past: the Asr-ı Saadet, the Age of Bliss. The era in which the Prophet and his close companions lived, when faith and justice were thought to exist in their purest form. In this view, history resembles a river that grows murkier the farther it flows from its source. To move toward the good is not to rush forward, but to return to the source. Now imagine these two ideas colliding in a single mind. On one side, the streets of London lit by gas lamps, the railways, the factories, the libraries; on the other, the belief that the finest age lay thirteen centuries in the past. Ziya Pasha's couplet is the cry of exactly this predicament. If the best is behind us, then what is this progress before my eyes? And if this is progress, why is it not in my world?

Namık Kemal does not turn away from this question; he walks straight into it. In eighteen seventy-two, in an article titled Terakki, or Progress, published in the newspaper İbret, he all but takes his reader by the hand and leads them through London. He describes the schools, the exhibitions, the gardens, and the eagerness with which people pursue knowledge and art, with an enthusiasm that is almost the stuff of fairy tales. But Namık Kemal's purpose is not to admire Europe. He wants to show that this progress belongs to no single race or religion, that it is something any society can reach through labor, education, and liberty. He even goes a step further, arguing that nothing in the essence of Islam stands in the way of science and reason, and that decline springs not from faith but from governance and neglect.

At this point, we need to recall a far older voice from Eastern thought, because the question of history's direction had been pondered deeply in these lands centuries before the Ottoman intellectuals. In the fourteenth century, the North African thinker Ibn Khaldun examined the rise and fall of societies in the Muqaddimah, which he completed in thirteen seventy-seven. In his eyes, history neither climbs upward in a straight line nor sinks steadily downward; it traces a circle. Communities living in the harsh conditions of the desert are tightly bound to one another, and Ibn Khaldun calls this spirit of solidarity asabiyyah. With this strength, they conquer cities and found states. But the comfort, luxury, and ease of city life slowly loosen that bond. According to Ibn Khaldun, a dynasty usually completes this journey in three generations, roughly a hundred and twenty years: the generation that builds, the generation that sustains, and the generation that finds everything ready-made and squanders it.

Ibn Khaldun's model whispers something to us: nostalgia and the belief in progress may in fact be two sides of the same coin. Luxury and comfort, the very fruits of progress, also carry the seeds of decline. This is surprisingly close to Rousseau's concern; but Ibn Khaldun presents it not as a moral sermon, but as an observable social law.

The way out that the Ottoman intellectuals were searching for was a bridge between these two extremes. At the start of the twentieth century, Mehmet Akif captured this search in two lines of his poem From the Pulpit of the Süleymaniye: we must draw our inspiration directly from the Quran, and make Islam speak to the understanding of the age. In other words, returning to the source and keeping pace with the times are not enemies. It is possible to draw inspiration from the past and let it speak in a language today can understand. In Akif's vision, the Age of Bliss becomes not a destination to return to, but a compass.

This debate is not foreign to us. Even today, as we embrace new technologies and new ways of city life, we also speak of the values we have lost. That unease, swinging between Ziya Pasha's two lines and Namık Kemal's tour of London, perhaps lives on in someone walking the gleaming corridors of a shopping mall, longing for the sounds of the old bazaar. But there was something these hopeful or anxious debates of the nineteenth century did not yet know: the most brilliant instruments of progress would very soon be used in the darkest scenes of human history.

In nineteen twenty-one, a young German thinker, Walter Benjamin, buys a small picture by the painter Paul Klee. Its name is Angelus Novus, the New Angel. Drawn on paper with just a few lines, it shows a strange being, its eyes wide open in astonishment, its mouth agape, its wings spread. Benjamin carries this picture with him for years. In every home he moves into, he hangs it on the wall.

In nineteen forty, as Europe burns once again, Benjamin, of Jewish descent and fleeing the Nazis, writes one of his last texts: On the Concept of History. In the ninth section of this work, made up of short, numbered reflections, he returns to the New Angel. For Benjamin, this is the angel of history. Its face is turned toward the past. Where we perceive a chain of events, it sees one single catastrophe, which keeps piling wreckage upon wreckage and hurling it at its feet. The angel would like to stay, to awaken the dead, and to make whole what has been smashed. But a storm is blowing from Paradise; it has caught in the angel's wings with such force that the angel can no longer close them. The storm irresistibly propels it into the future, to which its back is turned, while the pile of debris before it grows skyward. And Benjamin writes the final sentence: this storm is what we call progress.

A few months after writing these lines, in September nineteen forty, in the small town of Portbou on the Spanish border, Benjamin takes his own life, fearing he would be turned back. Roughly a hundred and fifty years separate Condorcet's hopeful vision from Benjamin's angel; both men wrote as fugitives, both wrote in the shadows. One looked to the future and saw light, the other looked to the past and saw ruins.

So where are we at the end of this journey? Was everything really better in the old days? Some questions are easier to answer with numbers. By historical estimates, around eighteen hundred, more than forty percent of the children born in the world did not live to see their fifth birthday. Today that figure is around four percent. For a moment, think of this not as an abstract statistic, but as a home. Most of our great-great-grandmothers had buried at least one of the children they bore. That loss was a silence in those homes, never spoken of, yet known to everyone. In that old neighborhood Grandfather longs for, perhaps one house in every three held such a silence. Literacy, vaccines, the wiping of smallpox from the face of the earth: these show how much of Condorcet's dream came true.

But Benjamin's angel is right as well. The same century turned chemistry to the service of death camps, and physics to bombs that could erase whole cities in an instant. Knowledge advanced and tools grew ever more refined, but the question of who would use them, and to what end, was never solved in any laboratory. Rousseau's warning was borne out, two centuries later, in the harshest way imaginable: perfectibility could carry humanity in either direction.

Perhaps the question itself is framed the wrong way. When we ask whether everything was better in the old days, we act as if there were a single set of scales, yet the pans of those scales hold very different things. In some areas, such as health, knowledge and lifespan, progress is real and measurable. In others, such as meaning, solidarity and peace of mind, the picture is more complicated. As Ibn Khaldun warned, comfort can loosen the bonds between people; and as Akif suggested, the past may be not a place to return to, but a compass that helps us find our way.

Perhaps the most honest conclusion we can reach today is this: we are not better, we are more aware. Hesiod waited for the end of the race of iron; we know what that race of iron is, how it works, and at what cost we have arrived where we are. Our grandmothers did not know why their children died; we do, and that knowledge carries a responsibility. Ziya Pasha saw the ruins, yet was content merely to debate their cause; we have the entire history of that debate laid out before us.

This awareness gives nostalgia a new meaning, too. When Grandfather, at the holiday table, says that in his day people greeted one another, there is no need to pull out statistics in order to doubt him. We can hear his words not as a claim about history, but as a statement of values. What he is really saying is this: this matters, do not lose it. Nostalgia is not proof that the past was better; it is a map of what we hold dear. The cartoons the grandchild misses, and the greetings the grandfather misses, both join the list of things we do not want to lose.

Benjamin's angel looked backward because it did not trust a progress that ignored the wreckage. But having its face turned toward the past did not mean it wished to return there; it wanted to awaken the dead and make whole what had been smashed. Perhaps that is what we can do as well: we cannot stop the storm, but with our wings spread, we can choose not to close our eyes. We can learn to look at the past with both gratitude and criticism, and to both celebrate progress and question it.

In that small room, Condorcet chose to face the future with confidence; Rousseau, weeping at the foot of a tree, reckoned the price of civilization. Ibn Khaldun saw a circle, Namık Kemal a rising road, Benjamin a storm. None of them alone shows the whole picture. But someone who listens to all of them together can lean in, with a little more tenderness and a little more alert an ear, both to a grandfather's story and to a grandchild's complaint. Perhaps the golden age never existed. But longing for it reminds us of what we must protect, and of what we have yet to build.
