Is It Good If It Says Natural? The Magic of the Word Natural

Two products sit on the shelf at the same price, and one of them carries a single word: "natural." Your hand reaches for it without a second thought. But snake venom is natural too, and so is smallpox. So why do we trust this word so much?

Imagine you're walking down the aisles of a supermarket. In your hands are two bottles of fruit juice. They cost the same, and what's inside them is almost identical. But one label shows a green leaf and a single word in large letters: natural. Which one goes into your basket? Most of us decide without thinking. The hand drifts on its own toward the bottle with the leaf. Yet that word told us nothing at all. It didn't say what was in the bottle, how much sugar it held, or whether it would do us any good. Still, it made a promise. And that is exactly where the word's power lies.

To understand why this word is so powerful, let's first look at how vague it is. In the United States, the agency that regulates food has for years avoided giving the word "natural" any official definition. All it has is a loose policy it has followed for a long time: if nothing artificial or synthetic has been added to a product that you wouldn't normally expect to find in it, the word is tolerated. In other words, all the associations that spring to mind when someone reads the label, cleanliness, purity, health, trust, rest on no legal guarantee whatsoever. We add those associations ourselves.

Now consider this. Arsenic is natural; it's found in soil and in rock. Botulinum toxin, one of the most potent poisons known, is produced by a bacterium, not invented in a laboratory. The hemlock Socrates drank when he was executed in Athens came from a plant. Aflatoxin, released by molds that grow on peanuts and grains, ranks among the most powerful cancer-causing substances there are. Meanwhile, the insulin that keeps millions of people alive is today largely made in laboratories. Eyeglasses are not natural, vaccines are not natural, and neither is the sterile glove in the operating room. Yet none of us sees these things as hiding some evil inside.

So why does that word on the label still seem so innocent? The answer lies in several meanings stacked on top of one another within the word itself. "Natural" sometimes means "untouched by human hands," sometimes "ordinary, expected," and sometimes "the way things ought to be." In everyday speech we slip easily back and forth between these meanings. When we say "it's natural for a child to cry," we mean it's normal. When we say "natural yogurt," we mean it has no additives. But when we say "this relationship isn't natural," we're passing judgment, implying that something is wrong. The word's magic is hidden in that shift. A word that describes how something is quietly turns into a word that says how something ought to be.

Philosophy has long looked at this shift with suspicion. The Scottish philosopher David Hume, in A Treatise of Human Nature, which began appearing in seventeen thirty-nine, makes a striking observation about the moral writers of his day. For a while these writers would describe how things are, and then, without any warning to the reader, begin telling us how things ought to be. Sentences built on "is" suddenly became sentences built on "ought." To Hume, this transition was a gap crying out for explanation. How did one leap from fact to value? Later generations would remember this question as Hume's guillotine. Roughly two hundred years later, in nineteen oh-three, the English philosopher George Edward Moore gave a name to a similar error in his book Principia Ethica: the naturalistic fallacy. What Moore meant was something more technical. He argued that it was a mistake to try to define the concept of "good" in terms of some natural property, such as pleasure or evolutionary progress. Over time the name took on a broader meaning and became the general label for the idea that "what is natural is good."

But here we should pause and point out an injustice. Treating what is "in accordance with nature" as good is not a trick dreamed up by advertisers. Some of humanity's most deeply rooted moral traditions were built on precisely this idea. If we brush them aside without taking them seriously and simply declare that the word "natural" is empty, we'll never understand the hold the word has on people.

In the early third century before the common era, a merchant from Cyprus, Zeno of Citium, began teaching in a painted colonnade in Athens. The school took its name from that colonnade: the Stoa. According to ancient sources, Zeno summed up the purpose of human life in a single phrase: to live in agreement with nature. If we read those words in today's sense, we get them wrong. The Stoics were not telling people to return to the forest or abandon the city. To them, the universe was a whole ordered by reason. Chrysippus, regarded as the school's most prolific thinker, says that nature has two layers: the nature of the universe as a whole, and the nature of the human being. And human nature is reason. So living according to nature did not mean chasing after anger, fear and ambition. Quite the opposite: it meant passing them through the filter of reason. Centuries later, in Rome, Epictetus, a freed slave, gave this idea a form that sits very close to everyday life. Some things are within our power, and some are not. Whether it rains, whether someone insults us, illness, death: none of these are within our power. How we respond to them, however, is. From the Stoic point of view, living in accordance with nature means yielding to what we cannot change, and taking responsibility for what we can change, namely our own judgment. Here, "nature" does not describe a given state of things. It describes a discipline. The Stoic who lives in accordance with nature is someone who has learned to rein himself in.

At the other end of the world, in China, a similar search was unfolding in an entirely different language. The Tao Te Ching, attributed to Laozi, which means the Book of the Way and of Virtue, warns against the person who tries to rein in the universe with boundaries and rules. In the twenty-fifth chapter of the book, a ladder is set up: humanity takes the earth as its model, the earth takes heaven, and heaven takes the Way, that is, the Tao. The Tao, in turn, takes "ziran" as its model. Although dictionaries translate the word ziran as "nature," its real meaning is "that which is so of itself." The Taoist sage is the one who does not interfere with the natural flow of things, who acts without forcing. This is called "wu wei": acting without forcing.

The thinker who conveys this idea through the most vivid examples is Zhuangzi. In one of his stories, a famous horse trainer takes horses from their mountain pastures. He clips their manes, pares their hooves, brands them, bridles them, and shuts them away in stables. In the end, some of the horses cannot endure this training, and they die. Elsewhere, Zhuangzi reminds us that the duck's legs are short and the crane's are long. Try to lengthen the duck's legs or shorten the crane's, and you will bring suffering to both. Here again, "the natural" is not a given state of things. It is a warning that the proper measure of each being must not be distorted by artificial molds.

In Islamic thought, the answer to these questions is the concept of "fitra." The thirtieth verse of the Surah ar-Rum speaks of the fitra upon which God created human beings. A well-known hadith opens up the idea further: every child is born upon the fitra, and it is the parents who make the child a Jew, a Christian, or a Zoroastrian. Fitra is a fundamental disposition placed within the human being, an inner constitution inclined to turn toward truth and toward the good. Toward the end of the eleventh century, al-Ghazali recounts a memory from his youth in a work that is a kind of intellectual autobiography, al-Munqidh min al-Dalal, or Deliverance from Error. He writes that he noticed how the children of Christian families grew up Christian, the children of Jewish families grew up Jewish, and the children of Muslim families grew up Muslim. This observation, together with the hadith he remembered, led him to separate belief acquired through imitation from a person's true fitra. For al-Ghazali, fitra is not something about which we can say, "it is already within us, and therefore it is right." We can only bring it to light by scraping away the layers of habit that cover it.

Notice something here. These three traditions, the Stoa, the Tao, and the idea of fitra, come from very different places, yet they do something in common. None of them treats "the natural" as the same thing as whatever you happen to crave in the moment. For the Stoic, nature is reason. For the Taoist, it is flowing without distorting one's own measure. And for al-Ghazali, it is a starting point that has been covered over and must be rediscovered. In all three, nature serves as a compass, but reading that compass takes effort. The label on the supermarket shelf, by contrast, promises to let us skip that effort. These ancient traditions looked at nature with the conviction that within all of it lay a certain goodness and order. And the question an English philosopher would ask in the nineteenth century would take aim at precisely that conviction: what if that order is not as good as we believe?

In the eighteen-fifties, during the most productive years of his life, John Stuart Mill wrote a long essay. Its title was a single word: Nature. Mill did not publish this text during his lifetime. The essay appeared in eighteen seventy-four, one year after Mill's death in eighteen seventy-three, published as a book together with two other essays he had written on religion. The essay on Nature reads like an indictment aimed at the counsel to "live according to nature."

Mill begins by taking the concept apart. The word "nature," he says, can mean two things. The first is everything that exists: all events, all forces, and the human being included among them. In this sense, the counsel to "follow nature" says nothing at all, because no one can step outside of nature. There is no thief who breaks the laws of physics, no liar who defies gravity. Everyone already conforms to nature. The second meaning of the word is whatever happens of its own accord, without human intervention. In this sense, Mill says, the counsel is not meaningless, but it is irrational and immoral. It is irrational, because every human action amounts to altering the spontaneous course of nature. Building a house, raising a bridge, even opening an umbrella to shelter from the rain: all of these are such acts. If we are to take nature as our model, then plowing a field or binding a wound would be wrong as well. He describes its immorality in the most unsettling part of the essay. We might paraphrase Mill roughly like this: nearly everything for which people are hanged or imprisoned when they do it to one another, nature does every single day. Nature impales people, has them torn apart by wild beasts, burns them in fire, crushes them with stones, starves them, freezes them, poisons them. And in doing so, it makes no distinction between good and evil, between the guilty and the innocent. An earthquake leaves the murderer and the mother nursing her child beneath the same rubble. An epidemic never asks anyone what they have done. If a human being did such things, Mill argues, we would not hesitate for a moment to call them by their proper name. So why should we look to such a nature as our teacher of morals?

Mill was not alone in this outburst in his day. Around the same time, in eighteen sixty, Charles Darwin wrote a letter to the American botanist Asa Gray in which he mentions a family of insects. The parasitic wasps of this family lay their eggs inside the bodies of caterpillars, and the larvae grow by eating the caterpillar alive from within. Darwin writes that he cannot persuade himself that a beneficent creator would have designed these creatures expressly for that purpose. For both Darwin and Mill, the worry was the price of treating nature's order as a moral model. Anyone who looked at nature closely came face to face with suffering beyond measure.

Mill's indictment does not end there. His real target is the way the idea that "what is natural is good" plays out in everyday life. He looks at the impulses that arise in us spontaneously. A child, he says, is not born generous, patient or honest. These things are learned. Even cleanliness, in his view, is not a natural inclination but a habit acquired through education. Courage is won through a struggle against fear. For Mill, nearly all the human virtues worthy of praise emerge from disciplining what nature hands us ready-made. Seen this way, humanity's moral task is not to imitate nature, but to correct it.

Let us pause here for a moment and return to the Stoics. Did Mill really refute them? Not entirely. As we saw earlier, for Chrysippus human nature was reason. What Mill called "correcting," the Stoic described as "living in accordance with nature." The two sides were saying almost the same thing in different words: do not leave a spontaneous impulse as it is; give it shape through reason. Mill's real objection was to how loosely the word "nature" was being used. If a single word can mean "reason," "instinct," "the ordinary" and "what ought to be" all at once, then anyone can win an argument simply by concealing which meaning they are using.

Mill's concern was not unfounded. History is full of examples of how this word can be put to use. In his work the Politics, Aristotle argued that some people are slaves by nature. For centuries, keeping women out of politics and education was defended on the grounds that it would be "contrary to their nature." In many schools, the hands of left-handed children were tied, because their condition was seen as a natural deviation. In each of these examples, "nature" was not being used to explain anything. It was being used to place an existing order beyond question. When you ground something in nature, anyone who objects to it seems to be standing not just against you, but against the universe itself.

And so Mill's essay leaves the reader alone with an unsettling question. If the word "natural" does not really describe anything, why is it laid on the table like a trump card at the end of every argument? The question cuts deepest when it is asked about human behavior itself.

"It's human nature." How many times have we heard that sentence, and how many times have we said it? When someone cannot rein in their anger, when someone is unfaithful, when a community looks upon a stranger with hostility, when the strong crush the weak, that sentence is always there. Sometimes it is said with a sigh, sometimes with a shrug. On the surface it is an explanation, but more often than not it serves as a full stop. The moment it is spoken, the discussion ends, because what can anyone do against nature?

Today this sentence is even more powerful than before, because it now speaks the language of science as well. Evolutionary biology, and the psychology that leans on it, look for the origins of aggression, jealousy, in-group favoritism and the pull toward short-term pleasure in the conditions our ancestors lived in. Much of this research is serious and valuable. Understanding where a behavior comes from also helps us find ways to change it. The trouble begins the moment a finding makes its way into everyday conversation. As it passes from ear to ear, the sentence "this tendency may have an evolutionary origin" becomes "this tendency is natural." And the next step is always the same: "If it's natural, there's nothing to be done." The very gap Hume pointed out two hundred and seventy years ago is crossed once again, this time wearing a lab coat. There is a sadness in this, because the slide usually comes not from bad intent but from exhaustion. We all know how hard it is to change ourselves. How many times have we resolved to break a habit and failed? How many times, after a flash of anger, have we said, "That's just how I am"? The phrase "it's human nature" lightens that burden. It lifts the blame from the individual and places it on the species, even on a past stretching back millions of years. In one sense, it is a compassionate phrase: it keeps us from being merciless toward ourselves. But in another sense, it also frees us from being accountable to ourselves.

The phrase has a flip side, too: "unnatural." Saying that something is not natural carries a verdict every bit as absolute as saying that it is. To describe a way of life, a choice, a technology or a treatment as "against nature" is to condemn it before any discussion begins. This was exactly the danger Mill warned about: whichever way the word is turned, it lets us reach a judgment without offering a reason. Seen with an impartial eye, whether we say "natural" or "unnatural," the question that needs asking stays the same: Is this thing good or bad? Does it harm people, or does it benefit them? The answer to those questions cannot be found by asking whether the thing comes from nature.

So does nature tell us nothing at all about morality? That, too, would be too harsh a conclusion. Perhaps the most balanced answer was given long before Mill, once again by Aristotle. In the second book of his Nicomachean Ethics, he writes that the virtues arise in us neither by nature nor against nature. Nature gives us the capacity to receive the virtues, and it is habit that brings them to completion. To be brave, we must act bravely; to be just, we must act justly. And if we remember that this same Aristotle grounded slavery in nature, the line becomes all the more meaningful. A single thinker left us, within the same body of work, both the most dangerous and the wisest use of the word.

The Roman orator Cicero, too, calls habit "a kind of second nature." The expression still lives in our language: we say, "That job has become second nature to him." Perhaps the way to break the spell of the word "natural" lies hidden in this second nature. Our first nature is a starting point we were given. The urges, inclinations, fears and desires that arise on their own are part of that beginning. We did not choose them. But our second nature, the character we acquire through repetition and choice, through reflection and trial, is something we can choose. Al-Ghazali's effort to scrape away the layers of imitation covering our innate nature, the Stoic's passing of his anger through the filter of reason, even Zhuangzi's warning that every being should keep to its own measure, all of them tell us that this second nature is built through labor.

Today the word "natural" is used more often than ever before, in the supermarket, at the pharmacy, in political debates and around family dinner tables. Sometimes it sells a product, sometimes it excuses a behavior, and sometimes it shuts a person out. The reason we love the word so much may be that it spares us the hardest question: Is this good? Asking that question demands thinking, weighing, and sometimes confronting our own habits. "Natural," on the other hand, arrives with the answer already in hand. That little green leaf on the label may be promising exactly this: that we can reach what is good without going to the trouble of thinking.

Perhaps we don't need to abandon the word altogether. It may be enough to ask a small question wherever we hear it: Natural in what sense? Because it happens on its own, because it is ordinary, or because someone wants to tell us that this is how things ought to be? Once we start asking that question, the word does not lose its power; it only loses its spell. What remains is a starting point, and from that point on, we decide for ourselves what to do.
