In June of eighteen seventy-six, a cavalry lieutenant colonel ignored his scouts' warning that there were more Sioux in the valley than his men could ever handle, and he split his regiment into three. A few hours later, of the more than two hundred men riding with him, not a single one was left to tell the tale. But here is the real story: the greatest victory the Native Americans ever won became the war that cost them their land.

In the spring of eighteen sixty-eight, at Fort Laramie on the plains of Wyoming, representatives of the United States government signed an agreement with chiefs of the Lakota, the Dakota and the Arapaho. Known as the Fort Laramie Treaty, it brought an end to two years of bloody conflict. The Oglala Lakota leader Red Cloud had besieged the forts along the Bozeman Trail for so long that the army was finally forced to withdraw. It was one of those rare moments when an Indian nation made the United States Army accept its terms.

The Fort Laramie Treaty recognized all of the land west of the Missouri River in what is now South Dakota as the Great Sioux Reservation. In the middle of that land stood mountains that rose suddenly from the plains, covered in pine forests, looking dark from a distance. The Americans called them the Black Hills. The Lakota called them Paha Sapa, and to them this place was far more than a hunting ground. It was sacred: a landscape where healing herbs were gathered, where people went on vision quests, where the stories of the people's very existence unfolded. The treaty was perfectly clear. Without the Indians' permission, no white person could settle on this land, or even pass through it. And any future transfer of land would require the signatures of three quarters of the adult men.

That promise held for exactly six years. In eighteen seventy-three, America plunged into a severe economic depression; banks collapsed and railroad companies went bankrupt. The country was hungry for a new source of wealth, and rumors of gold in the Black Hills had been circulating for years. In the summer of eighteen seventy-four, the army organized what was officially called a reconnaissance expedition. The stated purpose was to find a suitable site for a fort in the region. At the head of the expedition was the commander of the Seventh Cavalry Regiment, Lieutenant Colonel George Armstrong Custer.

Custer was thirty-four years old at the time and one of the best-known soldiers in the American public eye. During the Civil War he had become a brevet brigadier general at just twenty-three, and he had made his name with his long golden hair, the uniforms he designed himself, and his reckless cavalry charges. He was also a writer who loved addressing his readers; his accounts of life on the plains were published in magazines.

The eighteen seventy-four expedition was like a small army: around a thousand soldiers, more than a hundred supply wagons, an artillery battery, scientists, a photographer, journalists and two experienced gold prospectors. At the end of July, on the banks of a stream called French Creek, the prospectors saw flecks of gold in their pans. Custer sent the news by scout to the nearest telegraph line. Within a few weeks, newspapers on the East Coast were running headlines about great gold deposits discovered in the Black Hills. The amount actually found was modest, but for a country in depression, that was enough.

The following year, thousands of prospectors poured into the forbidden land. At first the army tried to remove some of them, but those arriving always outnumbered those being expelled. In the autumn of eighteen seventy-five, the government brought the Lakota an offer: to buy the Black Hills for six million dollars, or to use them in exchange for an annual lease. Most of the Lakota refused. Some said that sacred land was not for sale; others found the offer laughable and demanded a far greater price.

In November of eighteen seventy-five, a secret meeting was held at the White House, attended by President Ulysses Grant himself. Two decisions came out of it. First, the army would no longer stop the miners. Second, every Lakota and Cheyenne band living outside the reservation, in the areas the treaty recognized as hunting grounds, had to return to the agency headquarters on the reservation by January thirty-first, eighteen seventy-six. Those who did not return would be considered hostile.

It was an order that was almost impossible to carry out. In the dead of winter, messengers struggled across snow-covered plains to reach scattered camps. Some received the news late; some never received it at all. And the camps it did reach could not march hundreds of kilometers through freezing cold with their children and their elders. The deadline passed, and the very next day the matter was handed over to the army. A people who believed they had never given up any land had been declared enemies for living on their own land.

On paper, the army's plan was simple. Three separate columns would enter the vast basin in what is now southeastern Montana from three directions and close a pincer around the Indian camps. General George Crook would set out from the south, from Wyoming. Colonel John Gibbon would come from the west, from the forts in Montana. And General Alfred Terry would march from the east, from Fort Abraham Lincoln in North Dakota, with Custer's Seventh Cavalry Regiment forming the striking force of his column. Custer very nearly missed the campaign altogether. In the spring of eighteen seventy-six, in Washington, he had testified in a corruption inquiry that touched Secretary of War William Belknap, and he had indirectly pointed a finger at President Grant's brother as well. Furious, Grant stripped him of his command. Only Terry's intervention allowed Custer to return to the head of his regiment, and even then, command of the whole campaign would remain with Terry. It is not hard to imagine how eager Custer was to prove himself once again.

Through that winter and spring, families who had broken away from the agencies began to gather around Sitting Bull, the holy man of the Hunkpapa Lakota. Sitting Bull was then in his forties. In his youth he had made his name as a warrior, but by now his real weight came from his spiritual authority. He had refused to sign treaties with the whites and had never settled on a reservation. In March, the army attacked and burned a Cheyenne camp on the banks of the Powder River, and that drove the Northern Cheyenne toward him as well. The camp grew as it moved, because with the coming of spring, families leaving the agencies to join the summer hunt had also taken to the trail.

In the first days of June, on the banks of a creek called the Rosebud, the camp held the most important ceremony of the Plains peoples: the Sun Dance. In this ceremony, dancers prayed on behalf of their people by enduring pain, offering their own bodies as a sacrifice. That year, Sitting Bull allowed one hundred small pieces of skin to be cut from his arms, fifty from each arm. Then, covered in blood, without food or water, he danced for hours, gazing at the sun.

Toward the end of the dance, he collapsed. When he came to, he described the vision he had seen. He had seen countless soldiers falling from the sky toward the camp, heads down and feet up, raining down like grasshoppers. A voice had told him that it was giving these soldiers to his people, because they did not know how to listen. Those who heard the vision interpreted it in only one way: the soldiers would attack the camp, and they would be defeated. Falling head first was the sign of death.

The vision carried one more warning, and later great importance would be placed on it. Sitting Bull had told his people not to touch the weapons, horses, or belongings of the dead soldiers. This gift was for victory alone, not for plunder.

The vision's first test came sooner than expected. On June seventeenth, Crook's column coming up from the south, roughly one thousand soldiers along with their Indian allies, Crow and Shoshone warriors, had halted along the upper Rosebud when Lakota and Cheyenne warriors fell upon them in a sudden attack. Among them was the Oglala warrior Crazy Horse. The fighting lasted all day. When the warriors withdrew that evening, Crook held the field, but his ammunition was exhausted and his wounded were mounting. He fell back to his base camp and did not advance for weeks. Most important of all, he sent no word to Terry or Custer about what had happened.

And so the southern arm of the pincer was out of the game, and those who remained knew nothing of it. Meanwhile, the Indian camp moved west, into the valley of the Little Bighorn River. The Lakota called this valley the Greasy Grass. As more people arrived from the agencies, the camp stretched for kilometers. Hunkpapa, Oglala, Miniconjou, Sans Arc, Blackfoot Lakota, and Northern Cheyenne lodges lined the river. Estimates vary, but the camp held somewhere between six thousand and eight thousand people, among them perhaps fifteen hundred to two thousand men able to fight. It was one of the largest gatherings the Plains had ever seen.

On June twenty-second, Terry sent Custer and his regiment south along the Rosebud. Terry himself and Gibbon would swing around by way of the river and come in from the north. They offered Custer the slow-moving Gatling guns and an extra battalion of cavalry, but he turned down both. He believed his regiment, some six hundred cavalrymen along with Arikara and Crow scouts, would be equal to anything.

For three days the regiment pushed forward fast; some nights the march went on until morning. The scouts found the place along the creek where the Sun Dance had been held, and then the broad, trampled trail the camp had left behind as it moved. At dawn on the morning of June twenty-fifth, the Crow scouts climbed a hill between the two rivers. The soldiers would come to call that hill the Crow's Nest. From there, at a distance of more than twenty kilometers, the scouts saw an enormous horse herd and the smoke of campfires in the Little Bighorn Valley. By the time Custer climbed the hill, the sun was up and the valley was buried in haze; he could see nothing. Despite his scouts' warnings, he kept the size of the camp small in his mind.

That same morning, word came that soldiers who had gone back to look for a supply box dropped along the trail had run into a few Indians. Custer took this to mean that the regiment had been discovered. His greatest fear was not a fight; it was that the village would scatter and flee, and the campaign would come home empty-handed. So he did not wait for the next day, and decided to attack at once. Toward noon, he divided the regiment. Captain Frederick Benteen was to take three companies, about a hundred and twenty-five men, to the hills in the southwest and cut off anyone trying to escape. Major Marcus Reno, with three companies of about a hundred and forty soldiers and the support of the scouts, was to ride down into the valley and attack the village head-on. Custer himself, with five companies of about two hundred and ten men, would support Reno. One more company stayed behind to guard the slow-moving pack train carrying ammunition and supplies. So the regiment was actually split into four, but it was three separate pieces that would decide the outcome of the battle. And none of them knew exactly where the others were.

At around three o'clock, Reno crossed the river and galloped across the open valley toward the southern end of the village, toward the Hunkpapa lodges. The village's first reaction was surprise and panic. Women snatched up their children, and the elderly fled north. In those first minutes, the Hunkpapa warrior Gall, who would go on to become one of the leading figures of the battle, lost two of his wives and three of his children.

But attacking a crowded camp turned out to be something very different from what Reno had expected. A few hundred meters short of the village, seeing the warriors in front of him multiplying fast, he halted. He ordered his men to dismount and spread out. The cavalry unit became a scattered line of infantry on open ground. Because one soldier in every four had to stay back to hold the horses, their firepower dropped even further. When the warriors began to sweep around the left end of the line, the soldiers fell back to the stand of trees along the riverbank on their right.

The timber was a place that could have been defended well. But here, disorder turned into disaster. As Reno stood beside the Arikara scout Bloody Knife, a bullet struck the scout in the head, and Bloody Knife's blood splattered across the major's face. According to eyewitness accounts, Reno then gave a string of contradictory orders: mount, dismount, then mount again. Then he galloped out of the timber, heading for the bluffs across the river. Not everyone had heard the order. Some soldiers stayed behind in the trees, while the rest followed him in a disorderly mob.

The retreat became a hunt for the warriors. Riders galloped alongside the fleeing soldiers and knocked them from their saddles. The river crossing was steep and narrow. Horses shoved against one another to plunge into the water, then struggled to climb the slope on the far bank. By the time Reno got from the timber to the hilltop, he had lost about forty of his men, and many others were wounded or trapped back in the trees. Those who made it to the top, gasping for breath and half in shock, tried to form a defensive line.

Meanwhile, Benteen had scouted the empty hills he had been assigned and turned back. On the way, he met a messenger from Custer. The note, hastily scribbled by Custer's adjutant, William Cooke, was very short: it told Benteen to come on, that the village was big, to be quick, and to bring the ammunition packs. That note was the last written message to come out of Custer's command. The man who carried it, the Italian-born trumpeter Giovanni Martini, would also be one of the last soldiers to see Custer alive.

Benteen reached Reno's hill at around four o'clock. There he found a shattered command. Gunfire could be heard to the north, farther down the valley. One of the officers, Captain Thomas Weir, moved north with his company without waiting for orders and climbed a hill that would later bear his name, Weir Point. Several kilometers away in the distance, he saw clouds of dust and smoke, and riders moving across the hills. But by then, the warriors were turning toward him. When the other companies came up behind him, the force pulled back. And the gunfire had stopped.

No soldier could ever tell what Custer's five companies did that afternoon. Because not a single one of them survived. What we know comes from two sources: the accounts passed down over the years by the Lakota and Cheyenne who fought there, and the ground itself.

By the time Reno rode down into the valley, Custer had moved north along the high bluffs east of the river. He saw the village from above, and only then did he grasp how big it was. Perhaps he was planning to strike the northern end of the village and capture the fleeing families; taking women and children hostage was a known tactic the army had used in the Plains wars. Part of his command rode down toward the river through a dry creek bed that would later be called Medicine Tail Coulee. But under fire from the opposite bank, they turned back before they could cross.

Meanwhile, the warriors who had driven back Reno's attack were streaming north. Gall was pressing in from the south and the east. Two Moons and Lame White Man, leaders of the Northern Cheyenne, had joined the attack as well. And according to Native accounts, Crazy Horse led a large body of warriors in a wide arc to the north and came around behind the soldiers. The five companies, strung out along the ridges, were spread across a line several kilometers long, and they were being surrounded on every side. The terrain favored the warriors. Dry creek beds, ravines and tall grass let them close in on the soldiers without being seen. Many of them used bows. Arrows loosed upward on a high arc could rain down on the soldiers from out of sight. Some also carried repeating rifles that fired faster than the army's single-shot carbines. The soldiers' Model eighteen seventy-three Springfield carbines had a long range. But as they heated up, spent cartridge cases sometimes jammed in the chamber and had to be pried out with a knife. Historians still debate how much this affected the outcome of the battle.

The story the ground had to tell came to light in nineteen eighty-three. That summer, a brush fire burned the grass off the battlefield entirely. Teams led by the archaeologists Richard Fox and Douglas Scott swept the site inch by inch with metal detectors. They found thousands of bullets and cartridge cases. Because each weapon's firing pin left its own distinctive mark on a cartridge case, they could trace how individual guns moved across the battlefield. The picture that emerged did not match the orderly last-stand scene painted hundreds of times in American art. Companies had held their ground in one place, then broken apart and fled toward one another in small groups. The findings largely agreed with the accounts of Native American eyewitnesses. Those accounts described the battle collapsing into a rout within the final half hour, perhaps even less.

On the southern ridge, the company of Captain Myles Keogh and the company of Lieutenant James Calhoun, Custer's brother-in-law, gave way one after the other. The survivors ran north, toward the hill where Custer stood. That hill would later be known as Last Stand Hill. There, about forty soldiers shot their horses and used them as breastworks. Lame White Man was killed in one of these final assaults. When the resistance on the hill collapsed, some soldiers fled toward the river, into a ravine, and died there.

Among the dead were five members of the Custer family: George Armstrong Custer; his brother Tom Custer, a two-time recipient of the Medal of Honor; his younger brother Boston, who had joined the expedition as a civilian; his eighteen-year-old nephew, Autie Reed; and his brother-in-law, James Calhoun. Also killed was Mark Kellogg, the correspondent who had joined the expedition hoping to get his dispatches to the newspapers back East. Custer himself had been shot in the chest and in the left temple.

To the south, on Reno's hill, the siege went on through that evening and all the next day. Sheltering behind dead horses and supply boxes, the soldiers suffered from thirst and from the fire of sharpshooters. The wounded begged for water. A few volunteers made their way down to the river under the warriors' fire and filled canteens. Most of the Medals of Honor awarded for this battle went to these water carriers.

On the afternoon of June twenty-sixth, the village began to pack up. Scouts had brought word that a new army was approaching from the north. The women took down the lodges, and a procession stretching for miles moved south toward the Bighorn Mountains. On the morning of June twenty-seventh, the column under Terry and Gibbon reached the valley. Gibbon's chief of scouts, Lieutenant James Bradley, saw scattered white shapes on the ridges. As he drew closer, he realized they were the bodies of soldiers, stripped of their clothing.

Two hundred sixty-eight men of the Seventh Cavalry, its attached scouts and civilians had been killed; not a single soldier from Custer's five companies survived. The only living thing to come off that field was Keogh's badly wounded horse, Comanche. Comanche was later cared for as the regiment's symbol, and no one ever rode him again. Lakota and Cheyenne losses are not known for certain. Most estimates put the number somewhere between thirty and one hundred.

When the news reached the East, America was celebrating. July fourth, eighteen seventy-six, marked the one hundredth anniversary of the Declaration of Independence. A great world's fair had opened in Philadelphia, showing off the nation's industry and progress to the world. A few days later, the newspapers reported that the country's most famous cavalryman and five of his companies had been wiped out. Shock quickly turned to rage. Congress promptly set aside funds to send new regiments to the plains. New forts began to rise across the northern plains.

The victory at the Little Bighorn was the most brilliant military day in the history of the Lakota and Cheyenne. But it brought them nothing that would last even until the following spring. The great camp would have had to break up by the end of summer anyway. No single valley could feed that many people and horses for long. As the bands separated, the army found it easier to pursue them. Through the winter, the army found the camps, burned their stores of food and seized their horse herds. The buffalo herds, too, were vanishing fast. Hunger accomplished what the warriors had not allowed the soldiers to do. Those who remembered the warning at the Sun Dance would always stress one thing afterward: after the battle, the weapons, horses and belongings of the dead had been taken. Some Lakota would come to see this plunder as one of the causes of the disasters that followed. August eighteen seventy-six, Congress passed a law. Under it, no rations would be handed out at the reservation agencies until the Lakota agreed to give up the Black Hills and their hunting grounds. In the years that followed, this policy came to be known as "sell or starve." In September, a government commission arrived at the agencies. The Fort Laramie Treaty required the signatures of three quarters of all adult men for any transfer of land, but the commission managed to collect signatures from only some of the chiefs and leading men. Congress made the agreement law in February eighteen seventy-seven. The Black Hills now officially belonged to the United States.

The leaders of the war also met their end in these years. In May eighteen seventy-seven, Crazy Horse came in to Camp Robinson in Nebraska with some nine hundred of his starving people and surrendered. In September of that same year, he was stabbed with a bayonet in a scuffle with soldiers who were trying to lock him up, and he died that night. Sitting Bull, for his part, crossed north into Canada with some of his people. But there were no buffalo there either, and in eighteen eighty-one he came in to Fort Buford in Montana with about two hundred people and laid down his arms. Nine years later, in December eighteen ninety, he was killed outside his home on the Standing Rock reservation, in a clash with the Lakota policemen who had come to arrest him.

The echoes of the Little Bighorn inside the army lasted a long time. Custer's widow, Elizabeth Custer, spent the rest of her life guarding her husband's heroic image and wrote books about him. Those who placed the blame elsewhere usually pointed at Reno and Benteen. At Reno's own request, a military court of inquiry met in eighteen seventy-nine and found no evidence strong enough to charge him with cowardice. But the argument never ended. To this day, those who defend Custer's decisions still argue with those who fault him for splitting the regiment and for attacking instead of waiting.

The story of the Black Hills did not end in eighteen seventy-seven. From the very first day, the Lakota insisted that the transfer was illegal, and they pursued the case in the courts for decades. Meanwhile, between nineteen twenty-seven and nineteen forty-one, the giant faces of four American presidents were carved into the granite of the Black Hills. Today this monument, known as Mount Rushmore, stands in mountains the Lakota hold sacred, and draws millions of visitors every year.

In nineteen eighty, the case reached the United States Supreme Court. By a vote of eight to one, the Court ruled that the Black Hills had been wrongfully taken from the Lakota. According to the Court, the land seized in eighteen seventy-seven had been worth seventeen point five million dollars, and interest running from eighteen seventy-seven onward had to be added to that sum. The total award came to more than one hundred million dollars.

The Lakota tribes, however, refused the money. To them, accepting it would mean agreeing that the land had been sold, when what they wanted was the land itself. Ever since, the money has sat in a government account, and with interest it has long since passed one billion dollars. Meanwhile, the Lakota reservations remain among the poorest places in the United States. A people with a billion-dollar account set aside in their name will not touch it, as a matter of principle.

The battlefield itself changed over time. For many years it carried Custer's name, and the only monument there honored the dead of the Seventh Cavalry. In nineteen ninety-one, the United States Congress renamed the site the Little Bighorn Battlefield National Monument and decided that a memorial should also be built for the Native Americans who fought there. That memorial opened in two thousand three. Today, on the ridges, among the white marble markers showing where soldiers fell, there also stand red granite markers showing where Lakota and Cheyenne warriors died.

Every year, in the last days of June, riders gather in the valley of the Greasy Grass to remember the battle. A battle won in a few hours of a single afternoon, and a land that has not been won back in a century and a half, stand side by side in this small valley.
