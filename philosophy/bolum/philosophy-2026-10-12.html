A body is found in the neighbourhood one morning; no one knows who did it. The Ottoman court accuses no one, but it charges the whole neighbourhood for the death. So — are you obliged to pay the bill for an injustice committed by a grandfather you never met, or do you have a right to your share of his glory?

In the first grey light of a winter morning, a man is lying face down in the middle of the street. There is no snow, only frost; the blood on the stones has frozen. The neighbourhood watches from behind its doors, and nobody wants to take the first step, because everybody knows the same thing: if the killer of this body is never found, the neighbourhood will pay for him.

This scene is not from a novel. It comes from a legal practice that ran for centuries in the Ottoman city. Islamic law has an institution called kasâme, a procedure of collective oath-taking: when a body is found somewhere and the killer is unknown, the people of that place cannot stand outside the matter. In Hanafi practice, 50 men from the quarter or the village swear an oath in the name of God: we did not kill him, and we do not know who did. The oath does not clear them. After the oath, the adult men of that place pay the dead man's blood money, the diyet, dividing it among themselves. A debt spread over three years. There is no culprit, but there is a bill.

The registers of imperial rulings kept in the Ottoman archives show that this was not an abstract question for law books. In the correspondence sent to the capital from the districts of Rumelia in the 18th century, you can read line by line how these cases were conducted, which village paid for which body. Which means that a baker who had stood over his dough all night long could still end up paying for a corpse found in his street at dawn. Paying for a death that nobody had ever accused him of causing.

The first reaction is anger. What kind of justice is this? But consider, for a moment, the logic behind the institution. The neighbourhood was the smallest security unit of its age. It was the thing that closed its gates at night, hired its own watchman, questioned the stranger, broke up the brawl. A murder with no known killer is proof that the neighbourhood did not do its own job. The blood money is not a punishment but an invoice: you did not keep your eyes open, and this is the price. The institution does not aim to punish a criminal; it aims to hold a community answerable for its own street. And it works. A neighbourhood that knows it will be the one paying moves heaven and earth to find the killer.

We like to think we live entirely outside this logic today. We do not. If a piece breaks off the façade of an apartment building and lands on a passer-by, nobody asks who poured that concrete; the owners collectively shoulder the damage. The mistake of a single employee is paid out of the company's accounts, not out of that person's pocket. When the state loses a compensation case, the money comes from taxpayers, not from the official who made the decision. Modern law did not abolish collective responsibility. It rewrote it in a politer, colder, more institutional language.

But the example of the neighbourhood has a limit: a neighbourhood is a partnership in space. You stand on the same street, you live through the same night, you hire the same watchman. Responsibility spreads sideways. The question of this episode, however, is not horizontal but vertical. Not in space, but in time.

Because the genuinely uncomfortable question is this: what if the partnership is not built on the same street but in the same blood, the same surname, the same flag? What if the body was not found in this morning's street but in the street of 80 years ago, and you had not even been born yet? If your grandfather did something to someone, does any residue of it pass to you? As a shame, a debt, an obligation to apologise, or even just as a discomfort?

Most people answer this question immediately, and they do it by flinging themselves to one of two extremes. One side says: no one can be held answerable for what their father did, this is a self-evident principle of conscience, and the opposite view is the mentality of the blood feud. The other side says: a people cannot move forward without reckoning with its own past, and the grandchild who keeps silent is an accomplice. Each position looks coherent from the inside. And notice that both of them use the same word: guilt.

The whole matter lies in that word. In the middle of the 20th century, after his own country had carried out one of the greatest destructions in history, a philosopher bent over precisely this word and noticed something: with a single term we are describing at least four separate things. And unless those four are pulled apart from one another, every sentence spoken about the grandfather's crime hangs in the air.

The winter of 1945. Heidelberg University is reopening among the ruins. A professor of philosophy who had been removed from his chair during the war because of his Jewish wife, and whose name had appeared on deportation lists, Karl Jaspers, walks into a lecture hall and announces the subject of his course: the guilt of the Germans. Sitting in front of him are young men who a year earlier had worn the uniform of that regime. The following year these lectures become a book, Die Schuldfrage, in English The Question of German Guilt. It is a thin volume, and one of the texts in the history of philosophy whose weight is most inversely proportional to its page count.

What Jaspers does is not an accounting but a separation. Out of the blurred feeling we call guilt he draws four distinct layers, and he appoints a different judge to each one.

The first is criminal guilt. You have committed an act: you gave the order, you pulled the trigger, you locked the door. Its judge is the court, its measure is the law, its outcome is punishment. This layer is entirely individual and cannot be transferred. Your grandfather's file is your grandfather's file.

The second is political guilt. To be the citizen of a state is to take on the consequences of what that state does. You may not have voted for it, you may have opposed it, you may even have gone to prison for opposing it; the burden of what was done in the name of the state still falls to the citizens of that state. Its judge is not conscience but power, politics, treaties; its outcome is not punishment but reparation and a reconstituted order. Here Jaspers uses the word burden, not the word sin.

The third is moral guilt. The reckoning in the place the law never reaches. Closing the curtain while your neighbour is taken away, carrying on with something you knew to be wrong because it was easier, keeping quiet. No one else can be the judge of this; the only court is the person's own conscience and those friends honest enough to help them look at it. No verdict of moral guilt can be issued about an entire people, because conscience does not come in the plural.

The fourth, and the strangest, is metaphysical guilt. Human beings stand in solidarity with one another; if an injustice happened somewhere and you went on living, then even if there was nothing you could have done, a wound opens inside you that does not close. Its judge, according to Jaspers, is God alone. The philosopher deliberately steps outside the law here, because what he is describing is not a penalty but a shaking of the ground.

The real value of these four layers is this: in argument, people constantly change layers. Someone who wants to refuse the political burden escapes into the criminal layer by saying "I never killed anyone." Someone who wants to convict a whole people leaps from the political burden to moral guilt. There are four separate scales, and people keep lifting the weight off one and setting it down on another, and the result comes out wrong every time.

Hannah Arendt, Jaspers's student and his correspondent for the rest of his life, took this distinction and sharpened it further. In an essay written as early as 1945 she argued that the idea of collective guilt is far more dangerous than it first appears. Because if everyone is guilty, no one is. The sentence "all Germans are guilty" sounds like the heaviest possible accusation; in practice it is the broadest possible acquittal. The moment you place the man who gave the order and the man who closed his curtain, the engineer of the camp and the villager who knew nothing, on the same side of the scale, you lose the ability to judge any of them. Guilt, once universalised, evaporates.

This is why Arendt draws a hard line between two words. Guilt is individual: it attaches to an act, to an agent, to an intention; it cannot be shared and cannot be inherited. Responsibility, on the other hand, can be collective: it is the price of belonging to a community, and it finds you even if you committed no act. Arendt grounds this as follows: we do not live our lives alone but among others; we receive from a community its language, its trust, its protection, its inheritance. Whoever takes that inheritance takes the other page of the ledger as well.

This was one of the reasons that Eichmann in Jerusalem, the book Arendt wrote after watching the trial there, attracted so much anger. When she described the man sitting across from her not as a monster but as a functionary who never thought about the meaning of what he was doing, she was assumed to be shrinking the crime. In fact she was doing exactly the opposite: she was handing the crime back to the person. Not to the spirit of an age, not to the current of history, not to the character of a people, but to that man, at that desk, with that signature.

Now place this distinction on the grandfather's table. If your grandfather bore criminal guilt, it is not yours and cannot be. If he bore moral guilt, its judge died long ago. But the political burden, meaning the consequences of that act that are still standing today, may still be sitting somewhere. That is the page on which the grandchild appears. Not as a culprit, but as an heir.

Western philosophy arrived at this distinction in the 20th century by walking through a terrible destruction. But the principle itself, in its clearest and sternest form, sits in a far older sentence.

The Qur'an repeats it in different chapters in almost the same words: no bearer of burdens bears the burden of another. The word used in this phrase, which appears in the chapters of al-An'am, al-Isra and Fatir, tells us a great deal. Wizr means a heavy pack carried on the back; wazira is the one who shoulders that pack. Which is to say that the scene has been a porter's scene from the beginning. Everyone carries his own load. The chapter of al-Najm takes it one step further: a person has nothing but what he himself has worked for. And the chapter of al-Muddathir compresses the whole accounting into a single image: every soul is held in pledge for what it has earned. A pledge is what you leave behind as security for a debt. No one is left in pledge for another man's debt.

This principle is a direct intervention into one of the most powerful institutions of pre-Islamic Arab society: the blood feud. In tribal logic there is no difference between the killer and the killer's lineage; when someone is killed, vengeance may be taken from the killer's relatives too, and indeed ought to be. What the verse says cuts that chain: the reckoning belongs to the person.

And here is where the tension lies. Within the same legal tradition there is an institution called the âkile. The âkile is a person's circle of close kin and mutual support; when someone causes a death by accident, without intent, this circle divides the blood money among themselves and pays it. Which means that the cost of an accident caused by one man's hand is paid by 20 relatives who had nothing whatsoever to do with it. A tradition that says no bearer of burdens bears the burden of another is, on the same page, organising payment by others. Is this a contradiction?

Islamic jurisprudence answers with a very clean distinction, and that distinction overlaps strikingly with Jaspers's four layers. Uqubah, punishment, is personal: retribution, imprisonment, sin, the reckoning in the hereafter cannot be transferred, divided or inherited. Daman, the compensation of damage, is not a punishment; it is the repair of a broken balance. And repair, regardless of whose hand caused the break, falls to whoever has the means. The verse speaks of the ledger of conscience and of the hereafter; the âkile speaks of how an orphaned household will eat. The first can never be handed over; if the second is not handed over, a victim is left with nothing.

The thinker who best explains the social basis of this logic is probably Ibn Khaldun. The concept of asabiyyah, which stands at the centre of the Muqaddimah, is the bond of solidarity that keeps a community standing: the thing that defends a person, feeds them, gives them an identity, makes them capable of what they could never do alone. What Ibn Khaldun insists on showing is that this bond is not a one-way gift. The circle that protects you is also the circle that pays because of you. The people who will line up behind you when something happens to you are the people who will split the bill when you do something to someone else. That is the cost of solidarity, and there has never been such a thing as solidarity without cost.

A similar tension can be seen in a far older text. In one place the Torah says that God visits the iniquity of the fathers upon the third and fourth generation. In another place it commands plainly that fathers shall not be put to death for their children, nor children for their fathers. And in the book of Ezekiel the prophet takes a proverb circulating among the people and breaks it apart: the fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the children's teeth are set on edge. You shall no longer use this saying, he says; the one who dies, dies for his own sin. Which is to say that thousands of years ago people were already circling the same question, caught between the same two poles.

All three traditions end up in a similar place. Sin is personal, the reckoning is individual, and no one is born stained because of the family they were born into. But the consequence, the damage left lying there, does not clear itself away; it goes on standing as something that needs repair, and the obligation of repair falls to the living.

So your grandfather's crime is not your crime. But what your grandfather left behind is not only a criminal record; it is a house, a name, a story, a position. And it is a curious fact about human beings that there is a state in which they refuse absolutely to divide this package, and another in which they insist on dividing it.

Picture a dinner table. A man who carries his grandfather's photograph in his wallet is telling his children about that man's courage. He says the name of a great commander in his line with pride, shows his children the inscription of a charitable foundation established by an ancestor he never met on his phone, and on the anniversary of a battle says "we won" — even if 900 years have passed since that battle. The link he draws between himself and the man who rode a horse in 1071 is plainly a bond of "we," and nobody asks him to prove it, and nobody finds it strange.

At the same table, ask the same man about a dark page in the past of his family or his country. The answer changes instantly: I hadn't even been born. Notice that the sentence is true. But the same sentence was equally true five minutes earlier, and then it went unused.

This is the sharpest point of the whole question. If pride flows down the generations without any difficulty, why doesn't shame? A person cannot read the profitable page of the past as "we" and the costly page as "them." Identity is an account book; to take the assets and refuse the liabilities is not to read the book but to tear it.

The law has no patience at all for this asymmetry, and its answer is exceedingly cold-blooded. Under the Turkish Civil Code an heir may refuse an inheritance. Nobody can be forced to accept an inheritance they do not want. But the rule of refusal is this: an inheritance is a single whole. You cannot take the house and leave its mortgage, take the shop and leave the shop's debts. If you accept, you accept all of it; if you refuse, you refuse all of it. What the law is doing here is not delivering a moral lesson; it is simply making consistency compulsory. Closing off, with a single article, the trap into which human conscience falls most often.

This is exactly what Jaspers meant by political burden. And exactly what Arendt meant by the responsibility we carry for things we did not do ourselves. Both arrive at the same place: belonging to a community has a price, and that price is paid by people who are not guilty. One example of it is the agreement signed in Luxembourg in 1952, six years after Jaspers's lectures. Federal Germany undertook to pay 3 billion marks to Israel and 450 million marks to the conference representing Jewish material claims, on a schedule spread over years. The great majority of the taxpayers who paid that money had been children, or had not yet been born, when the thing they were paying for was done. Nobody called them guilty; no court was convened, no one was stamped with a mark. What they were told was something else: you are citizens of this state, this state carried out a destruction, and the money for repair will come from here.

The real lesson of this example is not the money. The real lesson is how well the distinction works in practice. Had they chosen the language of collective guilt, they would have ended up at one of two doors: either they would declare an entire people stained from birth, which is precisely the same form of thought that led to the destruction in the first place, or, as Arendt said, by counting everyone guilty they would leave nobody guilty. Choosing the language of responsibility instead of guilt was the only way to keep the perpetrator's file open while still placing an obligation on the grandchild.

And this is a distinction that can be applied directly to your own life today. If there is an injustice in your family's past that someone did to someone, you are not obliged to carry shame; shame is the wrong emotion, because shame is the perpetrator's emotion, and to carry it is to put yourself, without noticing, in the perpetrator's place. What you do have to carry is plainer and heavier: is there a consequence of that injustice still standing today? A confiscated property, a severed tie, an unsaid word, a human being still alive? If there is, looking at it is your business. If there is not, then using the file of the past as a stick against others or a whip against yourself has no moral value whatsoever.

And there is a third thing, perhaps the most lasting of all. We began by describing the neighbourhood's payment of blood money as though it were an act of oppression. Yet the hidden assumption of that institution was this: a community genuinely owns what happens on its own street. A neighbourhood that refuses to pay has, in effect, conceded that the street is not its own. And a person today who closes off every question about the past with "I hadn't been born" is doing the same thing without realising it: claiming his ancestor's victory on one side, and abandoning his ancestor's street on the other.

Your grandfather's crime is not your burden. But your grandfather's world is your world, and if an account was left open in that world, the hands that can close it are now only your hands.
