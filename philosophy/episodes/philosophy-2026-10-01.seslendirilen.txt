You are waiting at a red light in morning traffic. The lane to your right is barely moving, someone swerves in front of you without signalling, and comes to a stop twenty centimetres from your front bumper. There is not even time to put a name to what is happening in your body: a heat at the nape of your neck, a locking of the jaw, a force in your hands that grips the wheel harder than it needs to. Before the sound of the horn has died away, three sentences assemble themselves at once inside your head, and even if you speak none of them, they have still been assembled. Then the light changes, the man drives off, you drive off. Ten minutes later you cannot remember his face, but the tightness in your chest soaks into the whole first half of your day.

That same evening, at the supermarket, a woman at the till is waving a receipt and shouting at the cashier. The sum in dispute is seven lira. The young cashier apologises, the woman cuts the apology off and starts again, and the people in the queue choose to look at their phones. And you, at exactly that moment, feel the same thing again. The same heat at the nape, the same lock in the jaw. Your body does not distinguish between the two situations. Your physiology writes the anger that leans on a car horn and the anger that burns at an injustice in the very same alphabet.

This is where the whole problem begins. Because although these two angers carry an identical bodily signature, morally they are two quite different things. In the first, what was obstructed was my lane, my forty seconds, my sense of precedence. In the second, what was obstructed was somebody else's dignity, and I notice it while nothing of my own interest has been touched at all. The first anger reports on me. The second anger reports on the world.

Now let us look at the person who never gets angry. There is certainly someone like this in your life. He does not raise his voice. He does not get drawn into arguments. When bad news arrives, not a muscle moves in his face. Everyone who speaks of him reaches for the same word: mature. Serene. Settled. The man stands there like a statue of virtue, and reminds the tense people around him of their own tension.

But let us add one detail. That same man heard, for eleven years, a child being beaten every evening in the flat next door, and did nothing. When the sounds on the other side of the wall rose, he turned up the television a little. He never once knocked on the door, his face never once flushed, he never once lay awake.

Does the word mature still sit comfortably now? Or does that same serenity have another name: numbness, blindness, the art of never disturbing one's own comfort? Because to the eye looking in from outside, the two are indistinguishable. The man who has disciplined himself for years and the man to whom nothing matters wear the same calm face. They speak in the same soft voice. They listen with the same patient gaze. Serenity is the shared costume of virtue and insensibility.

Let us try the reverse as well. Picture a man who gives the waiter a dressing-down because his soup arrived cold. The same man, a month later, seeing three people dismissed unfairly at his workplace, becomes the only one who strikes the table and objects. In the first scene we look down on him. In the second we admire him. But the capacity for anger is the same capacity in both. Two ladles of water from the same cauldron.

Which means there is a fault in the way the question has been framed. When we ask whether the person who gets angry is better or the person who does not, we are asking a question with no answer, because the axis is the wrong axis. The right question is this: anger at what, how much, when, against whom, and for how long? And the truly decisive one is the last: what did I do after I became angry?

There are two thinkers who first asked these questions properly, and the two of them give almost exactly opposite answers. One says anger cannot be disciplined and must be torn out by the root. The other says a person incapable of anger cannot even be counted as a person.

Seneca wrote his work On Anger at the request of his brother Novatus. The text is thought to have been composed in the years after the killing of Caligula, that is, in a period when Rome had only just seen that the anger of a single man could become state policy. Right at the start of the book, Seneca relays that certain wise men had called anger a brief madness. The Roman poet Horace had put the same idea more sharply: anger is a short insanity. Seneca takes this diagnosis and follows it to the end. For him, looking at the face of an angry man is evidence enough: the eyes widened, the colour drained, the breathing irregular, the hands unsteady. These are not the signs of an illness but of a derangement of the mind. A madman is not ashamed of his madness, and the angry man, in the very moment of his anger, is at his most certain of being in the right. That is where the danger lies.

Seneca's real philosophical move comes when he answers this objection: but without anger no one fights a war, without anger no justice is done, without anger nobody lifts a finger. Seneca refuses this. No virtue, he says, needs the assistance of a vice in order to do its work. A good physician does not get angry with his patient, he treats the disease. A good judge does not get angry with the offender, he sets the penalty by the measure of the offence. Indeed quite the opposite: a punishment delivered in anger is always either excessive or lands in the wrong place. An army, too, fights by discipline and not by rage; the soldier who charges in anger breaks the line.

And then the distinction of Seneca's that fits the modern argument best of all: the first stirring of the body is not yet anger. That heat you feel at your nape when someone cuts in front of you lies outside our will, and it is not in our power to abolish it. Anger is born when the mind gives its assent to that stirring, when it says, this is an injustice done to me and it must be answered. Anger, in other words, is not an emotional accident but a judgement. Because it is a judgement, it can be mistaken; and because it is a judgement, it can be withdrawn. This is what the Stoic state, translated as freedom from passion and misunderstood for centuries, actually amounts to: not failing to feel, but refusing to sign off on that first stirring straight away.

Seneca also places in his book a scene that carries the full severity of the matter. Caligula has the son of Pastor, a man of the Roman equestrian class, put to death. On the evening of that same day, Caligula invites the father to his table. Pastor comes, he drinks, he even appears cheerful. Because he has another son. At that table, that evening, there sits a father who does not become angry, and his serenity is not a virtue; it is a calculation, the only bargaining chip a father has left. Seneca tells this scene in order to show how cruel the tyrant was. But the scene shows us something else as well: beneath the refusal to be angry there may be virtue, and there may equally be fear, helplessness, calculation. From outside, all of them are the same calm face.

Aristotle, roughly three hundred years before Seneca, enters the question from precisely the opposite end in the fourth book of the Nicomachean Ethics. In his account of character, every virtue stands between two extremes, and the virtue belonging to the field of anger is mildness. This virtue has an excess: the man who boils over at once, who is angry at everything, who does not himself know whom he is angry with. Aristotle even divides this type into sub-categories: those who flare up and subside immediately, those who store it inside and carry it for years, those who cannot be appeased at all.

But that other word in Aristotle's vocabulary is the key to the whole debate: aorgesia, angerlessness. And he classifies it not as a virtue but as a deficiency. The man who fails to be angry at what ought to anger him, he says, appears to be a fool. To put up with being insulted, to overlook an insult offered to one's own people, is the mark of a slavish soul. That sentence does what very few sentences in the history of moral philosophy have done: it sets calmness directly alongside servility. For Aristotle, being able to feel anger at something is proof that the thing has value for you. If you are angry at nothing, then either you have transcended everything or you hold nothing of worth; and the second is far more common than the first.

Aristotle's criterion from the second book of the same work belongs here too: getting angry is easy, anyone can get angry; what is difficult is to be angry with the right person, to the right degree, at the right time, for the right reason and in the right way. Notice that what is being tested here is not the existence of anger but its address and its calibration.

The difference between the two thinkers is not a difference of temperament but a difference in the design of the human being. For Seneca the passions are false judgements, and a false judgement is to be uprooted and thrown away. For Aristotle the passions are the raw material of virtue, and raw material is not thrown away, it is worked. According to the one, trying to carve anger into shape is like trying to domesticate an explosive: you cannot do it, you only delay the detonation. According to the other, tearing anger out of a person tears out his sense of justice along with it.

And into the exact middle of this dilemma, from an altogether different geography, falls a sentence just two words long.

There is a scene recorded in the hadith collections. A man comes to the Prophet and asks for a piece of counsel. The answer he receives is short: do not become angry. The man insists and asks for the counsel again. The same answer comes: do not become angry. The man asks a third time, and the answer still does not change. This report, compiled by Bukhari, stands like an entire moral teaching compressed into two words.

Those two words have been read in two distinct ways for centuries. The first reading is this: suppress the feeling, learn to fall silent, do not stir. The outcome of that reading arrives precisely at what Aristotle called the slavish soul. The second reading takes the sentence not as a prohibition but as an instruction in discipline: take hold of the reins of your anger, do not let it drag you, you drive it.

The first piece of evidence for the second reading is the sources themselves. Because those same sources also record the moments in which the Prophet did become angry. In an incident related by Bukhari, he sternly rebukes a prayer leader who was wearying the congregation by drawing out the prayer excessively, and demands to know whether he is driving people into hardship. So anger was not something that had never once occurred in the life of the man giving the counsel. It had occurred, but it did not fall everywhere; it fell in one particular place: on an unnecessary burden being loaded onto the back of the weak.

The second piece of evidence is hidden inside the language. In the Qur'an, in the one hundred and thirty-fourth verse of the chapter Al-i Imran, the expression used in describing the praiseworthy human type is kazimin al-ghayz. The root here means not the one who has no anger but the one who holds his anger in; its dictionary sense has to do with putting a bridle in an animal's mouth, with pulling on its halter. The verse then immediately adds forgiving others. The human being the text praises, in other words, is not the one in whom nothing stirs; it is the one who, while something stirs within him, can keep hold of its halter. A bridle is of no use to someone with no horse.

The third piece of evidence lies in practice. In a report compiled by Abu Dawud, a person who becomes angry while standing is advised to sit down, and if it does not pass, to lie down. This is not a denial of the emotion; it is an acknowledgement that the emotion is something that dissolves over time. For someone to say to you, sit down, is to say at one and the same time that what you feel is real and that it will pass.

The clearest philosophical statement of this tradition comes from al-Ghazali. In the work known as The Revival of the Religious Sciences, or Ihya for short, he devotes a separate section to anger and puts the matter directly: those who argue that the faculty of anger must be extinguished altogether are mistaken. For this faculty was not given to the human being for nothing; protecting oneself, resisting oppression, defending one's dignity are all done by means of it. What al-Ghazali is after is itidal, that is, correct calibration. Too much of it makes a man an oppressor, too little makes him content with humiliation. For him, the person with no anger at all is not virtuous but devoid of any sense of honour; and the absence of that sense is not a loftiness but a defect. Notice how close al-Ghazali's criterion stands to Aristotle's; one in Baghdad and Tus, the other in Athens, and both pointing at the same middle ground.

Ibn Miskawayh builds the same line more systematically in his work The Refinement of Character. The virtue corresponding to the soul's faculty of anger is shaja'a, courage. The excess of this faculty is tahawwur: unmeasured recklessness, an imitation of bravery that burns both its owner and others. Its deficiency is jubn, cowardice. Something very fine is being said here. In this scheme cowardice is not the opposite of anger but anger left incomplete. Some people, that is, keep silent not because they are calm but because they are afraid; and the outward appearance of the silence is the same in both.

Now, from inside this same tradition, there is a line we all know: he who strikes needs a man without hands, he who curses needs a man without a tongue. At first hearing, this saying of Yunus Emre's seems to exalt exactly the person Aristotle called slavish. But the one Yunus is addressing is not the oppressor out there; it is his own self. That line is not a proposal for the ordering of society but a rule a dervish lays down for his own inner discipline: I will not take the reckoning for what is done to me. The difference between renouncing your own right and overlooking someone else's right is the mother of every distinction this episode has been resting on. The first is a sacrifice. The second is complicity.

Indeed, the same tradition kept in circulation for centuries a saying that likens the one who keeps silent in the face of injustice to a mute devil. Specialists in hadith do not consider its attribution to the Prophet sound, and cite it not as a hadith but as a saying of wisdom. But the fact that a saying whose source is disputed has nonetheless remained standing for ten centuries says something in itself: this culture has wanted to know that silence is not always a virtue.

Now take all these criteria and bring them into our own age, and notice something strange. In the world Seneca, Aristotle and al-Ghazali were arguing about, anger was an expensive thing. To be angry meant looking into the eyes of the person in front of you, hearing their voice, receiving their answer, and sometimes paying the price. Because anger had a cost, where it was spent mattered.

Today anger is cheaper than it has ever been in human history. Writing a sentence takes nine seconds. The other party is not a face but a name. There is no cost, and in fact there is a reward: likes, shares, approval. The economy of anger has been turned inside out. It used to be expensive to be angry; now it pays.

There is a measurable side to this transformation as well. A study published in two thousand seventeen in the journal of the National Academy of Sciences in the United States found that in political messages shared on social media, each moral-emotional word raised the rate of diffusion by roughly twenty percent. The logic of the platform, in other words, carries the angry sentence faster than the calm one. In the same year, the neuroscientist Molly Crockett argued that the digital environment lowers the cost of expressing moral outrage while raising its reward, and that this can turn outrage from a moral response into a habit. Habit is an important word. Because habit is the word on which Aristotle's entire ethical system rests; virtue is acquired by habit, and so is vice.

So let us apply Aristotle's five criteria to the anger of the present. At the right person? Most of the time, no; anger falls less on the guilty party than on whoever stands nearest to them, whoever is most reachable, most defenceless. To the right degree? There is no scale; words of the same intensity are spent on a parking dispute and on the ruin of a country. For the right reason? Sometimes, but another reason is always mixed in alongside the first: being seen. And the real question, for the right length of time? Here there is almost nothing that passes the test. An injustice lasts two days, on the third day it gives way to a new injustice, and a week later nobody can recall the victim's name. Has the person who carried their anger for forty-eight hours and then set it down really been angry at that injustice, or have they merely consumed the brief sensation of moral goodness that being angry provides?

At this point contemporary moral philosophy splits cleanly in two, and the interesting thing is that the split almost coincides with the line Seneca and Aristotle drew. On one side there is Martha Nussbaum; in her study Anger and Forgiveness she underlines a wish buried inside anger: the wish that the other party suffer. And she asks: does the suffering of the perpetrator bring back what the victim lost? If it does not, then that portion of anger is not moral energy but no more than a fantasy. This is why Nussbaum says the only useful form of anger is the form that turns its gaze from punishment to repair. The echo, twenty centuries later, of Seneca's analogy of the physician.

On the other side there are thinkers such as Myisha Cherry. Cherry marks off a kind of anger that she names after a poet and writer: anger whose target is not the fall of a person but the breaking of an order. This anger, she says, carries information. It tells you what is unbearable, where a limit has been trampled, and no other emotion brings that news as quickly. A society that has entirely given up being angry at injustice loses the organ by which injustice is noticed. The echo, twenty-four centuries later, of Aristotle's diagnosis of the slavish soul.

And there is a single criterion binding these two sides together, in which all the threads accumulating since the beginning of this episode gather: the day after your anger.

Because anger in itself is neither a virtue nor a vice; it is a tension, an energy, an alarm. An alarm does not save a building; it saves it if it gets the people out. So the answer to the question of whether the person who never gets angry is virtuous or insensible lies not in that person's face but in their next day. The man who heard the child behind the wall stayed calm, and in the morning went and knocked on the door, then notified the people who needed to be notified, then followed it up for a month: his serenity is a virtue, indeed a virtue that has done more than anger could have done. The same man stayed calm and did nothing: that serenity is not virtue but comfort. And what separates these two is not the absence or presence of the feeling but the presence or absence of the act.

The same criterion tests the angry person too, and there it stings a great deal more. The one who struck the table, who raised his voice, who was in the right: did he do anything the next day? Did he write a petition, call one person, take on one risk? If he did not, then that anger was not justice but a discharge; worse, because it gave him the sensation of having done justice, it was the very thing that kept him from actually doing it. Seneca's calm and Aristotle's anger meet in the same examination: neither counts as a virtue until it turns into work.

Perhaps the last question to ask is this: who pays the bill for your anger? When you lose your temper at the man who cuts in front of you in traffic, you pay the bill, with half your day. When you are angry at the woman shouting at the till and say nothing, that young cashier pays the bill, by being left alone. And when you turn your anger into work at the place where you saw the injustice, the bill goes to the right address for the first time: to the one who committed it.
