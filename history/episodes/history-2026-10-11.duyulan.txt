You do not need to fire a single shot to starve a city, you only need to close the roads.  When every gate into Berlin was shut in the summer of 1948, what was left were three thin  corridors in the sky.  There were 62 seconds until the next aircraft could land, and that lasted 15 months.  In the summer of 1948, Berlin was a strange thing for anyone looking at a map.  The four powers that had won the war had divided the city into four parts among themselves,  but the city itself sat right in the belly of the Soviet occupation zone.  More than 160 kilometers inside the nearest occupation boundary to the west.  So the American, British and French sectors were not on the edge of a country, they were  in the middle of a sea.  Every sack of flour, every ton of coal, every barrel of petrol that entered those sectors  had to travel over railways, roads and canals under Soviet control.  When this arrangement was set up at Potsdam in 1945, nobody counted it as a danger, the  Allies were still in the mood of the war they had won together, and they intended to govern  Germany together.  Three years later, nothing of that intention was left.  The Allied Control Council, which was supposed to govern the city and the country jointly,  broke apart in practice in March 1948.  The Soviet representative walked out of a meeting and never returned.  But what really accelerated the rupture was not politics.  It was money.  The Reichsmark left over from the war, the old German mark, meant nothing.  Because prices were frozen on paper, the shops were empty, real trade was done on the black  market, and the real currency was the American cigarette.  To get out of this dead end, the Western powers decided to tie their zones into a single  economy and issue a new currency.  They announced it on the 18th of June, 1948, and put it into force on the 21st of June,  the Deutsche Mark.  250 million new marks, printed in America and carried secretly into Germany, changed  prices, shop windows and expectations across the western half of the country overnight.  The Soviets issued a separate currency for their own zone and treated the whole of Berlin  as that currency's territory.  On the 23rd of June the Western powers brought the new mark into the Western sectors.  The answer came the next morning.  On the 24th of June, 1948, all passenger and freight traffic to the Western sectors stopped,  for technical reasons, a bridge on the motorway went in for repairs.  Day A fault appeared on the railway, maintenance began on the canal.  The electricity supplied to the Western sectors from power stations in East Berlin was cut  off.  The potatoes, milk and vegetables that came in from the surrounding villages stopped as  well.  The Western sectors had 36 days of food and 45 days of coal in their stores, and more  than 2 million people were living off those stores.  In the Western capitals, three courses of action were discussed that week.  The first was to drive an armored convoy up the motorway and force the barriers open.  The American military governor, General Lucius Clay argued for this, while Washington thought  it might end in war.  The second was to evacuate the city, to Clay, that would be a letter of surrender handed  to the rest of Europe.  The third was the option nobody regarded as a serious solution.  The air.  Here, a small piece of paper written at the end of the war turned out to be decisive.  In November 1945, the four powers had agreed in writing on three air corridors running  from Western Germany to Berlin, three lanes approaching from the direction of Hamburg,  Hanover and Frankfurt.  Each of them 32 kilometers wide.  Rights of passage on the ground had never been tied to any such document, rights of  passage in the air had been.  Throughout the blockade the Soviets left those corridors alone, to touch them would have  meant disregarding their own signatures and accepting the risk of direct conflict.  Clay telephoned Curtis LeMay, the commander of the American air forces at Wiesbaden, and  asked whether his aircraft could carry coal.  As the exchange has come down to us, LeMay's answer was that they could carry anything.  On the 26th of June, 1948, the American transport aircraft in Europe, twin-engined C-47 Dakotas,  took off for Berlin.  80 tons of cargo were set down in the city that day.  Two days later the British opened their own route.  Berliners called the whole business the Luftbrucke, the air bridge and the name stuck.  The gulf between the 80 tons of that first day and what the city actually needed was  itself the real problem that the coming months would have to solve.  Because feeding a city from the air is a romantic sentence, but a concrete calculation.  The planners sat down and did the arithmetic.  Keeping Berliners from starving meant something like 1500 tons of food a day, flour and grain,  dried potato, powdered milk, fat, meat, sugar, salt, and a little coffee.  On top of that, heating, electricity generation, and keeping the factories turning required  some 3,500 tons of coal and liquid fuel.  The total came to a figure close to 5,000 tons a day.  The aircraft on hand was the C-47, which carried 3 tons per flight.  That meant well over a thousand flights a day, and it was not possible.  So the first piece of the solution was technical, four-engined C-54 Skymasters, which carried  10 tons and were easier to unload, were pulled into Germany from American bases all over  the world.  The British thickened the route too, with York transports and with aircraft from the  civilian companies they chartered.  The second piece was the airfields, and that was where matters turned ugly.  Templehof Airport, in the American sector, was right inside the city.  There were apartment blocks, seven stories high on the approach line, aircraft passed  over the rooftops and came down at a steep angle, and at the end of the runway stood  a cemetery wall.  The runway surface had not been built for this work either, thin asphalt laid over rubble  fill cracked under 10-ton aircraft landing one after another.  German work crews would run onto the runway in the minutes between landings, fill the  holes with sand and asphalt, and then pull back to the edge.  Gatto Airport, in the British sector, was in better shape, but its capacity was limited.  On the 28th of July, 1948, General William Tunner arrived in Berlin.  Tunner had run the supply route, flown over the Himalayas to China in the Second World  War.  That route had been won not with heroics but with a timetable, an industrial process in  which every lost aircraft was entered as a line and a ledger.  Tunner saw air supply not as an adventure but as a factory.  In his own words, a successful air bridge in operation was about as exciting as water  dripping onto stone.  The day that proved how right he was came on Friday, the 13th of August, 1948.  The weather was foul, low cloud had settled over Berlin, it was raining, visibility kept  dropping.  Tunner had set out that day for a ceremony in the city, and his aircraft joined the queue  above Tempelhof.  First, at the end of the runway, a C-54 came down faster than it should have, went out  of control and caught fire.  The pilot behind him, dropping out of the cloud and seeing an aircraft burning on the  runway, braked hard, his tires blew and he closed the runway.  The third aircraft turned for the auxiliary dirt strip and skidded off it.  Three minutes of confusion turned into a disaster that would last an hour.  Above Berlin, aircraft were stacked in layers up to 3,000 meters, circling inside the cloud.  The tower was wrestling with several voices on the radio at once.  And from the bases in the west, a new aircraft was taking off every three minutes, and entering  the same cloud, because nobody had told them to stop.  Tunner switched on the radio, identified himself and silenced the tower.  Then he took that stack apart one aircraft at a time, everyone would return to their  departure base with their cargo.  For an hour Berlin received nothing.  Luck helped that day, and nobody was killed, but as he circled overhead Tunner saw one  thing with complete clarity.  The problem was neither the courage of the pilots nor the number of aircraft.  The problem was that each of 200 aircraft was making its own decisions.  Every pilot flew at his own speed, judged the weather with his own eyes, attempted the  approach according to his own experience.  In good weather this worked, in bad weather the entire system jammed at the same moment.  What would feed Berlin was not more aircraft, but fewer decisions.  Tunner built after Black Friday was a structure that had never been attempted in the history  of aviation.  Turning the sky into an assembly line.  The logic was simple.  If every aircraft does the same thing, none of them has to think.  First he removed the weather from the realm of debate.  Whether the sky was overcast or brilliantly clear, every flight would be flown under instrument  rules, under no circumstances would a pilot decide by eye.  Then he made the speed uniform in the corridor everyone would fly at the same speed, around  270 kilometers an hour.  There was no place for a slow aircraft or a fast one.  This was one of the reasons the C-47s were withdrawn from the route.  The corridors were divided into five layers 150 meters apart, and each aircraft moved  in its own layer, in its own minute.  Aircraft were lined up nose to tail, three minutes apart.  And the hardest rule of all, every aircraft had a single right of approach.  A pilot who missed the runway would not try again, he would turn back to his base with  his cargo.  One aircraft's second attempt disrupted the sequence of dozens behind it, one flight was  sacrificed and the line was preserved.  Waiting over Berlin, stacking up, was forbidden outright.  The ground section of the line was even more exacting.  The target was for an aircraft to spend no more than half an hour between landing and  taking off again.  The crews did not get out.  Fuel, the weather report, the signatures, all of it came to the aircraft.  Tunner had mobile snack bars selling coffee and sandwiches loaded onto jeeps and sent  to the edge of the runway right up to the nose of the aircraft, and these snack bars  were run by young women from Berlin.  What eliminated a crew's wish to walk to the canteen was the canteen coming to them.  The unloading was done by German teams of 12, working for money and for meals, a competition  for records broke out among them, and the 10-ton load of a C-54 came to be taken off  in under 10 minutes.  Maintenance fell into the same discipline, an inspection every 50 flying hours, and heavy  maintenance every 200 hours at the Burton Wood base in England.  Aircraft now went into the workshop not when they broke down, but when the schedule said  so.  The cargo itself was redesigned too, because every kilogram meant a kilogram of fuel.  Instead of potatoes they carried dried potato, Berlin had water.  Instead of milk, powdered milk went.  The bone was stripped out of the meat.  Coal was loaded in army duffel bags made from surplus military cloth, the dust of that coal  got into everything, into the aircraft's radios, into their wiring, into the crew's lungs.  Salt was a harder cargo, because it ate metal.  For that reason the British carried salt and Sunderland flying boats taking off from the  Elbow.  Those aircraft, with hulls built to withstand seawater, landed on Lake Havel in the west  of the city.  When the lake froze over in winter, the salt was loaded into containers slung under the  bellies of bombers.  To understand what this machine was capable of, one had to wait for the spring of 1949.  In mid April, for the sake of morale and competition, Tunner staged a planned maximum  effort trial, for 24 hours, from the 15th to the 16th of April, the whole road carried  nothing but coal.  The number of flights reached 1383, 12,941 tons of coal came down in Berlin, and there  was not a single accident.  That worked out to one landing, across all the airfields, every 62 seconds on average.  A city's sky had become a shift line running at intervals of less than a minute.  After that day the daily average never fell back to its old figures, 9000 tons was now  what an ordinary working day was worth.  But a landing every 62 seconds was not possible with two airfields.  Underneath that record lay a third runway, dug largely by hand in the autumn of 1948.  Tempelhof and Gattau had hit their ceiling by the autumn.  The only place to look for a third airfield was the French sector, and there, in the northwest  of the city, stood an old artillery training ground, Tegel.  The first pick struck the ground on the 5th of August, 1948.  There were no machines on that site, there were people.  Over the following months 19,000 Berliners worked on the job, women and men, three shifts,  day and night.  Most of the workers were women, because they had already been clearing the city's rubble  for years.  The wage was a few western marks a week and one hot meal a day, in the winter of the blockade,  everyone knew that the second item was worth more than the first.  The fill for the runway came out of the city itself.  The bricks of bombed houses were broken up, sieved, and poured under a runway of roughly  1700 meters.  Berlin built the ground that aircraft would land on out of its own ruins.  The one thing that could not be solved was heavy machinery.  There were no graders' rollers or asphalt pavers in the city, and none of them would  fit through the door of any transport aircraft.  In workshops in western Germany the machines were cut into pieces with welding torches,  carried into Berlin piece by piece in wide-bodied C-82s, and welded back together at the edge  of Tegel.  On the 5th of November 1948, the first C-54 landed on that runway.  90 days had passed between the first pick striking the ground and the landing gear touching  it.  One problem remained with the new runway.  Two tall radio masts stood directly above the approach line.  The masts were inside the French sector, but they belonged to Berliner Rundfunk, Berlin  Radio, which was under Soviet administration, and the broadcasts went out from there.  The French commander, General Jean Guineville, asked for the masts to be moved, offering  to have the Allies cover the cost.  His request was refused.  On the morning of the 16th of December, 1948, French engineers blew the masts up.  When the Soviet officer who learned of it asked how he could possibly have done such  a thing, Guineville's answer was short, with dynamite.  Even so, the real surprise of these 462 days was not in the aircraft.  The Soviet plan rested on the assumption that a city under pressure would side with its  own stomach rather than with the western powers, in the eastern sector.  But food distribution open to residents of the western sectors was even announced.  What was expected did not happen.  On the 9th of September, 1948, 300,000 people gathered in front of the ruins of the Reichstag,  the old German parliament building burnt out in the war.  Berlin's elected mayor, Ernst Reuter, addressed not the crowd but the world.  The peoples of America, of Britain, of France, of Italy should look at this city, and see  that they could not abandon it, or these people.  The same shift happened on a smaller scale at the fence around Tempelhof.  In July 1948, an American pilot, Gale Halverson, shared two packets of gum among the children  pressed against the fence, watching the aircraft, and told them he would be passing again the  next day.  So that they would recognize him, he would rock his wings on the approach.  He made little parachutes out of handkerchiefs, tied sweets and gum to them, and dropped them  out of the window on the landing run.  Word spread both in Berlin and in America, boxes of sweets and handkerchiefs began arriving  from schools and factories, and the thing turned into an official relief operation.  The children of Berlin named those aircraft, the Rausinen bomber, the Raisin bombers, and  Halverson's own name became Uncle Wiggly Wings.  These look like charming details, but the rest of that winter was not charming at all.  In November and December 1948, the fog that settled over Berlin very nearly stopped the  air bridge, on some days the number of aircraft able to land in the city was limited to a  handful.  The coal ration was cut, and people burnt their furniture and the trees in the parks.  Electricity was supplied for only a few hours a day, and a household's turn often fell after  midnight.  Berliners set their alarms for two in the morning and got up to cook, to iron and to  heat water.  Three and a half years earlier, aircraft of the same types had dropped bombs on this city.  Now a city that listened to that same sky counted the days on which it did not hear  engines.  The calculation that had started the blockade cracked in two places at once in the spring  of 1949.  The first was economics.  Against the blockade imposed on them, the western powers had built a quiet counter-blockade.  Shipments of steel, machine parts, timber and chemicals from the eastern zone to the  west came to a halt.  East German industry depended on those parts from the west, and the longer the blockade  lasted, the more the side under real pressure began to change.  The second was the winter.  The expectation in Moscow had been that the air bridge would collapse of its own accord  in the autumn fog and the winter cold.  The fog did indeed strike, but the line did not break.  After the winter, the daily tonnage multiplied instead of falling.  Stalin was now better fed than it had been at the start of the blockade.  The first sign came in January 1949.  In written answers to the questions of an American news agency, Stalin made no mention  at all of the currency question he had until then set as the condition for lifting the  blockade.  Washington noticed that silence and probed it.  In New York, in a corridor of the United Nations building, the verperv conversation that began  between the American representative Philip Jessup and the Soviet representative Yakov  Malik, turned into months of secret bargaining.  The result came into force one minute after midnight on the clock showing the 12th of  May, 1949, the roadblocks came away, the barriers lifted, and at the helmstead crossing a convoy  of lorries that had been waiting for days switched on its headlights and turned east,  towards Berlin.  The moment the street lamps in the city came back on, people poured out into the streets.  But the aircraft did not stop.  The western commanders knew that the blockade could be reimposed one morning on the same  grounds, and they wanted a safety stock to build up in the city.  The air bridge continued until the 30th of September 1949.  From beginning to end it lasted 462 days.  In that time 278,228 flights were made to Berlin and 2,334,000 tons of cargo were set  down.  Roughly three-quarters of it was carried by American aircraft, the remainder by the British  and the civilian companies they chartered.  More than two-thirds of the cargo carried was coal.  The cost was recorded as well, 101 people died, most of them in accidents, 40 British,  31 Americans, 1 Australian and 15 German civilians.  The blockade's real legacy though, took shape outside Berlin.  While the blockade was still running, the North Atlantic Treaty was signed in Washington  on the 4th of April, 1949.  What lifted the idea of a permanent military alliance, debated for years in Western Europe,  out of hesitation was in large part what had happened in Berlin.  On the 23rd of May, 1949, the Basic Law came into force and the Federal Republic of Germany  was founded.  On the 7th of October, 1949, the German Democratic Republic was proclaimed in the East.  The move launched in order to push the Western powers out of Berlin made the division of  Germany permanent, turned the Western sectors into the emblem of an alliance, and, in the  eyes of Berliners, transformed the occupying armies that had won the war into protectors  within three and a half years.  The city drew its own lesson as well, never again would it depend on somebody else's power  station for electricity.  In a separate series of flights that began on the 6th of April, 1949, the steel and turbine  parts of a power station in Spandau were cut down into sizes that could be carried, flown  in across 580 flights, as 1416 tons of cargo, and reassembled on site.  The station came online on the 1st of December, 1949, with 60 megawatts of output, it was  Reuter who pressed the switch, and after his death in 1953 the plant took his name.  The runway dug by hand proved long-lived too, Tegel served as Berlin's main airport until  2020.  In front of Tempelhof today stands a three-pronged concrete monument reaching into the sky.  Berliners call it the Hungerkrau, the Hungerclaw, the three prongs represent the three air corridors,  the only legal route into the city, and the names of those who lost their lives are inscribed  on its base.  What that monument records is the proof that a city can be fed not by roads but by a timetable.  That the answer to a blockade could be given with arithmetic rather than with tanks was  noted by both sides, for 40 years Berlin remained the most sensitive point of the cold war,  divided walled in, but never besieged again.
