A liar at least knows what the truth is, and works hard to hide it from you. But there is another kind of person, one who speaks without caring at all about what is true, simply for the sake of having said something. One philosopher considers this second kind more dangerous, because he does not wage war against the truth. He makes it irrelevant.

A friend asks you about a restaurant that has just opened. You have never been there. But you don't quite feel like saying "I don't know," either. So you recall half a sentence you heard somewhere, add a guess of your own, and speak in a confident voice: "They say the meat is good, but it's a little pricey. Don't go in the evening, it gets crowded." One of the things you said might be true, or all of them might be false. But here is the real point: while you were talking, whether it was true never concerned you at all. You only wanted to say something, to seem well informed, to keep the conversation from stalling.

This little scene captures the very question that led an American philosopher, near the end of the twentieth century, to write his most widely read work. Harry Frankfurt, who taught moral philosophy at Princeton University, published a short essay in nineteen eighty-six. Its English title contained a slang word considered rather crude in everyday speech. Here, let's just call it "On Nonsense." Some twenty years later, in two thousand five, this slim text was reissued as a small book. And something unexpected happened: a philosophical essay landed on the bestseller list. In those pages, people had recognized something they had never been able to name.

Frankfurt's starting point was this: all of us recognize this kind of empty talk, we are exposed to it every day, yet we don't really know what it is. We confuse it with lying. And yet there is a deep difference between the two.

Think about the liar. To tell a lie, the liar must first know the truth, or at least what he believes to be true. Then he says the opposite and steers you in that direction. The liar, strangely enough, is someone bound to the truth. The truth is his compass; he simply walks in the direction opposite to where the needle points. In a sense, he takes the truth seriously, because he has to hide it.

The windbag works differently. For him, the line between true and false is beside the point. If what he says turns out to be true, fine; if it turns out to be false, that's fine too. What concerns him is how his words will make you feel, how they will make him look, what purpose they will serve in the moment. In Frankfurt's words, the windbag is neither on the side of the truth nor against it. He never looks at the truth at all. He stands somewhere apart from it.

To illustrate this, Frankfurt turns to an anecdote. A woman named Fania Pascal, who knew Wittgenstein from Cambridge, recounted an incident in a memoir she wrote years later. She had had her tonsils out and was lying miserably in the hospital. Wittgenstein came to visit her. Pascal told him she felt "just like a dog that has been run over." Wittgenstein's face twisted, and he answered sharply: "You don't know what a dog that has been run over feels like."

At first glance, this reaction seems too harsh, even rude. But Frankfurt catches something important here. Pascal was not lying; it was true that she felt awful. Yet the sentence she chose did not come from any concern to describe what she actually felt, but from a wish to say something striking. Her words rested on no knowledge. What bothered Wittgenstein was not a lie, but precisely this carelessness: the way speech, for a moment, suspended its responsibility to the world.

The conclusion Frankfurt reaches is unsettling. In his view, this kind of talk is a greater enemy of the truth than lying is. Because the liar, even when caught in a lie, acknowledges that the truth exists. The windbag, on the other hand, quietly does away with the very idea that speech should be tied to what is true. When lies multiply in a society, people grow suspicious, they investigate, they search for the truth. But when empty talk multiplies, people gradually stop searching for the truth altogether. They grow used to the idea that nothing really means very much.

This may look like a modern American discovery. But the many guises of speaking without regard for the truth reach far back, all the way to the streets of Athens.

[MOOD: gerilim]
In the Athens of the fifth century before Christ, a new profession had emerged: teachers who traveled from city to city, teaching young men the art of speaking in exchange for money. They were called sophists. In Athenian democracy, being able to persuade, in the assembly, in the courts, before the people, meant power. And the sophists were selling that power.

In Plato's dialogue Gorgias, Socrates comes face to face with the famous orator Gorgias. The scene is tense. Gorgias boasts of how powerful an art oratory is. His brother is a physician, and patients sometimes refuse to take their medicine or to submit to surgery. Where the physician failed, Gorgias says, I succeeded; I persuaded those patients with my words. He even adds that if a physician and an orator in some city were to debate before the people over which of them should be chosen as the city's doctor, the orator would win.
Socrates pauses here and asks the decisive question: if an orator who knows nothing of medicine can be more persuasive than a physician, then before an audience that does not know, the one who does not know is more convincing than the one who does. Is that an achievement, or a danger? For Socrates, oratory of this kind is not a true art at all, but a form of flattery. What cookery is to medicine, this sort of oratory is to justice. The physician gives the body what is good for it; the cook gives it what is pleasing. One looks after health, the other after the palate.

This analogy fits Frankfurt's distinction almost exactly. The problem is not that what is said is false, but that the speaker has no relationship with the truth at all. The orator does not need to know what he is talking about; it is enough to know what the listener wants to hear.

Frankfurt also offers an observation on why this kind of talk has become so widespread. When people are obliged to speak about something they know nothing about, empty talk becomes inevitable. In democratic societies, citizens are expected to have an opinion on everything, from the economy to foreign policy, from vaccines to the climate. But none of us can know about all of these things. The gap between the pressure to hold an opinion and the possibility of holding real knowledge gets filled with hollow verbiage.

Let us leave the public square of Athens and step into a meeting room of today. A presentation is cycling across the screen. Someone says they are proposing "a holistic approach that will create synergy." Someone else talks about "a value-driven transformation journey." Everyone nods. On the way out, no one can say exactly what was decided. Nobody told a lie. But nobody said anything, either.

The power of this kind of speech lies in its vagueness. It cannot be proven false, because it makes no claim. It cannot be questioned, because whoever questions it is afraid of looking as though they have not understood. And the speaker pockets the reward of appearing to know something, to be doing something.

Researchers have even tried to measure it. In twenty fifteen, the Canadian psychologist Gordon Pennycook and his colleagues carried out a curious experiment. They showed participants sentences produced by a computer stringing together randomly chosen grandiose words. Sentences like "Hidden meaning transforms unparalleled abstract beauty": grammatically sound, yet saying nothing at all. Participants were asked to rate how profound these sentences were. A considerable number of people found deep meaning in these meaningless strings. The researchers called this tendency "receptivity to pseudo-profound bullshit," and found that it was linked to a habit of accepting one's first intuition without questioning it.

So the success of empty talk does not depend on the speaker alone. The listener's readiness matters too. When our ears catch an impressive voice or a clever-sounding word, we are inclined to approve without looking at the substance. The sophist exists because there is a crowd willing to listen to him.

So what do we have at our disposal as listeners? In the West, this question has mostly been debated in terms of logic and critical thinking. But there is another powerful tradition that asks the same question from a very different place: through the ethics of speech.

[MOOD: sakin]
Sahih Muslim, the hadith collection compiled by Imam Muslim, opens with a lengthy introduction before it turns to the hadiths themselves. In this introduction there is a short saying attributed to the Prophet: "It is enough of a lie for a person to relate everything he hears."

At first hearing, this sentence may seem strange. Does someone who passes on what he has heard become a liar? He did not make it up, after all. He simply relayed what he heard from someone else. And this is exactly where the subtlety of the sentence lies. The saying broadens the definition of a lie. Knowingly saying something false is a lie, yes. But spreading everything you hear, without ever asking whether it is true, without filtering it, without weighing it, leads a person to the same place. Because someone who repeats everything he hears will sooner or later repeat something false as well. And since he does not concern himself with the truth while doing so, he cannot escape responsibility.

Set this beside Frankfurt's distinction and something striking comes into view. The Western philosopher says that speech indifferent to truth is something different from a lie. This hadith, by contrast, places that very indifference inside the lie. The two arrive at the same point by different paths: the ethics of speech concerns not only what you say, but how much care you take for the truth as you say it.

The same introduction records another saying. Attributed to Abdullah ibn al-Mubarak, the scholar from Khorasan, it reads: "The isnad is part of the religion. Were it not for the isnad, anyone could say whatever they wished." The isnad is the chain of transmission showing through whom, and to whom, a saying was passed down. For centuries, hadith scholars in the Islamic world examined these chains. Did the narrator have a strong memory? Was he an honest man? Had he really met the person he claimed to quote? An entire science was built on asking where a saying came from. The thinking behind it was simple: a saying has no value unless its source is known, however beautiful it may be.
It is possible to trace this same concern in the Quran as well. In the Surah al-Hujurat, believers are told that when an untrustworthy person brings them news, they should investigate it; otherwise they may unknowingly harm others and come to regret it. Here, too, responsibility does not end with the one who brings the news; the one who receives it is equally bound to verify it.

Imam al-Ghazali approaches the matter from a different angle. In his great work, the Ihya Ulum al-Din, the Revival of the Religious Sciences, he devotes an entire section to the afflictions of the tongue and lists some twenty of them, one by one. At the top of the list are not the grave sins, such as lying, backbiting, or slander. At the top of the list is speaking about things that are none of one's concern. Next comes saying more than is needed. For al-Ghazali, the greatest dangers often begin with what seems most innocent. The habit of idle talk is a letting go of the tongue's reins. And a tongue whose reins have been let go will, sooner or later, drive a person over a cliff.

Al-Ghazali supports this idea with a hadith transmitted by al-Tirmidhi: "Part of the beauty of a person's Islam is leaving aside what does not concern him." This principle speaks not only to the devout, but to anyone who wishes to preserve the worth of their words. Not speaking on matters you do not know, not meddling in affairs that are not yours, lends speech a certain weight.

This awareness left a deep mark on Turkish thought as well. The Kutadgu Bilig, written by Yusuf Khass Hajib in the eleventh century, reminds the reader again and again that the tongue is a power that can both elevate a person and bring about their ruin; it regards speaking little, and speaking fittingly, as a sign of wisdom. Some two centuries later, Yunus Emre expressed the same truth with a simplicity the common people could grasp: A word can end a war, a word can cost a head; a word can turn a poisoned meal into honey and butter.

In this tradition, speech is a trust. Every sentence that leaves the mouth must be accounted for. And that account is not only about whether or not you told a lie; it is also about how much care you took to convey the truth to the one listening.

So what do we see when we consider these words today, in an age when anything we hear can be passed on to thousands of people with the press of a button?

The saying "Either appear as you are, or be as you appear" shares a similar fate. It is attributed to Rumi in countless places. But this sentence, in this form, is nowhere to be found in his works.

No one here means any harm. Whoever made that image, whoever shared it, whoever liked it, most likely believes they are doing something good. The sentence is lovely, the message is kind, the name is revered. But this is exactly what Frankfurt described: no one cares whether the words are true. What matters is the feeling they create, the prestige they carry. Rumi's name is used like a seal, and that seal places the sentence beyond question. And so, across generations, in direct defiance of that warning at the opening of Sahih Muslim, a chain forms of people repeating everything they hear. This chain has no isnad.

The sad part is this: the more the fabricated sayings multiply, the more the real Rumi fades. His true voice, so often unsettling, difficult, a voice that disturbs, is lost behind the pink sunsets. Fewer and fewer people know the verses of the Masnavi that open with the tale of the reed flute, the lament of that reed crying out against separation, that arduous inner journey stretching across thousands of couplets. Everyone believes they know Rumi, but what they know is very often the things Rumi never said.

This is not something that happens to a single poet alone. In twenty eighteen, researchers at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology examined news stories that had spread on social media over a period of more than ten years. They found that false news spread far faster and wider than true news, and that it was roughly seventy percent more likely to be shared. What's more, those spreading it were mostly not automated accounts, but people. What was new and surprising proved more appealing than what was true.

Years ago, the software developer Alberto Brandolini put this state of affairs into a half-joking rule: the effort required to refute nonsense is many times greater than the effort needed to produce it. A fabricated quote can be written in seconds; showing that it is not genuine requires combing through old texts and consulting experts. Empty talk always has the advantage, because it is never obliged to answer for itself.
Today, a new dimension has been added to this debate. In twenty twenty-four, three researchers from the University of Glasgow turned directly to Frankfurt in a paper they wrote on large language models. When these artificial intelligence systems produce a text, they argued, their aim is not to tell the truth, but to find the next word that looks fluent and convincing. When they produce false information, it is not a lie, because the system has no intention to deceive. But there is a structural indifference to the truth. The portrait Frankfurt drew of human beings in nineteen eighty-six is now, forty years later, being debated for machines as well. This is less a question of whether to trust them than a reminder to us: a fluent sentence is not the same as a true sentence. Asking for the source matters more than ever.

So what can we do in this age? Perhaps the answer lies with Rumi himself. Many of the ghazals in the Divan-e Kabir end with a single word: Khamush. That is, hush, be silent. Rumi uses this word almost like a pen name. A poet who speaks with such passion, and at such length, closes his poems by counseling silence. There is no contradiction in this. Because for him, being silent did not mean having nothing to say. Silence meant standing before something deeper than words themselves. It meant not forcing words where words fall short.

In everyday life, the equivalent may be very simple. Being able to say "I don't know, I haven't been there" to a friend who asks about a restaurant. Not hesitating to ask about a sentence we didn't understand in a meeting. Pausing for a moment before sharing that beautiful quote that lands on our phone, and asking, "Who really said this?" Admitting that we have no opinion on a subject is not a weakness, but a stance that protects the value of words.

Frankfurt had said that bullshit wears away the truth more than lying does. What it wears away is not individual truths, but our very need for the truth. That short saying at the opening of Sahih Muslim, meanwhile, pulled a person one step back: do not repeat everything you hear. Between these two voices runs a line stretching from Athens to Konya, from Princeton to the screens of today. And their shared counsel leads to the same place: to say something, first know it, and if you do not know, be willing to stay silent.

Perhaps this is the hardest virtue of our age. In a world where speaking is so easy, so cheap and so fast, to remember that we owe an account for every word. To care not about filling the bag, but about making sure that everything we put into it is true.
