The Stone That Chooses to Spin One Way: The Stubbornness of the Celt

2026-10-27 · 18 dk

Describes the boat-shaped celt (rattleback), which, when spun in the wrong direction, begins to wobble on its own and then reverses its direction of spin. The episode shows that this phenomenon does not violate the conservation of angular momentum. A small angular offset between the body's axes of curvature and its mass axes transfers energy between rocking and spinning, and friction against the table surface mediates this transfer. The episode also traces the line of work running from George Walker's first analysis in eighteen ninety-six to Hermann Bondi's treatment in nineteen eighty-six and later studies of chaotic behavior.

You spin a boat-shaped stone clockwise on a tabletop, and it turns smoothly. But spin it the other way, and after a few turns it stops, begins to rock all on its own, and finally starts spinning back in the direction it prefers. How does a stone decide which way to turn, and how does it do so without breaking one of the most fundamental conservation laws in physics?

On the table sits a smooth stone about the size of your palm, shaped a little like a boat. Its underside is gently curved, its top is flat. You take it by the edge with your finger and spin it clockwise. The stone turns a few times, slows down, and then something unexpected happens. It stops spinning and begins to rock, its nose tipping up and down, rattling against the table. The rocking lasts a few seconds, and then, all by itself, it reverses and starts spinning counterclockwise. Nobody touched it, yet the stone rejected the direction you gave it and switched to one of its own choosing.

Now spin the same stone counterclockwise. This time there's no objection at all. The stone turns calmly, and it keeps on turning until friction slowly brings it to rest. It's as if the stone has a preference: it accepts spinning one way, and refuses to spin the other.

In the English-speaking world this object is called a rattleback, after the way its back rattles. Another name for it is the celt. In Turkish, too, it's known as the Kelt stone, a name derived from that second term. But the name is misleading, because it has nothing to do with the Celts. In archaeology, a celt is the name given to prehistoric axe and chisel heads made of polished stone. And the story of the word itself is almost as strange as the stone. For a long time, it was accepted that a Latin word, celtis, meant chisel. The source cited for this word was a line in the Book of Job, in the Latin translation of the Bible. Later comparisons, however, revealed that in more reliable manuscripts, the word written at that point was certe, meaning "certainly." In other words, a single copyist's slip of the pen may have given archaeology a term it would go on using for centuries.

According to the story that has been handed down, in the nineteenth century some researchers studying these polished stone tools noticed that certain specimens, when spun on a flat surface, favored a single direction. Exactly who made this observation, and where it was first recorded, isn't clear, and here we need to be careful. There is no evidence whatsoever that prehistoric people deliberately shaped these axes to behave this way. The behavior was most likely a by-product of the fact that an object shaped by hand can never be perfectly symmetrical. If the stone held a secret at all, that secret lay not in the knowledge of its maker, but in the stone's geometry.

And that is precisely what caught the attention of physicists. Because it seemed to shake one of the most basic intuitions of classical mechanics. A spinning body tends to keep on spinning. You spin a top, the top slows down and stops, but it never starts turning the other way on its own. The quantity we call angular momentum is conserved unless something outside acts on it. The celt, though, seemed to be breaking that rule, reversing its direction of spin all by itself.

The first person to take this puzzle seriously in mathematical terms was the English physicist and mathematician Gilbert Walker. In a paper published in eighteen ninety-six, Walker set out the equations of motion for this object, which he described as a kind of dynamical top. Today, Walker's name is remembered mostly in a different field. He would later go on to head the meteorological service in India, and to carry out groundbreaking work on the great oscillations between the ocean and the atmosphere. But as a young Cambridge mathematician, he was wrestling with why this stone, small enough to fit in the palm of his hand, behaved so stubbornly. And the conclusion he reached was this: there is no magic in the stone. But there is, in its shape, a very subtle twist, one that doesn't catch the eye at first glance.

To understand that twist, we need to think about two different identities of the stone, one at a time. The first is how the stone's mass is distributed. A long, flat object has certain axes around which it is easiest, and hardest, to turn. In physics, these are called the principal axes of inertia. Think of a long ruler: spinning it around its long axis is easy, but holding it by the middle and spinning it like a propeller takes more effort. The distribution of mass draws an invisible set of axes onto the body.

The second identity is the shape of the stone's underside. The base of a celt is curved, like the bottom of an egg or an ellipsoid. But that curve is not the same in every direction. Along one direction the curve is sharper, and along the direction at right angles to it, the curve is flatter. These two directions form the base's own set of axes. In an ordinary, symmetrical object, these two sets of axes line up exactly. The long axis of the mass runs along the same line as the long curve of the base. In a celtic stone, they don't. There is a small angle between the two sets of axes, often a shift of only a few degrees. Sometimes this is done with weights placed on top of the stone, set slightly askew from its long axis. Sometimes it is done by carving the base with a slight twist. The eye usually can't catch the difference. But the stone does. All of its stubbornness comes from this hidden angle.

Now, instead of spinning the stone, let's try something else. While the stone sits still on the table, press gently on one end with your finger and let go. The stone starts rocking back and forth like a seesaw, its nose bobbing up and down. This is called pitching. On a symmetrical stone, the rocking would simply die away where it started. On a celtic stone, though, the stone slowly begins to turn while it rocks, and always in one particular direction. If you press on the side edge instead and tip it from side to side, which is called rolling, most stones will start turning the other way. The rocking turns into spin. And the kind of rocking decides which way the stone spins.

Let's follow, step by step, why this happens. As the stone pitches, the point where it touches the table slides back and forth across the base. If the base were symmetrical, this contact point would travel exactly along the stone's long axis, on a line running straight in front of and straight behind the center of mass. But because the curvature axes of the base are turned slightly away from the mass axes, the contact point doesn't move in a straight line. It moves along a slightly diagonal path. When the stone tips forward, the contact point shifts a little to the right. When it tips back, it shifts a little to the left, or the other way around, depending on how the stone is built.

This shift is the key. The table is pushing on the stone at the contact point at every moment. That push is made up of an upward force holding the stone up, and a friction force running along the surface of the table. When these forces act not directly in line with the center of mass but a little to one side, they produce a small twist, a torque, that tries to turn the stone around a vertical axis. Pushing a door in line with its hinges does nothing, but pushing it at its edge swings it open. In the same way, the table pushes the stone slightly off center with every rock. And here is the real subtlety. If the stone is pushed from the right when it tips forward, and from the left when it tips back, the twists from the two half swings don't cancel out. They add up in the same direction. Every rock gives the stone another small nudge of spin in the same direction.

Now let's go back to the very beginning, to the moment we spun the stone the wrong way. While the stone spins in the direction it doesn't like, there is a link between the spinning and the rocking, caused by that same hidden angle, and this link works in reverse. The spin feeds small pitching oscillations inside the stone. The tiny vibrations that are always there, instead of fading away, grow when the stone spins in this direction. In the language of mathematics, spin in this direction is unstable. The moment the stone starts to rattle is the moment spin energy begins flowing into the rocking. As the rocking grows, the mechanism we just described kicks in, and the twist produced by the rocking starts working against the existing spin. The spin slows, stops, and finally starts up again in the direction the stone prefers. In this new direction, the link works the other way. The spin no longer makes the rocking grow, it damps it. The stone settles down and spins along easily.

So there is no will at work here, only a loop. Spin in the unwanted direction creates the rocking that brings about its own end. Spin in the preferred direction suppresses the rocking and keeps itself going. What we call the stone's stubbornness is nothing more than the geometry treating these two directions unequally.

Although this explanation was laid out in qualitative terms at the end of the nineteenth century, the full details kept physicists busy for a long time. Walker built a linearized model of the motion for small oscillations and showed which direction was unstable. But following the whole reversal, that long, nonlinear process in which the stone slows, stops, and switches direction, only became possible once computers came to the rescue with numerical solutions. Simulations in the nineteen-eighties showed that the behavior seen in real stones came straight out of the equations. It even turned out that some stones, depending on their design, can be unstable in both directions, meaning that after reversing once, they can reverse yet again. These stones never settle on one direction. They go back and forth between the two several times, a little slower each time.

But how does all of this square with the firm rule we stated at the very start? Wasn't angular momentum supposed to be conserved? The stone was spinning clockwise, and now it's spinning the other way. Where did that spin go, and where did the new spin come from?

The answer lies in remembering when the conservation of angular momentum actually applies. The conservation law holds for an isolated system, one that receives no twisting force from outside. But the celt stone is not isolated. At every moment it touches the table, and at every moment the table pushes back against it. That off-center push we just saw is precisely a twisting force applied to the stone from outside. So when the stone's own angular momentum changes, no law is being broken.

The conservation law hasn't vanished; it has simply moved to a larger system. If you consider the stone and the table together, along with the floor the table stands on, the building, and ultimately the entire Earth, the total angular momentum is still conserved. When the stone, spinning clockwise, slows down and reverses direction, the table applies a twist to it in one direction and receives an equal twist in the opposite direction. That angular momentum is ultimately passed on to our planet. The Earth's mass is so enormous that the change this transfer makes to its rotation is far too small to measure. But in principle, it is there. Every time a celt stone changes direction, the Earth's rotation shifts by an unimaginably tiny amount. The law hasn't disappeared; it has simply hidden itself inside the Earth.

There is one more important detail here. For this mechanism to work, the table has to be able to grip the stone horizontally, which means friction has to be present. On a perfectly slippery surface, say a flawless ice rink, the table could only push the stone straight up. And those vertical forces could have only a very limited effect, because producing a twist around a vertical axis requires a force acting along the surface. Real experiments and calculations show that as friction decreases, the reversal effect grows weaker. The true driver of the stone's stubbornness is the friction of the table, holding it in place without letting it slide.

But the word friction invites a misunderstanding here. When we hear friction, most of us think of lost energy, heat, slowing down. Yet the friction at play in the celt stone is, to a first approximation, the kind that consumes no energy. As long as the stone rolls across the table without slipping, the point of contact is, for that instant, motionless. A force applied to a point that doesn't move does no work, which means it spends no energy. This force only steers the direction of motion, transferring energy from one kind of movement to another.

And this is one of the most surprising layers added to the celt stone story in the late twentieth century. Hermann Bondi, known for his work in cosmology, took up the problem in a study published in nineteen eighty-six, treating it as a question of the rigid-body dynamics of one-way spinning. When physicists examined the stone's motion using an idealized model, in which it rolls without ever slipping and without ever losing energy, they found that the reversal still happened. In other words, the loss of energy is not what causes the change of direction. The energy of the spin flows into the wobble, and the energy of the wobble flows back into a spin in the opposite direction. In real stones, of course, some energy does escape as heat and sound. That clattering noise is exactly that. But the heart of the story is not energy disappearing; it is energy changing form.

This idealized model has an unexpected side as well. In physics, we tend to think that systems which conserve energy can usually run backward in time under the same rules, oscillating in a kind of balance. Systems bound by the condition of rolling without slipping, however, belong to a separate family that physicists call nonholonomic systems, and they don't always obey these familiar rules. When the Russian mathematicians Alexey Borisov and Ivan Mamaev studied this idealized celt stone model in the early two thousands, they found that even though energy is conserved, the system can display chaotic behavior, and even strange attractors, which are normally considered a hallmark of systems that lose energy. This toy that fits in the palm of your hand became, for mathematicians, a laboratory showing that the conservation of energy does not mean order.

The story has one more layer, and this one touches the stone's deepest property. Imagine placing a celt stone in front of a mirror. Its reflection is a stone whose hidden angle is skewed the opposite way. If you were to actually make that mirror-image stone, it would prefer to spin in the other direction. In other words, the celt stone, just like our right and left hands, is an object that cannot be superimposed on its own mirror image. Scientists call this chirality, or handedness. In a study from two thousand eight, the mathematicians Keith Moffatt and Tadashi Tokieda approached the celt stone's reversal from exactly this angle, treating it as a prime example of chiral dynamics. The stone's preference is the handedness of its shape reflected in its motion. Neither the table, nor gravity, nor the Earth's rotation favors one direction over the other. The only thing that chooses the direction is the stone's own asymmetry. This perspective turns the Celt stone from a simple tabletop toy into a small model of a much broader question. Nature is full of processes that favor one direction over the other. In chemistry, the right-handed and left-handed forms of a molecule behave differently; in biology, the proteins of living things are built almost entirely from amino acids of the same handedness. The Celt stone is not the answer to these great questions, and it would be wrong to claim that it is. But it shows something very concrete: even when the laws themselves treat both directions equally, a tiny asymmetry in an object's shape can turn into a strikingly clear preference at the level of motion. Symmetric rules need no great force to produce asymmetric outcomes. A shift of just a few degrees is enough.

Today, Celt stones are made of plastic, wood, or stone, and they pass from hand to hand in science museums and physics classrooms. Anyone who sees one for the first time almost always reacts the same way. First they are surprised, then they look for the trick, wondering whether there is a magnet inside, a hidden motor, or some kind of weighted mechanism. Learning that the trick is actually in plain sight, in the very shape of the stone, may be the most beautiful lesson this object has to offer. This journey, which begins with a prehistoric axe head, takes its name from a manuscript error, passes through a meteorologist's notebook, and reaches all the way to the equations of chaos theorists, arrives at a single point. Even in its strangest-looking behavior, nature never breaks its own laws. It simply applies them in a corner we don't immediately notice.

And the stone is still spinning on the table. Whichever direction you give it, it will eventually turn back to its own. But now we know that behind this stubbornness there is neither a secret nor a will. There is only a curved base, a mass sitting slightly turned against it, and a table that quietly pushes it from the edge at every moment.
