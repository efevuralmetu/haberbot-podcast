The human eye has three types of cells that detect color. A shrimp living on coral reefs has twelve. For years, the internet claimed that this animal sees colors we cannot even imagine. Then someone actually gave the shrimp a color test, and the result surprised everyone.

On the shallow coral reefs of Indonesia, two eyes peer out from the mouth of a small burrow dug into the sand. Each eye sits at the tip of a stalk and swivels independently of the other. One looks upward while the other drifts to the side, and then both lock onto the same spot at once. Their owner is a mantis shrimp, no more than a few centimeters long. Despite its name, it is not a close relative of the shrimp we eat. It belongs to a separate order of crustaceans called the stomatopods, an order with more than four hundred species. Its fame usually comes from its punch: some species fire their front appendages so fast that they can shatter snail shells. But what has truly occupied scientists for many years is not the punch. It is the eyes at the ends of those stalks.

First, let's look at our own eyes. The human retina contains three types of cone cells for color. One is most sensitive to short wavelengths, that is, to blue; one to medium wavelengths, that is, to green; and one to long wavelengths, the region close to red. The millions of shades we see arise from comparing the signals these three cells send. In dogs, the number is two; in many birds, four. But in the species of mantis shrimp that have been studied, the number of receptor types devoted to color vision rises to twelve. Some of these capture ultraviolet light, the part of the spectrum beyond violet that we cannot see at all. Counting the cells dedicated to polarized light as well, the total number of receptor types reaches around sixteen. It is one of the most crowded visual toolkits known in the animal kingdom.

Something else is just as striking as the numbers: the architecture of the eye. Like an insect's, the mantis shrimp's eye is a compound eye made up of thousands of tiny units. But this eye is divided into three regions. There is a hemisphere on top, a hemisphere below, and running straight through the middle, a narrow strip made up of six rows of units. Researchers call this strip the midband. The upper and lower hemispheres mainly handle shape, motion and brightness. Color, on the other hand, is almost entirely the job of this thin midband. In the units of the band's first four rows, receptors are stacked one on top of another along the path the light travels, with colored filters between them. These filters absorb part of the light and pass the rest down to the cell beneath. In this way, each cell comes to see a fairly narrow slice of the spectrum. The last two rows deal not with color but with the direction in which light vibrates, that is, with polarization.

This structure has an interesting consequence. Because the upper hemisphere, the lower hemisphere and the midband can all look at the same point from different angles, a single eye may not need a second eye to judge how far away an object is. We work out depth from the difference between the images our two eyes see. The mantis shrimp, so to speak, carries three separate views in each eye. Taken together, the two eyes make for an extraordinarily flexible surveillance system.

Anyone looking at such equipment has the same thought. If the world we see with three receptors is this colorful, the world seen with twelve must be unimaginably rich in detail. In popular writing, this idea was often exaggerated. It was said that the mantis shrimp sees colors we cannot even imagine, and that it catches even the slightest difference between shades. The logic seemed simple: more receptors, finer discrimination. But this was an assumption, and the way to test an assumption was to ask the animal itself.

At the University of Queensland in Australia, a team that included Hanne Thoen and Justin Marshall decided to do exactly that. The star of the experiment was a small species of mantis shrimp called Haptosquilla trispinosa. The question was clear: how close can two colors be before this animal can no longer tell them apart?

Asking a crustacean a question about color is not easy. The team took advantage of the animal's natural behavior. Mantis shrimp are quick learners, capable of learning to strike at a target. The researchers placed optical fibers in the aquarium, each glowing at its tip with light of a specific wavelength. Whenever the shrimp went for the light of the correct color, it received food as a reward. Once this training was complete, the real test began. The animal was shown the color it had learned on one side and, at the same time, a similar color on the other. Could the shrimp pick the right one?

Here, the idea of wavelength needs a little unpacking. The light our eyes can see spans roughly four hundred to seven hundred nanometers. A nanometer is one billionth of a meter. Around four hundred fifty looks blue to us, around five hundred thirty green, around five hundred eighty yellow, a little above six hundred orange, and six hundred fifty and beyond red. In some regions of the spectrum, the human eye can distinguish two lights that differ by just one to four nanometers. In the transition between yellow and orange, we can pick up even shifts so small they are barely noticeable.
When the results were published in the journal Science in twenty fourteen, they turned expectations upside down. The mantis shrimp did well when the difference between the two lights was large. But as the difference shrank, their consistency collapsed. The exact threshold varied across different parts of the spectrum, but once the gap between two wavelengths fell below roughly twelve to twenty-five nanometers, the animal could no longer reliably make the right choice. In other words, two shades that we tell apart with ease, say an orange leaning toward yellow and a yellow leaning toward orange, were often one and the same to this creature.

To grasp just how surprising this was, consider the inverse relationship. An eye with three receptors was making color distinctions many times finer than an eye with twelve. It was as if a three-stringed instrument could play subtler notes than a twelve-stringed one. Something was off. Either the experiment was failing to measure the animal's true ability, or our assumptions about color vision simply did not apply to this creature.

The team leaned toward the second explanation, and to understand it, we need to look at how the human eye works once more, but this time from the brain's side. Our three cone cells do not tell us colors on their own. For instance, just because the cone sensitive to long wavelengths sends a strong signal, our brain does not immediately say red. Our nervous system subtracts the cones' signals from one another and compares them. It builds opposing channels, such as red against green and blue against yellow. What determines a color is the subtle ratio between the receptors' signals. This method of comparison is extraordinarily sensitive, because even a tiny shift in the ratio between two receptors is perceived as a new shade. The price, however, is intensive neural processing. In our retina and our brain, layer upon layer of nerve-cell circuitry is devoted to color.

Now imagine applying the same method to twelve receptors. Comparing each of the twelve receptors with all the others would mean building dozens of opposing channels. That would place an enormous burden on the nervous system of a small crustacean. What's more, the weak discriminating power that Thoen and her colleagues measured suggested that this kind of comparison was not happening at all. Had it been, receptors covering the spectrum so densely should have delivered very fine discrimination. They did not. So the shrimp must have been recognizing color some other way.

The idea the team proposed was this: the mantis shrimp may see colors not by comparing them with one another, but by recognizing them directly. Let's think about it through an analogy. In a paint shop, to compare the shades of two cans of paint, you set them side by side and weigh the difference with your eye. That is the human method. Now imagine you are holding a mold with twelve compartments. Each compartment is tuned to a particular range of color. When you hold a color up to the mold, whichever compartment lights up is the answer. You compare nothing with anything; you simply look at which compartment has been triggered. This method is fast and requires little computation. But it comes at a price: two similar colors that fall into the same compartment cannot be told apart. And that matches exactly the picture seen in the experiment.

According to this idea, the twelve receptors exist not for fine discrimination, but for coarse yet very rapid classification. In the mantis shrimp's world, what really matters is not knowing the difference between two shades of orange, but grasping in an instant whether the thing in front of it is prey, a rival, or a mate. The reef is an environment where light shifts constantly with depth and with the murkiness of the water. Here, a fast and robust labeling system may prove more useful than a refined system of comparison. This needs to be said with a caveat: this explanation is a strong hypothesis consistent with the experimental results, but exactly how this recognition is carried out in the shrimp's nervous system has not yet been shown in detail. Some researchers also keep open the possibility that some comparison, however limited, may take place between the receptors.

So how does the eye perform this recognition? This is where the narrowness of the midband comes into play. Because the band responsible for color vision consists of only a few rows of units, at any given moment the shrimp sees only a thin strip of its field of view in color. To gather the color information for the whole scene, it has to move its eyes. And that is exactly what mantis shrimp do. They pass their eyes over an object in slow, deliberate movements, sweeping the midband across the scene like the light bar of a scanner. You might picture the barcode reader at a supermarket checkout, or the light of a photocopier gliding beneath the glass. This is why Hanne Thoen and her colleagues call their proposed mechanism color recognition through temporal scanning. The color information is gathered over time as the eye moves, and each color is recognized by when, and how strongly, each receptor responds.
This perspective also makes sense of another astonishing ability of the eye. The last two rows of the midband, as we mentioned earlier, detect the direction in which light vibrates. When light waves vibrate in a single plane, this is called linear polarization. Sunglasses that cut the glare reflecting off the surface of water work on this principle. Many animals can see linear polarization. But light can also be circularly polarized. In that case, the direction of vibration rotates like a corkscrew as the light travels forward, sometimes clockwise, sometimes counterclockwise. In two thousand eight, Tsyr-Huei Chiou, Justin Marshall and their colleagues showed that certain species of mantis shrimp can distinguish this circularly polarized light as well. It was the first time this ability had been documented so clearly anywhere in the animal kingdom.

The mechanism is elegant. In the units of the last rows, a special cell creates a delay of a quarter of a wavelength between the two components of incoming light. This is exactly what the glass components known as quarter-wave plates do in physicists' optics laboratories. In this way, the rotating light is converted into light that vibrates in a straight line, which the cells beneath can detect. What's more, this biological plate performs consistently across a wider range of colors than many man-made plates. The position of the eye is no accident either. Like color information, polarization information is gathered through the scanning motion, and the shrimp can strengthen this signal by rotating their eyes around their own axes.

This ability appears to serve a purpose. Certain regions on the bodies of some mantis shrimp species reflect circularly polarized light, and the animals display these regions during courtship or while defending their territory. This could mean they are able to communicate among themselves through a hidden channel that most predators cannot see. This interpretation, too, is supported by experiments, but it cannot yet be generalized to all species.

Taken together, this picture shows that there is no single definition of what we call color vision. We see color with only a few receptors, but with powerful processing in the brain. The mantis shrimp, on the other hand, seems to have placed the burden on the eye itself: a large number of narrow-band receptors, a layered structure that separates them with filters, and a moving strip that scans the scene. Much of the computation is solved in the physics and movement of the eye, before the signal ever reaches the nervous system. And since the animal's brain is extremely small compared to ours, this trade-off looks like an evolutionarily sensible solution.

This idea caught the attention of engineers as well. A sensor that makes fast decisions with little energy and little computation is exactly what is wanted for satellites, robots and small devices. Cameras inspired by the eye's polarization hardware are being developed. For example, at the University of Illinois, Viktor Gruev's team, working with Justin Marshall, developed a camera inspired by the mantis shrimp eye that records color and polarization at the same time. Researchers are investigating whether cameras like these, by revealing structural differences in tissue through polarization, could help identify certain cancerous tissues. These are not yet established tools in hospitals; they are promising, but ongoing, lines of research.

Perhaps the most important thing this small animal teaches us is how misleading it can be to jump to conclusions by looking at numbers. Twelve receptors did not mean a world of color twelve times richer. They meant an entirely different answer to an entirely different question. We see colors by comparing them with one another, chasing after subtle differences. The mantis shrimp, by contrast, in the rapidly shifting light of the reef, sweeps its eye calmly across an object and recognizes it in an instant. Less detail, but more speed. Nature has solved the same problem not once, but many times, and differently each time. And to understand one of these solutions, the first thing needed was to question the animal not with prejudice, but with attention.
